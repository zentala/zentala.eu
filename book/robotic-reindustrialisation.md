This is the top of the stack, and it only works because of what sits
underneath it. [The demographic argument](/book/designing-our-retirement) says
why we need it: there will not be enough people to do the work our retirement
depends on. A [common working language](/vision#language) is what lets the
engineers who build it work with each other. A [digital
state](/book/the-total-state) is what it plugs into. Robotics on its own,
without those, is a trade fair.

What follows is how Europe could actually get there.

## Europe needs a new industry anyway

The old engine was the car. It is being taken apart — by electrification, by
Chinese manufacturing, by a shift in what a vehicle even is — and the Union's
own electric-car transition has been half reversed to keep the car companies
alive. Germany knows this better than anyone, and so does every supplier town
from Bavaria to Silesia that grew up around it.

Robotics is the natural successor. It needs precision manufacturing, which
Europe has; industrial engineering culture, which Europe has; and software,
which Europe has been losing at — and which is exactly where Central Europe can
contribute something the rest of the continent needs. Germany could rebuild its
entire car industry to produce robots, and be an industrial-robotic power. So
this is not only a demographic defence. It is the reindustrialisation Europe was
going to have to attempt regardless, aimed at a problem that makes it urgent.

## What Poland and Central Europe bring

Every member state asks what it can offer the Union that the Union actually
wants. For Poland the honest answer is software, and a share in building the
machines. The layer around robotics — control systems, fleet coordination,
simulation, safety, the integration work that decides whether a machine is
deployable or a demo — is software work at industrial scale. Poles are good at
software; the Germans built SAP. In robotics the software *is* the product;
the metal is a commodity.

That is worth stating plainly, because Central Europe has spent twenty years
as the place where other people's systems get maintained. This is a chance to
own a layer — and to export it, rather than only run it at home.

## The path runs through the war

Here is the uncomfortable part, and skipping it would make everything above
wishful. Europe is in a conflict with Russia, and that conflict is already
doing what peacetime industrial policy never managed: pushing serious money
into autonomy, sensing, drones, and the manufacturing capacity to build them at
volume. Defence budgets are funding robotics research right now, at a scale no
civilian programme was ever going to get approved.

<Callout kind="claim" label="The strategy">
  Stimulate the market now through defence, and convert the sector to civilian
  purposes after the war — the same autonomy, sensing and manufacturing
  capacity, pointed at demographic collapse instead of at an enemy.
</Callout>

**That capacity converts.** Autonomous navigation, machine perception,
teleoperation, ruggedised actuators, and above all the factories and supply
chains that produce them are the capabilities the civilian problem needs. A
drone company and an agricultural robotics company are separated by their
customers, not by their engineering. No committee needs to schedule the
conversion: when the wartime customer goes away, the capacity looks for a new
one, and demographic collapse will be the largest market in Europe. Industrial
policy's job is to know this in advance and make sure the civilian demand is
waiting — procurement, standards and pilot deployments ready on the other side.
What this is not: an argument for prolonging anything. It is an argument for
making sure the industrial base built under one pressure survives to answer the
next one, instead of collapsing with the contracts.

<EvidenceBox>
  Whether defence-funded autonomy actually converts is checkable: a count of
  European suppliers that entered military autonomy after 2022 and now carry a
  civilian product line, against those that did not. Nobody has published one.
  If the number is small, the conversion needs to be planned harder than this
  chapter assumes; if it is zero, the strategy is wrong.
</EvidenceBox>

## Where the machines go first

In the order they are ready, not in the order they are exciting.

**Municipal and communal services.** Cleaning, maintenance, waste,
groundskeeping, basic logistics — heavily staffed, highly mechanical, already
short of workers, and operating in spaces a city controls. The easiest place to
deploy and the fastest place to feel the benefit. I would not bet on humanoids
here. I would bet on simple machines: the ones that take the rubbish out, the
ones that cut the grass, the ones that drive.

**Food production and delivery.** Autonomous farming and autonomous transport
are the most advanced civilian robotics we have. Food security stops being
theoretical when the people who grow and move it are retiring.

<PullQuote>Being cared for by a machine and being alone are not the same thing.</PullQuote>

**Predictive medicine and healthcare.** The cost that grows fastest as a
population ages. Robotics here means less a machine at the bedside than an
entire system that catches what would have become a hospitalisation.

**And the part that is not machinery at all:** systems that let older people
support each other, live together, work if they want to, and stay in contact.
Being cared for by a machine and being alone are not the same thing. We are
designing the world we retire into, and it should be one worth arriving in.

## Cheap beats owned

The gains from this can be captured by a handful of firms, and by default they
will be. <T slug="who-captures-the-gains">Who captures the gains</T> is the
question every proposal on this site has to answer, and my answer is that
ownership is the second question: what matters first is that the machines are
<T slug="cheap-is-wealth">cheap</T> — affordable, easy to implement, built on
open standards, hard to monopolise. A machine a municipality can buy from four
suppliers, repair locally and run on software that is not a rental does not
need a complicated ownership theory. A locked platform with a per-hour licence
concentrates its returns no matter who signs the contract.

## What has to happen

- **Treat robotics as national and European strategy**, the way energy and
  defence are treated — not as a research topic.
- **Plan the conversion before it is needed**: civilian procurement, standards
  and pilot programmes waiting for the capacity built under defence contracts.
- **Cheap, open and hard to monopolise** as the condition every deployment has
  to meet, decided as policy because the market will not decide it in our
  favour.
- **Deploy in municipal services first**, where the public sector is both
  customer and beneficiary, and where results are visible to citizens within a
  budget cycle.
- **Let Central Europe own the software layer**, deliberately, as industrial
  strategy rather than subcontracting.
