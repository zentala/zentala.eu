Education Instead of Import

The frame · Proposal

By Paweł Żentała · 25 September 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

Europe met its labour shortage by moving people and hoping. The alternative: free online education, published entry criteria, an open door for those we want.

Importing labour will not carry an aging continent; machines will have to do most of the work. That argument is in Designing Our Retirement and it leaves a hole people are right to point at. Saying “not this” is not a position. Europe will still need people. Some of them will come from outside Europe, and they should. The question is how we choose them, what we offer them, and what we owe the places they leave.

This is not about closing a door. It is about deciding, in the open, who walks through it, and about doing something for the people who never will.

What our governments actually did

They took the lazy route, and then acted surprised.

Instead of teaching people where they already live, we moved them. We set up incentives that selected for who could reach a border and endure the crossing, rather than for who had prepared. We brought people into countries whose language they did not speak, into labour markets that had no use for their actual skills, with no published standard anyone could have worked toward. Then we looked at the result, expensive, resented and bad for almost everyone involved, and called it an unavoidable consequence of a complicated world.

It was not complicated. It was stupid. And I want to be precise about who I am calling stupid, because this argument gets dirty fast: I mean the governments that designed this, not the people who walked into it. Somebody who crosses a desert for a chance at a wage is behaving rationally. The policy that made that the entry exam is the thing that failed.

The failure is a design failure. If you never state your criteria, you still have criteria; you just let geography, smugglers and luck pick for you.

Teach first, then invite

Almost all of the alternative is available today over the internet.

Europe builds free online education for anyone in the world who wants it. Real courses, in European working languages: English first, and German, French, Polish and the rest where there is demand. Language teaching, vocational training, and full remote degrees from European universities. Free at the point of use, open to anybody with a connection. This should be our strategic idea about work, not a side programme.

And alongside it, we publish the criteria. Plainly, on a page anyone can read: this is the language level, this is the qualification, this is the demonstrated skill. Meet them and you may come and work. Do not meet them and you may not, and you will have known that from the beginning rather than after selling everything you own.

That turns the whole thing around. The selection stops being who got here and becomes who put in the work. You show that you learned the language. You show you finished the school. You show you can do the job. Then we bring you over, deliberately, knowing what we are getting, and so do you. We will need workers; that is the premise of this site. I would rather we chose the capable and the determined, openly, by a standard they could see, than take whoever arrives and pretend it was a plan.

The certificate is not a problem

A lot of people in the global south will enrol in a remote European university mainly for the European piece of paper. I do not think that is an objection.

The education is real either way. A degree taken seriously enough to finish leaves somebody more capable, whatever their first motive was. It helps lift people out of poverty, with us or at home. And in many countries the remote European course will simply be better than what is locally on offer, which means our self-interested recruitment channel is also, without any extra effort, the best schooling available to a lot of people who will never leave.

I am content with a system where the incentive that gets someone in the door is a piece of paper, and the thing they walk out with is a skill.

Taking responsibility instead of importing people

Now the larger part, and the one I care about most.

If we only build a pipeline for the ablest, we have creamed off exactly the people a poor country needs and left everyone else where they were. So the education programme has to be much bigger than recruitment, and it has to be aimed at people staying put. Practical knowledge, free, translated by machine into hundreds of languages. This is the thing that changed in the last few years: translating a course into three hundred African languages used to be an impossible budget. Now it is a technical task, and it is cheap for the reason everything on this site is meant to be cheap: made once, used everywhere.

To stop global migration we have to start helping people where they are. Europe has Africa next door: a continent with a young and growing population and, in many places, nothing worth staying for. Perhaps we should take responsibility for that Africa. Either we do, or we spend the next fifty years arguing about boats.

Collecting what humanity knows about the practical business of living, translating it into every language it can reach, and giving it away is the cheapest serious thing Europe could do for the world. It happens to serve us too, which is why it might actually get done.

Bringing people here, and sending them back

The last piece is one people find uncomfortable, and it is the piece I am surest about.

China sent its people to the United States to study and work, and then brought them home. We should do the same thing, pointed the other way: this time we do it for them. Educate people where they are. Bring them to Europe for a few years to work and learn properly. And prepare them, while they are here, to go home carrying something: capital, a qualification, contacts, the concrete experience of how a functioning system runs.

That means the return is designed, not enforced as an afterthought. Some will want to stay. Fine; we needed workers, that was the point. But the default relationship should be an exchange, not a drain.

The two objections I hear most

You are creaming off the ablest and calling it aid.

A recruitment channel alone would do exactly that. The answer is the size of the programme and its direction: most of it aimed at people who stay, and the people who come trained to go back with something. Without those two parts the objection stands.

Fair against a pipeline. Not against a programme with a return path.

People will enrol for the paper, not the education.

A course finished for the certificate still leaves a skill behind. The incentive can be the paper; the outcome is the person who can do the job.

Yes, and it does not matter.

An open door for Japan and Korea

Here is a case where Europe has the opposite problem. Not “how do we choose among people who want to come”, but “why are we making it hard for people we should be actively inviting”.

It is a scandal that we do not offer Japanese and Korean people easy access to our labour market and an easy way to settle here. The Union should make that simple and has not. They are exactly the people a country should want: creative, hard-working, and often looking for a way out of a work culture that grinds them down. It is a scandal that we make it hard for them. I would like Poland to be the country that opens the door: work visas that are easy to get, a settlement path a person can plan a life around, and on top of that the thing Canada does under the name of a working holiday. Come, look around, take casual work or a permanent job, and be told plainly that you are welcome.

We already have a thin version of this, which is the good news and also the point.

Canada’s International Experience Canada admits Japanese citizens under Working Holiday and South Korean citizens under all three of its categories. Poland signed a working-holiday agreement with Japan in 2015 and one with South Korea in 2018; they sit alongside six others on the ministry’s list. So the argument is not “we have nothing”. It is that what we have is tiny and kept that way: five hundred places a year, ages eighteen to thirty, one year, and written so that the work is incidental to a holiday rather than the point of the trip. A cultural-exchange gesture. Not a door.

Widen it. Raise or drop the cap. Lift the age ceiling. Let the work be the reason you came. Make the year convertible into a residence permit for anyone who found something worth staying for. None of this requires a new theory of migration; it requires taking an instrument that already exists and stopping being timid with it. And Poland could do most of it alone: admission rules are largely national, and we signed the last two agreements without asking anyone’s permission.

A national decision

If there were direct democracy, this is one of the things I would bring to a vote, as my own proposal, in Poland. Every country can settle such things for itself, and I am against having them imposed on me. I will make the case and try to win the vote. That is the whole of my claim.

It is the same principle as the rest of this chapter. Decide openly who you are inviting, say so out loud, and build the route. The alternative is not neutrality; it is having no policy and then describing whatever happens as though somebody chose it.

What would have to be established first

The scheme rests on one claim: that somebody who learned the language and finished a remote course integrates and performs better than somebody selected the current way. That is testable. Run one cohort, follow it for five years, publish what happened. Two smaller questions sit under it: whether machine translation is good enough to teach wound care in a language with little written material (one wrong instruction in medicine is worse than none), and what makes a returnee’s knowledge stick once they are back, which is the part with the worst track record. Which language level and which qualifications, for which jobs, is work for people who know the labour market, not for me.

What this means for you

Europe has spent twenty years with one side shouting let them in and the other keep them out, and hardly anyone saying the third thing.

  • If you can teach something practical, a trade, first aid, bookkeeping, it can be recorded, translated and given away this year, by you, without waiting for a European programme.
  • If you work at a university, a remote track open to students outside Europe is an administrative decision, not a treaty.
  • If you argue about migration in public, carry the constructive half: teach them, publish the standard, choose openly, and do something about the reason they are leaving.
