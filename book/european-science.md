Ask why Europe has no AI of its own and you will get answers about venture
capital, energy prices and regulation. I think the answer is older and duller
than that.

Perhaps there is no European AI because there is no European science, and there
is no European science because we do not speak English together.

## What we have and what we do not

We do have joint research. There is CERN. But look at what it is: theoretical
physics, one place, one narrow field, somewhere people go on trips. Now look at
the fields that will decide the next twenty years, the practical ones that
actually count: artificial intelligence, robotics, bioengineering. There is no
place where researchers from every member state sit next to each other, day
after day, shoulder to shoulder, and work on those.

| What Europe has | What Europe does not have |
|---|---|
| Conferences | A room |
| Consortia that file reports | People from every country working side by side on one hard problem |
| A shared physics laboratory | A shared laboratory for AI, robotics or bioengineering |
| Twenty-four official languages | One working language for science |

Sitting in one room requires being able to talk in it. That is not a detail you
can solve later with better funding; it is the floor everything else stands on,
which is why [European English](/book/european-english) comes first in this
book and this chapter comes after it. Language integration is upstream of
European science, and European science is upstream of European business. If we
get the first one, the most able people in Europe can work together. If we do
not, they will keep working for someone else, in English, somewhere else.

## What a European science would work on

Not everything. Our problems.

Europe is old and getting older; that is the whole argument of
[Designing Our Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement), and it points at a
short list of things worth putting money and talent into: predictive medicine,
longevity and healthy life, psychology, artificial intelligence,
bioengineering.

Predictive medicine is the one people understand least. The nearest definition
in the literature is what Leroy Hood called P4 medicine, predictive, preventive,
personalised and participatory
([Hood and Flores, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nbt.2012.03.004)): instead
of waiting for disease to declare itself and then treating it, you use data to
estimate who is heading toward it and act early. Treating diabetes is medicine.
Knowing five years out that this person is heading for diabetes, and changing
the course, is predictive medicine. For a continent with a shrinking
working-age population, the difference between those two is the difference
between a health service we can afford and one we cannot.

But the interesting work is not only the medical science. It is everything
around it, and almost none of it is done:

- How do you program the AI that does this, and how do you build a health
  service around it rather than bolting it onto the side?
- How do you measure all of it, so that the system learns from the medical
  data?
- How do you make that safe, and how do you learn from the data at all?
- How do you keep improving the whole system once it is running?
- And the question underneath all of them: are our research methods actually
  good, and are we studying the right things?

That last one is not a rhetorical flourish. A research programme that never
checks whether its own methods work will produce a large amount of confident
nothing.

Above all, European scientists should be the ones sitting over European medical
data. This should be a European project, since we already have the rules on
personal data. The **European Health Data Space**, Regulation (EU) 2025/327, in
force since 26 March 2025, is the legal route for reusing health data across
borders for research and policy, through data access bodies, permits and secure
processing environments, on top of GDPR
([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/327/oj)). The rules exist.
The science that should be using them does not exist in the same way. We built
the door and no one built the building behind it.

## European universities

If the point is people from every country working shoulder to shoulder, it has
to be built as such. European universities, taking people from across the
Union, with no dominance of any one nation: not a French institute with foreign
guests, not a German one; ours.

Where they go, I do not know. In all the cities that want the AI centres, or
somewhere next to them. Or perhaps they should not be there at all. What does
not depend on the map: people from different countries have to work on
artificial intelligence here, and the same goes for bioengineering, and the
same goes for psychology.

## Mental health is an illness, not a personal failing

That last one is where I would start, because it is the largest untreated
problem we have and the one where the method can be tested fastest.

I think what we are looking at is a civilisational illness, and it comes from
having changed the way we live very fast. We did not get enough social support,
and we did not see how relationships work between different people: not only
our parents and children in a closed home, where we are hostages of our
parents, with no aunt, no uncle, no grandmother to go to, cry at, hug, ask for
something, and use to balance the power of the parents. Only those parents, who
are themselves stressed by work and by carrying the whole responsibility for the
family, in a way that was not true before. On top of that, parents keep
children at home, under a bell jar, so they do not get enough social skill or
social awareness.

You cannot learn to read other people without other people to read.

The conclusion I draw is not that anyone failed. It is that this should be
treated the way we treat illness, which it probably is: something that gets
diagnosed, treated and supported, without shame. Not a private weakness you are
supposed to fix on your own time.

## Therapy at scale

Who, if not the state, is to stand behind the chatbots?

Millions of people already confide in them. OpenAI, I am told, hires
psychologists. But somebody has to stand behind the chatbots that people bring
their problems to, and start helping them put those problems in order, in an
organised way. This could not be done with therapy alone; it can be done with
intelligent chatbots. From inside the conversation the system should be able to
say: this is the kind of thing a therapist deals with, would you like to hand
this over and talk to a human therapist? Behind the chatbot there is a human
therapist, funded by the state, helping the chatbot, or even a panel of them.
They can switch you over digitally, if you agree. I think many people will
agree, if the care on the other side is good.

With AI we can research, measure and test an enormous amount: psychological
things, therapeutic things. What a therapist once had to do with a person, we
can automate or half-automate. My rough estimate, and I want it read as an
estimate and not a finding, is that around seventy per cent of therapeutic work
can be automated and around thirty per cent should stay with a human. Nobody
has measured this. Measuring it is exactly the kind of thing a European science
of psychology should do.

The thirty matters more than the seventy. It is a human who has contact with
the patient, meets them from time to time and talks, and, this is the part
people miss, shares their own feelings and insight with the agent; that gives
the agent a human perspective. That person is also the supervisor of the
therapy. Perhaps several people are: one therapist who holds the meetings, and
two supervisors who follow the case and talk it over weekly or monthly. Care at
scale, with humans where humans are irreplaceable, and oversight that is plural
rather than a single overworked person.

<ObjectionList title="The two objections, answered as I would answer them">
  <Objection claim="There are nowhere near enough therapists, and there never will be." verdict="Correct, and it is the reason for the design, not against it.">
    On the old model the supply of care is capped by how many trained humans
    can sit in a room for an hour. That is how the shortfall gets closed: not by
    training ten times more therapists, which will not happen, but by putting
    the scarce human hours where they count and letting the machine carry the
    rest, under supervision.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="People will not hand their worst moments to a state-backed system. It is a privacy question." verdict="Less than it looks.">
    Many people have similar problems. If it is treated as an illness, which it
    probably is, one that is treated and supported, then the handover is
    consent to care, not surveillance. It happens only if the person agrees, and
    I think many will, if the care is good.
  </Objection>
</ObjectionList>

The same logic runs outward from there. Psychologists at the back of every
large event, and visible information about the other ways of coping: therapy,
therapists from the different schools, the reasonably sensible ones, people who
can spot a problem, help from another angle, say that perhaps this would suit
you better than that. And the personal-development and psychology market
regulated, because at the moment anyone can sell anything to people at their
worst.

<EvidenceBox title="The first study a European psychology should run">

Whether the split above is anywhere near right: take one condition with a
standard therapeutic protocol, run it with a supervised agent doing the
structured work and a human doing the rest, and publish the outcomes against
the fully human arm. That one study would also test the method this chapter is
about: whether European scientists over European data, under the rules we
already wrote, can answer a European question faster than anyone else.

</EvidenceBox>

None of this works without the research underneath it. Which is the point of
the chapter: a European science, on European problems, with European scientists
over European data, and mental health as the first thing large enough, and
neglected enough, to prove whether we can do it.
