I want to argue two things in one chapter, and they cut against each other.

The first is that our public administration still runs on the habits of the
typewriter era, that this wastes enormous amounts of human life, and that we
could fix it inside a decade. The second is that my own complaint is unfair,
because digitising a state is not a technical job. **A state becomes total as it
becomes digital.** The digital state and the total state are the same system
seen from two sides: the more of your life runs through it, the more completely
somebody at a console can reach into it.

Both halves are true at once. Anyone who only says the first is selling
software. Anyone who only says the second ends up defending the fax machine. The
question is what has to be built so that the first can happen without the
second eating us.

## The opportunity we are sitting on

Go into a public office and watch what happens to a piece of paper. Somebody
retypes what somebody else already typed. A form is checked against a register
the clerk cannot query directly. A case moves at the speed of an envelope. None
of this is anyone's fault; it is a set of procedures designed before the
machines existed that would make them unnecessary, and never redesigned after.

The potential in digitalisation and automation is enormous, and it is not mainly
about saving money. It is about quality of life: work nobody has to do by hand
any more, decisions that arrive in days instead of seasons, a citizen who can
see the state of their own case. The courts get something extra out of being
rebuilt properly — a system that records what it did, in order, as a by-product
of working at all. **Transparency you get for free because the thing was built
well is worth more than transparency somebody has to promise.**

In Poland there is a clock on this. The people who staff that administration
are leaving. My mother retires in two years, and that is roughly the window we
have to reform public administration before the knowledge that keeps it running
walks out of the building with the people who hold it. Nobody is queuing to
replace them at those wages, and most of that work does not need a human being
at all. I would not take one of those jobs, and I do not know many people who
would. That is the honest measure of how long this can be postponed.

## The objection I have to make against myself

Then somebody asks the question I cannot answer with code: **who rules the data,
and on what terms?**

<Callout kind="claim" label="Three properties of a digital state">
  Once the register is the truth, whoever controls the register controls what is
  true about you. Once identity is a credential rather than a face, the
  credential can be suspended. Once money is an entry in a ledger, the entry can
  be frozen, expired, or made conditional on behaviour. None of this needs
  anyone to have evil intentions; they are properties of the system, available
  to whoever holds it next.
</Callout>

This is why people protest against digital ID and digital currency, and the
protests are more rational than their opponents admit. The objection is not
technophobia. The objection is that **a switch exists, and somebody can turn a
person off.** You do not have to believe today's government would do it. You
have to notice that today's government is not the last one.

So my complaint about the typewriter-era state was unfair — not wrong, unfair.
Digitalisation was never a project for engineers to deliver and politics to
approve afterwards. The question of who governs the data is the harder half,
and it has to be settled first, in public, by the people whose lives run through
the thing.

## Why a digital state is a total state

"Total" here does not mean totalitarian. It means total in reach: a state that
touches everything, sees everything, and can act everywhere at once.

The old state was limited by friction. Surveillance cost people. Enforcement
cost time. If a government wanted to make your life impossible, somebody had to
do the work, and the work was expensive enough to be reserved for people who
mattered. Those costs are what protected ordinary citizens — not laws, friction.

A digital state removes the friction. That is the whole point of it and why it
delivers real benefits: the same property that lets a service reach you in a
second without an office visit lets a decision about you take effect in a second
without a hearing. You cannot keep one and refuse the other. They are the same
capability seen from two ends.

**The conclusion is not to stop building.** A state that refuses to digitalise
does not stay free; it stays slow, and eventually buys the systems anyway, late,
from someone else, on someone else's terms. The conclusion is that the power has
to be balanced at the same time as the capability is built.

## The counterweight

The balance I want is direct democracy, at every level where a decision is
made: the school, the municipality, the region, the nation, the Union.

Not as a mood, and not as consultation. As the two instruments with teeth that
[Direct Democracy](/book/direct-democracy#the-words-in-one-place) sets out: a
citizens' initiative to force a vote on a change, and an optional referendum to
strike down a law parliament has passed. Parliaments carry on — the precise name
is semi-direct democracy — and citizens decide specific questions, often, and
the result binds.

Then the relationship inverts. A state with total reach and citizens who can
overrule it between elections is a tool. A state with total reach and citizens
who can only complain for four years is something else, and we should say the
word out loud rather than discover it later.

<Callout kind="demand" label="The demand">
  Digital identity arrives together with universal direct democracy across the
  Union. Same package, same decade. Not the capability first and the
  accountability "in a later phase", because later phases are where
  accountability goes to die.
</Callout>

And the mirror of it, which is the uncomfortable part: **while no counterweight
ships alongside these systems, citizens are entitled to resist them.** Not
because resisting is wise — refusing digitalisation forever is a losing position
— but because the resistance is a correct reading of the deal on offer.
Governments across Europe have spent two decades failing at the ordinary things,
crisis after crisis, and are now asking for more direct power over people's
lives. Declining that trade is not superstition. It is arithmetic.

I would rather the demand were the other one: give us the vote and we will help
you build the system.

## Keep the paper

There is a cheap way to take most of the fear out of this, and I do not know why
it is not the default position.

<Playbook title="The order of build" steps={[
  { action: "Keep paper as the emergency layer of the state.", why: "Not only for voting — for the basic functions. Today's procedures stay in place, simplified if you like, as the fallback that works when the systems are down, when somebody has been switched off by mistake, or when a government has to be shown the old route still exists." },
  { action: "Vote in the school and the town hall first.", why: "We already vote in one room in a school; a town hall can do the same. If the cost of frequent votes is the objection, the honest response is to work out how to run them cheaply, in public — not to postpone." },
  { action: "Build the digital layer in parallel, as learning.", why: "Start now, make mistakes where they are survivable, and let it carry something serious only once it has proved in the open that it deserves to." },
  { action: "Build it as Europeans, together.", why: "One set of systems for identity, records and voting, designed jointly, with each country deploying and running its own. The engineering is done once; the sovereignty stays where it is; nobody depends on a platform they cannot inspect." },
]} />

A state that cannot operate without its database has handed whoever holds the
database a power nobody voted to give them.

<EvidenceBox title="What would show this can work">
  Nobody has done this at Union scale, so the chapter does not claim it works.
  Three pieces of work would settle it: whether a binding citizen vote can be
  given legal force at national and European level without rewriting the
  treaties; whether a jointly built identity system can be deployed nationally
  without creating one central operator with a switch; and whether frequent
  voting at municipal and regional level changes outcomes, which the Swiss
  record can answer better than speculation.
</EvidenceBox>

## What Central Europe puts in

This is where my part of Europe stops being a recipient and starts being a
contributor, and I would like that said plainly for once.

Poland and Central Europe have the software engineers. If the new Union needs a
digital administration, an autonomous health service, digital courts and
offices, a voting system nobody can quietly rig, then that is work we can
actually do, and it is what we should put into the common system rather than
another round of asking what we get out of it.

Germany built SAP: software that runs companies, sold to the world. **Europe
could build the system that runs a state** — digital administration, digital
democracy, predictive healthcare, digital courts — built here, deployed here
first, then exported, because every other democracy on earth will face the same
problem and most would rather not buy the answer from a superpower. That is the
difference between plumbing and an industry. We should aim at the industry.
