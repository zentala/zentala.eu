We vote every four years. For twenty years it has got worse.

That is not a mood, it is a record. Start at the crash of 2008 and follow the
line: one crisis, then another, then a third, and at the end of it a continent
talking about war rather than about what it is going to build. Every few years
we were offered a new set of names and we took it, and the direction did not
change. Twenty years is enough evidence. Representative democracy is not
delivering for Europe, and it is not going to start next term.

I have been waiting for the election that turns this around since I started
writing about reform, and it has not come. At some point the honest conclusion
is that the problem is not which people we elect. It is the arrangement we
elect them into.

## Why it fails — the fair version

I do not know which of these weighs most. Four explanations, and they lead to
the same place:

- **Some of them may mean badly.** Possible, and unprovable at the scale people
  usually claim it.
- **Some of them cannot cope.** The problems are large, slow and technical; the
  job rewards speed, visibility and the next cycle.
- **A body of a few hundred people is an easy thing to manipulate or corrupt.**
  Wherever a country's decisions are taken by three hundred or six hundred
  identifiable individuals, influencing that country means influencing a few
  hundred people. That is a cheap, well-understood operation.
- **The capable people do not go.** In Poland a well-paid programmer earns more
  than the president, and does not have strangers throwing filth at him for a
  living. We built a selection mechanism and then act surprised by what it
  selects.

Whichever mix is true, the fix has to be structural. This system has failed us
and we need a new one.

## Where the lever is

If I am going to fight for something, I want it to be the thing that moves the
most.

Most political energy online goes into a loop: a government does something
foolish, we name it, we prove it is foolish although everyone already knows, we
build enough outrage to raise the cost, and next month it happens again. That
loop does not compound. So the question is not *what is wrong* but *where the
levers are*, and I find two.

**The first lever is direct democracy.** The ability to decide specific
questions ourselves, often, with a result that binds. It changes every other
fight at once, because it changes who has to be persuaded.

One piece of precision, once. What Switzerland has, and what I am proposing, is
**semi-direct democracy**: parliament and government carry on, and citizens
hold two instruments to intervene in their work — the **citizens' initiative**,
which proposes a change and forces a vote on it, and the **optional
referendum**, which strikes down a law parliament has passed. The accelerator
and the brake. I will go on saying "direct democracy" through the rest of this,
because it is the phrase everyone uses and it names the thing we are adding.

  The two instruments, the Swiss numbers and the ask on this site are stated in

</SideNote>

**The second lever is English as a working language.** That one is economic —
the floor under a European digital economy, European media, European companies
whose capital is not French or German but simply European. [The floor
layer](/vision#language) makes that argument. Language is the economy's lever.
Direct democracy is the political one, and without it the rest is a wish list
handed to people who have not delivered for twenty years.

## It is also the democratic answer to the anger

I hear voices calling for aggression — against migrants, sometimes against
politicians. That anger is real and much of what caused it is real too. But
there are democratic ways to settle this, and if we do not put one of them in
front of people, the other kind of answer is what fills the space.

**Direct democracy is the democratic answer.** It takes the same anger and
points it at a mechanism instead of at a group of human beings. It gives a
person who feels ignored something to demand that is specific, legal and
winnable. Discontent with nothing to do becomes contempt; discontent with an
instrument becomes reform.

## Copy the Swiss model

Switzerland has run binding citizen votes at federal, cantonal and communal
level for well over a century: the one large, long-running, working example of
a country where citizens routinely decide policy directly and the state still
functions. It has costs — turnout, money, slowness — and [the safeguards
chapter](/book/safeguards#what-the-swiss-model-costs) lists them. But it is
proven, it is European, and it is documented. **Adopt it broadly. Do not
reinvent it.** Every hour spent designing a novel mechanism is an hour not
spent demanding one that already runs.

Two things follow from copying rather than inventing. **The votes are
binding** — both instruments. A vote the government may read as advice is the
instrument we already have, and it is the one that failed. And **the thresholds
are shaped by Switzerland's**, scaled to our electorates and rounded: my figure
is **2% of the electorate to launch an initiative and 1% to force a
referendum**, stated once [below](#the-words-in-one-place), with the arithmetic
for Poland and the Union in [the safeguards
chapter](/book/safeguards#the-arithmetic-at-our-scale). Copying the model means
copying its safety mechanisms too; that chapter is the itemised list.

## The whole Union at once

This is the part people get wrong, and the cost of getting it wrong is
historical. We wrote the Constitution of the Third of May in 1791, the first
written constitution in Europe, and within four years Poland did not exist. A
single country that reforms itself into something better while its neighbours
do not is a country that gets rolled over. **One state reforming alone is a
target. A continent reforming together is a fact.**

So the demand is European from the first day — school councils, municipalities,
regions, national parliaments, the Union — everywhere at once, by peoples who
have understood that they have the same problem. Each country deploys its own
system; we build the systems together, as Europeans.

And the countries that do not want it? Then they do not want it, and nothing
happens to them. We aim at doing it together anyway, the way we should with a
common working language: by making it obviously worth joining. Doing it
together is part of the point. A demand made across the continent at the same
time is itself an act of European identity — Europeans changing something
together, by their own decision — and even a Europe of nations can own it,
because it takes nothing from any nation. It would show how much we already
have in common.

## Build something to vote for

A mechanism is not a programme. If we win the instrument and have nothing to
put through it, we will have replaced one kind of drift with another.

So the second half of the playbook is to build technocratic political
movements across the whole Union — patriotic and national as much as European,
because these are not opposites. Their offer is real work rather than
grievance: rebuild the Union digitally, on a common working language, with
digital administration, digital healthcare, digital courts and a serious
industrial programme in robotics. We have roughly thirty years of working
economy left to build it, and nobody currently in charge is building it. What
Central Europe can put in — software, digital democracy, administration and
courts, as an export — is argued in [The Total
State](/book/the-total-state#what-central-europe-puts-in).

## Elections do not stop

I am not proposing that we abolish parliaments or stop voting for governments.
We keep electing representatives and we keep having parties; that is what makes
this semi-direct. **Direct democracy is how citizens force things through in
between** — the initiative to push something on, the referendum to stop
something passing — and a correction mechanism changes how representatives
behave long before it is used, which is most of its value. If the
representatives will not deliver it, people demand it in the street until a
government that refuses it stands down. That sentence belongs on the page, not
between the lines.

A separate proposal on this site — [electing representatives by
portfolio](/book/choosing-people) — changes how they are chosen rather than
what citizens can do between elections. Whether it belongs inside this
programme is not settled here.

## Brexit, and what it proved

Consider what happened in Britain. An enormous social mobilisation, years of
argument, a vote to leave — and afterwards, immigration from elsewhere went up
rather than down. That was not democracy. It was a one-off vote, and citizens
went on not steering their state, which is why they are dissatisfied again with
the next thing it does. There is no continuous mechanism, and a single vote
every few years holds almost nothing.

## The Union of Nations

What I am proposing is a **Union of Nations**: nations get the tools to steer
themselves — real instruments, held by their own citizens, at every level from a
school council upward — and at the same time we integrate deeply where
integration actually pays, at the level of digital administration, of how our
states function as software.

The reason is economic. The economy we are walking into has few workers, and
the ones it has must be able to move — take a job in another member state
without losing their records, qualifications, healthcare history or pension.
That requires common digital plumbing. It does not require a nation to stop
being itself. And it is not a replacement for the Union we have: the Union of
Nations rebuilds the present Union from the inside. Nobody has to leave
anything, and nothing has to be dissolved first.

<EvidenceBox title="What I cannot settle, and what would">
  Two things. **Legal force:** what it takes for a binding citizen vote to have
  real effect at national and at Union level, and what the existing European
  Citizens' Initiative can and cannot do today — a lawyer's memo would settle
  it, and [the first move](/book/first-move) is built to find out. **Which
  questions:** where the boundary sits between what citizens decide directly and
  what needs people working on it full-time. My answer so far is Swiss practice:
  specialists prepare, citizens can intervene at any stage, and on the important
  questions they approve or reject. Where exactly that line falls is a study of
  the Swiss record, not a sentence from me.
</EvidenceBox>

## The words, in one place

These are the standard terms of the field, defined here once. The other
chapters and the glossary point back to this section rather than restating
them.

<Callout kind="definition" term="Semi-direct democracy" label="Definition · Semi-direct democracy">
  A system where parliament and government keep working as they do now, and
  citizens hold instruments to propose law and to overturn it. Switzerland's
  system, and what this site proposes.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="definition" term="Citizens' initiative" label="Definition · Citizens' initiative">
  Citizens collect signatures to propose a change and force a national vote on
  it, whether or not parliament wants one. In Switzerland: [100,000 signatures
  within 18 months](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/1999/404/en), for a
  change to the federal constitution. The ask on this site: 2% of the
  electorate, the author's figure. The accelerator.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="definition" term="Optional referendum" label="Definition · Optional referendum">
  Citizens collect signatures to put a law parliament has already passed to a
  national vote, which can strike it down. In Switzerland: [50,000 voters or
  eight cantons within 100 days](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/1999/404/en)
  of publication. The ask on this site: 1% of the electorate, the author's
  figure. The brake.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="definition" term="Unity of subject" label="Definition · Unity of subject">
  An initiative may not bundle unrelated matters together, and may not mix a
  general proposal with a specific draft. Breach it and the Federal Assembly
  declares the initiative invalid. It kills the rider — the popular thing
  carrying the unpopular thing through on its back.
</Callout>

## We are the sovereign

Underneath all of it is a claim of right, and I want to state it as a claim
rather than as a complaint. We are the sovereign. Not the parliament, not the
administration, not the governments that have failed us in one country after
another. The power is ours and it is lent. Given what the last twenty years
have produced, taking more of it back is not a radical act. It is the ordinary
response of an owner who has watched a manager underperform for two decades.
