Redesigning the State

The frame · Argument

By Paweł Żentała · 25 September 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

Political systems expire: conditions change and incentives get used up or hacked. Then you restart the game, and who writes the design brief decides everything.

I had a dream once. We were flying through space on a ship, and we were the fourth generation aboard. Our parents were around forty-two; we were twenty-three, twenty-four. We were patching the ship. Pipes, mostly, with two-part universal glue, the kind that is meant for everything, and grey insulating tape. They leaked. The ship was breaking down around us, coming apart while we watched. Parts rusting. A handrail came away and somebody fell. Pressure escaping somewhere, constantly. It was hot, and we could not regulate the temperature.

Here is the part of the dream that stayed with me. The people who designed that ship designed it well. So well that the next two generations never had to service it. One generation did not have to think about it at all, and only the one after that started having any problems. But because it had been so good for so long, the knowledge of how to tune the thing so it did not produce those problems in the first place, how to maintain it, how to rebuild parts of it, how to repair, did not survive. So the fourth generation could not cope. Glue and tape were all they had.

It is a little like that with the European Union, and with every other political system. A system goes out of date, because the times and the circumstances it was built for have changed. But something else happens too: the incentive systems get used up, or they get hacked. Everybody reaches the finish line, and then something unsettling starts: corruption, elbowing in at the trough. At some point competition disappears and what counts is the access you inherited, handed down from one generation to the next.

That is when you have to restart the game. We are living in those times. We will be restarting this game, and it is worth thinking hard about how to set it up properly, so that the incentives are right for the generations after us, and maybe even for us, if we happen to live a long time.

The redesign is not a takeover

We need to redesign democracy so that it serves our interests: not so that anyone dominates it in some bad way, but because there has to be a balance between public servants and society, and the balance has to pay off. Good for the people who want to work and get somewhere. Good for the people who want to serve the state well; they should get something out of doing it well. A motivating package, with benefits for citizens and for officials alike. The state has to be thought through again, from the beginning.

This is not a generous afterthought. It is the whole mechanism. A system where serving the public honestly is a worse deal than the alternatives will select for people who take the alternatives, and no amount of complaining about their character changes the arithmetic. Anything that only works for one of the two sides is not a design; it is a fight, and fights get won back.

Popularity contests, not contests of solutions

What counts in politics today is popularity. Popular views, you have to match them, and that is how groups form: around popular opinions, or popular ways of feeling. These are popularity contests. I feel this way, you feel that way. There is no contest of solutions.

I am a positivist, so my question is a different one: how do you see this being solved, so that both you and I end up satisfied? Where is the line, where is the mechanism? How do we find the core, and build from the beginning, from values, from what actually matters to us?

Those values are under threat now, perhaps even the European ones, from the institutions that were supposed to guard them and from the people who work inside them, who do not respect us and who trampled the fact that we are the sovereign. So we start from values again. Democracy, for instance: where is it, how do you build it. Not complaining, not shouting; doing the groundwork, and the groundwork is designing a state. I think we can design one. I do not think it is that hard, with artificial intelligence to help. The problem is simply that almost nobody is working on it.

Who designs the digital state

This is the thesis I hold most strongly in this chapter, and I would rather it were argued against than ignored.

A digital state

The digital state

A state that touches everything, sees everything and can act everywhere at once — total in reach, not totalitarian.

Read where it is argued → All terms

is the same system The Total State describes from the other side: total in reach, and therefore potentially totalitarian. Which makes it dangerous for officials to be the ones deciding what it looks like and what its architecture is. They will build it so that steering us is convenient for them, so that they can do it easily, along the path of least resistance. That is not malice. It is normal. Everyone optimises the energy they spend.

The mistake is not theirs; the mistake is handing them the pen.

The nation’s interest points the other way, and it is worth writing out as a design brief rather than as a complaint.

The design brief

The political and digital system should extract the most value out of the state machine, not be the easiest thing to steer. It should force competition and advancement among those who govern, reward competence and good ideas, support European diversity while integrating, and leave room to experiment.

Every item on that list makes the system harder to operate from a console and better at producing outcomes. That tension is the point. If a proposed architecture is easy to run and produces little, somebody optimised for the operator. Ask who wrote the brief.

A shared core, your own deployment

There is one thing worth taking from China: their regions have a lot of autonomy. They do not have a common digital state. I would build one.

Every country and region gets its own deployment, and decides how to customise it. But the core, where we have an enormous amount in common, can simply be shared. That way the whole European digital state works together, because we are all running the same infrastructure. And European digital startups get built on top of it, on European digital infrastructure. What that system would look like is set out as a proposal in European eGovernment.

Then the part I find more interesting than the country layer. Ideally, digital systems would be distributed where they are needed, rather than along administrative borders.

Different systems, deployed where they fit, built and shared in common. The same goes for legal and administrative arrangements: they can differ between regions according to the function the region performs. It does not always have to be “this is one country, so here we do it this way”. It can be more functional than that.

An offer to Germany

Now a proposal, and I want to label it clearly: this is my idea, not an agreed plan, and nobody has been asked.

What the Germans built, that whole complex German administration, could be carried into the digital world with its complexity intact, and I suspect it would then be comparatively light. I think the Germans could take this on. At least, it is what I would expect of them, and I think it is worth us expecting it of them. Germany is a bit lost right now, and we should have expectations of them. Offer a hand and say: listen, this is the role we would like you to play. Europe is short of that kind of leadership. As it stands, it may be the United States that says who leads, what Germany’s role is, what we allow them. Germany does not entirely know what to do with itself either. So give them an idea, one they gain from and one we all gain from. Win–win.

I assume we would end up considerably more uniform than China, and customise where we want to. Or perhaps not uniform at all, if the function-based picture above turns out to be the better one. I do not know yet which way that goes.

Longevity changes the equation

One more thing, and I flag it as speculation: I am guessing at a future, not reporting a fact.

The strongest case against this site's own premise, from its author

It is possible we will significantly extend life, and rejuvenate. If that happens, there probably is no labour-force problem at all in the shape we currently argue about it: not if we can biologically rejuvenate and bioengineering goes through a boom. The frame this site stands on assumes no such boom arrives in time; if one does, the argument about who will do the work changes shape, and the argument about who holds the state does not.

Whether we get access to those benefits, to bioengineering and to a long life of decent quality, depends on whether we organise ourselves, whether we arrange Europe so that it happens here. We are the sovereign; we have to organise it. Waiting for one party or another to do something does not work, and not because parties are bad: because the system is set up this way. We have to change the settings. We have to work out which system creates the right incentives for those in power.

What this means for you

The ship in the dream did not fail because it was badly built. It failed because nobody kept the knowledge of how to run it. We are the fourth generation, and the glue and tape are not going to hold.

  • Treat the system as a design, not as weather. It was built by people, for conditions that have changed, with incentives that have been used up or gamed. What was designed can be redesigned.
  • When somebody proposes a digital system, ask who wrote the brief. If the answer is the people who will operate it, expect a system that is convenient to operate and produces little.
  • Ask for the solution, not the feeling: how do you see this being solved so that both of us come out satisfied? Most positions do not survive that question.
