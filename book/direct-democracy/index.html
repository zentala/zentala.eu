Direct Democracy

How we get there · Argument

By Paweł Żentała · 25 September 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

Representative democracy has failed Europe for twenty years. Direct democracy is the highest-leverage demand, and this is the playbook for making it.

We vote every four years. For twenty years it has got worse.

That is not a mood, it is a record. Start at the crash of 2008 and follow the line: one crisis, then another, then a third, and at the end of it a continent talking about war rather than about what it is going to build. Every few years we were offered a new set of names and we took it, and the direction did not change. Twenty years is enough evidence. Representative democracy is not delivering for Europe, and it is not going to start next term.

I have been waiting for the election that turns this around since I started writing about reform, and it has not come. At some point the honest conclusion is that the problem is not which people we elect. It is the arrangement we elect them into.

Why it fails — the fair version

I do not know which of these weighs most. Four explanations, and they lead to the same place:

  • Some of them may mean badly. Possible, and unprovable at the scale people usually claim it.
  • Some of them cannot cope. The problems are large, slow and technical; the job rewards speed, visibility and the next cycle.
  • A body of a few hundred people is an easy thing to manipulate or corrupt. Wherever a country’s decisions are taken by three hundred or six hundred identifiable individuals, influencing that country means influencing a few hundred people. That is a cheap, well-understood operation.
  • The capable people do not go. In Poland a well-paid programmer earns more than the president, and does not have strangers throwing filth at him for a living. We built a selection mechanism and then act surprised by what it selects.

Whichever mix is true, the fix has to be structural. This system has failed us and we need a new one.

Where the lever is

If I am going to fight for something, I want it to be the thing that moves the most.

Most political energy online goes into a loop: a government does something foolish, we name it, we prove it is foolish although everyone already knows, we build enough outrage to raise the cost, and next month it happens again. That loop does not compound. So the question is not what is wrong but where the levers are, and I find two.

The first lever is direct democracy. The ability to decide specific questions ourselves, often, with a result that binds. It changes every other fight at once, because it changes who has to be persuaded.

One piece of precision, once. What Switzerland has, and what I am proposing, is semi-direct democracy: parliament and government carry on, and citizens hold two instruments to intervene in their work — the citizens’ initiative, which proposes a change and forces a vote on it, and the optional referendum, which strikes down a law parliament has passed. The accelerator and the brake. I will go on saying “direct democracy” through the rest of this, because it is the phrase everyone uses and it names the thing we are adding.

Defined once

The two instruments, the Swiss numbers and the ask on this site are stated in one place, at the end of this chapter; every other chapter points there.

The second lever is English as a working language. That one is economic — the floor under a European digital economy, European media, European companies whose capital is not French or German but simply European. The floor layer makes that argument. Language is the economy’s lever. Direct democracy is the political one, and without it the rest is a wish list handed to people who have not delivered for twenty years.

It is also the democratic answer to the anger

I hear voices calling for aggression — against migrants, sometimes against politicians. That anger is real and much of what caused it is real too. But there are democratic ways to settle this, and if we do not put one of them in front of people, the other kind of answer is what fills the space.

Direct democracy is the democratic answer. It takes the same anger and points it at a mechanism instead of at a group of human beings. It gives a person who feels ignored something to demand that is specific, legal and winnable. Discontent with nothing to do becomes contempt; discontent with an instrument becomes reform.

The two objections I expect

Nationalists will be the first to use the instrument.

The globalists warn us about the nationalists, and they may be partly right; I am wary of those forces too. That is an argument for the instrument. A new party that happens to be nationalist is no guarantee that the nation’s interest is served, and if representative democracy has failed once it can fail again in different colours. Direct democracy is what makes the change safe whoever wins the next election. It may even be that Europe has not swung harder because people fear those forces and have no other handle on their state. Give them the handle.

The fear of the next government is the case for the brake, not against it.

Crowds vote terrible things into constitutions on a bad Tuesday.

History’s record of mass harm is a record of what governments did, not what electorates voted for. And the Swiss model comes with its safety mechanisms attached — validity checks, a double majority

The playbook

So here is what I think we should do, in order. Three of the steps need more than a line; they have their own sections below.

  1. 1

    Do not only demand that this or that policy stop.

    The wave passes and the machine is unchanged. On the wave of the anger, demand direct democracy itself; every scandal is an argument for it.

  2. 2

    Treat this as the real dividing line.

    Not left against right: whether a person is willing to give power to people or not. Demanding it shows who is actually a democrat, because refusing to answer is an answer.

  3. 3

    Demand it of everyone.

    Government and opposition, the ones you like and the ones you do not. Push and vote for whoever is for it, from any camp, and note who suddenly finds reasons the public cannot be trusted.

  4. 4

    Paper referendums first, digital later.

    Paper works and nobody can dismiss it as a technology experiment. The digital layer is built beside it, as something we learn on, and paper stays as the fallback.

  5. 5

    Decide every three months, not every four years.

    A mandate you cannot correct for four years is not control, it is a receipt. Frequent decisions on real questions turn an audience into a principal.

  6. 6

    Copy the Swiss model. Do not get clever.

    A proven, European, documented model, adopted broadly rather than reinvented — binding votes, and the safeguards that come with them.

  7. 7

    Demand it across the whole Union at once.

    One state reforming alone is a target. A continent reforming together is a fact.

  8. 8

    Build something to vote for.

    A mechanism is not a programme. Technocratic movements across the Union, whose offer is real work rather than grievance.

Copy the Swiss model

Switzerland has run binding citizen votes at federal, cantonal and communal level for well over a century: the one large, long-running, working example of a country where citizens routinely decide policy directly and the state still functions. It has costs — turnout, money, slowness — and the safeguards chapter lists them. But it is proven, it is European, and it is documented. Adopt it broadly. Do not reinvent it. Every hour spent designing a novel mechanism is an hour not spent demanding one that already runs.

Two things follow from copying rather than inventing. The votes are binding — both instruments. A vote the government may read as advice is the instrument we already have, and it is the one that failed. And the thresholds are shaped by Switzerland’s, scaled to our electorates and rounded: my figure is 2% of the electorate to launch an initiative and 1% to force a referendum, stated once below, with the arithmetic for Poland and the Union in the safeguards chapter. Copying the model means copying its safety mechanisms too; that chapter is the itemised list.

The whole Union at once

This is the part people get wrong, and the cost of getting it wrong is historical. We wrote the Constitution of the Third of May in 1791, the first written constitution in Europe, and within four years Poland did not exist. A single country that reforms itself into something better while its neighbours do not is a country that gets rolled over. One state reforming alone is a target. A continent reforming together is a fact.

So the demand is European from the first day — school councils, municipalities, regions, national parliaments, the Union — everywhere at once, by peoples who have understood that they have the same problem. Each country deploys its own system; we build the systems together, as Europeans.

And the countries that do not want it? Then they do not want it, and nothing happens to them. We aim at doing it together anyway, the way we should with a common working language: by making it obviously worth joining. Doing it together is part of the point. A demand made across the continent at the same time is itself an act of European identity — Europeans changing something together, by their own decision — and even a Europe of nations can own it, because it takes nothing from any nation. It would show how much we already have in common.

Build something to vote for

A mechanism is not a programme. If we win the instrument and have nothing to put through it, we will have replaced one kind of drift with another.

So the second half of the playbook is to build technocratic political movements across the whole Union — patriotic and national as much as European, because these are not opposites. Their offer is real work rather than grievance: rebuild the Union digitally, on a common working language, with digital administration, digital healthcare, digital courts and a serious industrial programme in robotics. We have roughly thirty years of working economy left to build it, and nobody currently in charge is building it. What Central Europe can put in — software, digital democracy, administration and courts, as an export — is argued in The Total State.

Elections do not stop

I am not proposing that we abolish parliaments or stop voting for governments. We keep electing representatives and we keep having parties; that is what makes this semi-direct. Direct democracy is how citizens force things through in between — the initiative to push something on, the referendum to stop something passing — and a correction mechanism changes how representatives behave long before it is used, which is most of its value. If the representatives will not deliver it, people demand it in the street until a government that refuses it stands down. That sentence belongs on the page, not between the lines.

A separate proposal on this site — electing representatives by portfolio — changes how they are chosen rather than what citizens can do between elections. Whether it belongs inside this programme is not settled here.

Brexit, and what it proved

Consider what happened in Britain. An enormous social mobilisation, years of argument, a vote to leave — and afterwards, immigration from elsewhere went up rather than down. That was not democracy. It was a one-off vote, and citizens went on not steering their state, which is why they are dissatisfied again with the next thing it does. There is no continuous mechanism, and a single vote every few years holds almost nothing.

The Union of Nations

What I am proposing is a Union of Nations: nations get the tools to steer themselves — real instruments, held by their own citizens, at every level from a school council upward — and at the same time we integrate deeply where integration actually pays, at the level of digital administration, of how our states function as software.

The reason is economic. The economy we are walking into has few workers, and the ones it has must be able to move — take a job in another member state without losing their records, qualifications, healthcare history or pension. That requires common digital plumbing. It does not require a nation to stop being itself. And it is not a replacement for the Union we have: the Union of Nations rebuilds the present Union from the inside. Nobody has to leave anything, and nothing has to be dissolved first.

What I cannot settle, and what would

Two things. Legal force: what it takes for a binding citizen vote to have real effect at national and at Union level, and what the existing European Citizens’ Initiative can and cannot do today — a lawyer’s memo would settle it, and the first move is built to find out. Which questions: where the boundary sits between what citizens decide directly and what needs people working on it full-time. My answer so far is Swiss practice: specialists prepare, citizens can intervene at any stage, and on the important questions they approve or reject. Where exactly that line falls is a study of the Swiss record, not a sentence from me.

The words, in one place

These are the standard terms of the field, defined here once. The other chapters and the glossary point back to this section rather than restating them.

Definition · Semi-direct democracy

A system where parliament and government keep working as they do now, and citizens hold instruments to propose law and to overturn it. Switzerland’s system, and what this site proposes.

Definition · Citizens' initiative

Citizens collect signatures to propose a change and force a national vote on it, whether or not parliament wants one. In Switzerland: 100,000 signatures within 18 months, for a change to the federal constitution. The ask on this site: 2% of the electorate, the author’s figure. The accelerator.

Definition · Optional referendum

Citizens collect signatures to put a law parliament has already passed to a national vote, which can strike it down. In Switzerland: 50,000 voters or eight cantons within 100 days of publication. The ask on this site: 1% of the electorate, the author’s figure. The brake.

Definition · Unity of subject

An initiative may not bundle unrelated matters together, and may not mix a general proposal with a specific draft. Breach it and the Federal Assembly declares the initiative invalid. It kills the rider — the popular thing carrying the unpopular thing through on its back.

The double majority (of voters and of cantons or states) and the turnout quorum are defined where they do their work, in the safeguards; the European Citizens’ Initiative, the Union’s existing and non-binding instrument, in the first move.

We are the sovereign

Underneath all of it is a claim of right, and I want to state it as a claim rather than as a complaint. We are the sovereign. Not the parliament, not the administration, not the governments that have failed us in one country after another. The power is ours and it is lent. Given what the last twenty years have produced, taking more of it back is not a radical act. It is the ordinary response of an owner who has watched a manager underperform for two decades.

What this means for you

The playbook above needs no permission and no organisation to start. It starts with the next thing that makes you angry.

  • The next time something the state does makes you angry, do not demand the reversal. Demand the instrument — binding, Swiss, at every level — and say so wherever you were going to complain.
  • Ask every politician you can reach one question: will you give this power to people? Their answer sorts them faster than any manifesto.
