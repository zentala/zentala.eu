Robotic Reindustrialisation

Ceiling — robotics · Argument

By Paweł Żentała · 20 September 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

How Europe funds robotics through defence research now, converts it to civilian use after the war, and keeps the machines cheap enough that everyone benefits.

This is the top of the stack, and it only works because of what sits underneath it. The demographic argument says why we need it: there will not be enough people to do the work our retirement depends on. A common working language is what lets the engineers who build it work with each other. A digital state is what it plugs into. Robotics on its own, without those, is a trade fair.

What follows is how Europe could actually get there.

Europe needs a new industry anyway

The old engine was the car. It is being taken apart — by electrification, by Chinese manufacturing, by a shift in what a vehicle even is — and the Union’s own electric-car transition has been half reversed to keep the car companies alive. Germany knows this better than anyone, and so does every supplier town from Bavaria to Silesia that grew up around it.

Robotics is the natural successor. It needs precision manufacturing, which Europe has; industrial engineering culture, which Europe has; and software, which Europe has been losing at — and which is exactly where Central Europe can contribute something the rest of the continent needs. Germany could rebuild its entire car industry to produce robots, and be an industrial-robotic power. So this is not only a demographic defence. It is the reindustrialisation Europe was going to have to attempt regardless, aimed at a problem that makes it urgent.

What Poland and Central Europe bring

Every member state asks what it can offer the Union that the Union actually wants. For Poland the honest answer is software, and a share in building the machines. The layer around robotics — control systems, fleet coordination, simulation, safety, the integration work that decides whether a machine is deployable or a demo — is software work at industrial scale. Poles are good at software; the Germans built SAP. In robotics the software is the product; the metal is a commodity.

That is worth stating plainly, because Central Europe has spent twenty years as the place where other people’s systems get maintained. This is a chance to own a layer — and to export it, rather than only run it at home.

The path runs through the war

Here is the uncomfortable part, and skipping it would make everything above wishful. Europe is in a conflict with Russia, and that conflict is already doing what peacetime industrial policy never managed: pushing serious money into autonomy, sensing, drones, and the manufacturing capacity to build them at volume. Defence budgets are funding robotics research right now, at a scale no civilian programme was ever going to get approved.

The strategy

Stimulate the market now through defence, and convert the sector to civilian purposes after the war — the same autonomy, sensing and manufacturing capacity, pointed at demographic collapse instead of at an enemy.

That capacity converts. Autonomous navigation, machine perception, teleoperation, ruggedised actuators, and above all the factories and supply chains that produce them are the capabilities the civilian problem needs. A drone company and an agricultural robotics company are separated by their customers, not by their engineering. No committee needs to schedule the conversion: when the wartime customer goes away, the capacity looks for a new one, and demographic collapse will be the largest market in Europe. Industrial policy’s job is to know this in advance and make sure the civilian demand is waiting — procurement, standards and pilot deployments ready on the other side. What this is not: an argument for prolonging anything. It is an argument for making sure the industrial base built under one pressure survives to answer the next one, instead of collapsing with the contracts.

What would settle this

Whether defence-funded autonomy actually converts is checkable: a count of European suppliers that entered military autonomy after 2022 and now carry a civilian product line, against those that did not. Nobody has published one. If the number is small, the conversion needs to be planned harder than this chapter assumes; if it is zero, the strategy is wrong.

Where the machines go first

In the order they are ready, not in the order they are exciting.

Municipal and communal services. Cleaning, maintenance, waste, groundskeeping, basic logistics — heavily staffed, highly mechanical, already short of workers, and operating in spaces a city controls. The easiest place to deploy and the fastest place to feel the benefit. I would not bet on humanoids here. I would bet on simple machines: the ones that take the rubbish out, the ones that cut the grass, the ones that drive.

Food production and delivery. Autonomous farming and autonomous transport are the most advanced civilian robotics we have. Food security stops being theoretical when the people who grow and move it are retiring.

In the frame

The same three targets, in the same order, as the frame’s table: Designing Our Retirement.

Being cared for by a machine and being alone are not the same thing.

Predictive medicine and healthcare. The cost that grows fastest as a population ages. Robotics here means less a machine at the bedside than an entire system that catches what would have become a hospitalisation.

And the part that is not machinery at all: systems that let older people support each other, live together, work if they want to, and stay in contact. Being cared for by a machine and being alone are not the same thing. We are designing the world we retire into, and it should be one worth arriving in.

The objection I raised myself

The robots run on chips and rare metals Europe does not have.

True. The machines depend on microprocessors made from rare metals sourced across the whole world, on AI centres, and on enough political stability and purchasing power to keep buying them. For the robots to exist, a whole mass of things has to be organised — this is one of them, and the site does not yet have a chapter on it.

Probably the weakest link, and not yet written.

What has to happen

  • Treat robotics as national and European strategy, the way energy and defence are treated — not as a research topic.
  • Plan the conversion before it is needed: civilian procurement, standards and pilot programmes waiting for the capacity built under defence contracts.
  • Cheap, open and hard to monopolise as the condition every deployment has to meet, decided as policy because the market will not decide it in our favour.
  • Deploy in municipal services first, where the public sector is both customer and beneficiary, and where results are visible to citizens within a budget cycle.
  • Let Central Europe own the software layer, deliberately, as industrial strategy rather than subcontracting.

What this means for you

A demonstration is worth more than a strategy paper.

  • If you build control systems, perception, fleet software or mechanical design: this is the largest problem available in Europe, and it has a deadline, a customer and a funding source that already exists.
  • Start with a piece that runs. The Union will not commission this from the top; the pieces turn out to work, and the policy catches up to what is already running.
