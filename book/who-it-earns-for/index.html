Who It Earns For

How we get there · Argument

By Paweł Żentała · 25 September 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

Money cannot buy a service nobody supplies. The system we build must produce a surplus that reaches society, not only the owners of the machines.

You can print as much money as you like. It will not make a nurse appear.

That sentence is the floor under everything else on this site. The pension argument ends with a demand for work that will not be there; this chapter is about what has to be true of the thing we build so that the work is there, and so that the benefit of it reaches the people who paid for it.

Money cannot buy a service nobody supplies

We talk about the demographic crisis as if it were a funding problem. A funding problem has a monetary answer: raise contributions, cut benefits, shift the retirement age, borrow. Every one of those answers moves claims around. None of them creates the thing being claimed.

When there are too few working-age people, the shortage is physical. Somebody has to wash an eighty-year-old, drive the bus, harvest the field, answer the phone at the clinic at two in the morning.

The claim

If nobody does it, the service does not become expensive. It stops existing. You stand at the counter with a full account and there is nobody behind it.

So the task is not to fund the future. It is to design the market that will supply it, care, food, transport, public services, with far fewer hands than we have now. If we do not design it, no amount of money will conjure it. We can print until the presses wear out and it changes nothing. That is also why this is an engineering project with a political wrapper, rather than a political project with an engineering appendix.

It has to produce a surplus

A system that only feeds itself is not enough.

Whatever we build, automated municipal services, autonomous farming and logistics, machines in care, has to earn more than it costs to run. It has to keep the people who work on it, and then have something left over. That leftover is the whole point. It is what pays for the services the rest of us live on without working for them: children, the old, the sick, and everyone between jobs. Every productive economy has done this; we are going to ask it of a smaller number of people and a larger number of machines, which means the surplus has to be designed in rather than hoped for.

School meals, and why they are the example

Here is the worked case I keep coming back to.

An automated food chain, growing, processing, moving, that is genuinely cheap can feed every child in every school in the country, free, or so close to free that the price stops being a question anyone asks. Not as charity. As a normal output of capacity that exists anyway. That is how the technology gets popular.

Right now, when an ordinary person hears “robots”, they hear “my job”. They are not being stupid; that is the version they have been shown. The argument that automation is good for them is abstract, and abstract arguments lose. A hot meal their child eats every day, for free, produced by the thing everyone is arguing about, is not abstract. It is the difference between a technology people tolerate and a technology people defend. If the first visible thing automation does in a town is feed its children, the politics of the next twenty years look very different from the politics we get if the first visible thing it does is empty a warehouse.

So deploy it where people see it, and where the benefit is unmistakably theirs. Municipal services first, as the robotics chapter argues, and food on the table of somebody who did not have to pay for it.

It has to earn for society, not only for capital

Now the part that decides whether any of this was worth doing.

The machines will earn. The question is whose account the earnings land in. This technology has to pay for itself, and it has to pay not the owners of capital but us; it has to earn for society. If automation replaces the labour our pensions depend on and the returns flow to whoever owns the fleet, we will have solved production and destroyed the distribution that made solving it worthwhile. A continent full of robots and empty of purchasing power has not fixed its demographic problem; it has changed the shape of it.

Argued in full

Cheap before owned, open standards, hard to monopolise: the design goal is in Cheap Is Wealth; the rule that every proposal must answer it is at Who captures the gains.

Cheapness and open standards are the best defence we have, and they are not a guarantee. “It must earn for society” is easy to say and easy to mean nothing by. Three tests, proposed here, for what it would mean in practice:

  • Public buyers get the output, not just the invoice. When a municipality or a pension system funds capacity, what it gets back is the service, meals, cleaning, transport, care hours, not a financial return it then spends buying the same service back at whatever price the owner sets.
  • Pension contributions buy the thing they were meant to buy. Money set aside for our old age, invested in the machines that will care for us, is the one investment that delivers the service directly rather than a claim on somebody else’s.
  • The surplus is visible and named. A system whose surplus nobody can point to will be quietly captured. Whoever runs it should have to say, in public and in numbers, what it produced and where that went.

Direct democracy is the instrument

Something has to keep making the decision, year after year, in our favour. That something is us, and this is what direct democracy is also for: so that the benefits of the technology are ours.

A body of a few hundred people can be lobbied into handing a public asset to whoever asks nicely and pays well. It is cheap to do and it has been done, repeatedly, to things we built together. Decisions taken by everyone are not incorruptible, but they are far more expensive to corrupt. The choices this chapter is about, what the surplus is spent on, who may own the fleet that does public work, whether school meals are free, are simple to state, they affect everybody, and people have real opinions about them. That is the profile of a question that belongs to a citizens’ vote rather than to a committee.

Is this communism?

I want to deal with this myself rather than wait for someone to say it.

This was, in part, what communism was about. Only in communism the point was to give power to the party, so that the party would take the technology and divide it among the people. I think the power should go to the people, and to a system that balances itself. Then, perhaps, a better world can be built.

So what I am saying is probably mildly communist, in that sense. Though it may not be the right word; I would look for a better one, and I understand what is meant by it. I assume the state can build a better world through intervention. But I am also for the free market, in the way China does it, in the way they steer.

Words that fit better

Three terms were offered to me while this chapter was being edited, as words that describe parts of the position more precisely than the one above. I have not chosen one. They are set out here because they are useful, with their sources, not because any of them is yet mine.

Predistribution. Coined by the political scientist Jacob Hacker, it means shaping how income and assets are distributed before tax and transfers, rather than letting the market produce an outcome and correcting it afterwards with benefits: raising wages rather than topping them up, changing who owns a thing rather than taxing what it earns (Equitable Growth; Hacker’s argument). Most of what this site proposes is predistributive in that sense: it decides the outcome upstream instead of arguing about compensation downstream.

A citizens’ wealth fund. Norway put its petroleum revenue into the Government Pension Fund Global and draws on it to support the state budget (NBIM). Alaska did something different with the same idea: the Alaska Permanent Fund pays an annual cash dividend to every resident (Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation). The open question for us is whether automated productive capacity can be held the same way, and whether the model survives when the asset is a fleet of machines that depreciates rather than oil that sits in the ground. What I asked for is services, school meals first. A cash dividend is an option that has been offered alongside, not the demand.

The social market economy

Social market economy

Not defined yet — the chapter is the definition.

Read where it is argued → All terms

. Article 3 of the Treaty on European Union commits the Union to “a highly competitive social market economy, aiming at full employment and social progress”, language added by the Lisbon Treaty (Article 3 TEU). A market that produces, with an obligation that its gains reach society, is a fair description of what this chapter asks for, and it is written into the founding text of the thing being asked. That is worth knowing before anyone calls the idea foreign to Europe.

What would settle the open parts

Whether automated public provision produces a surplus at all, and how big: a real municipal deployment with published costs and outputs over a budget cycle. Whether public services change how people feel about automation, the load-bearing claim in the middle of this chapter: feed children in one region and measure opinion there against a region where the first visible robot took a job. Whether a fund model works when the asset depreciates: somebody with the right training can model it, and the answer changes how the instrument should be designed. The legal questions, public ownership of productive capacity, pension money in public infrastructure, procurement written to keep suppliers cheap and interoperable, join the list in The Political Path.

What this means for you

If you are building any of this, build it so the surplus is visible. That is a design instruction, not a sentiment.

  • Publish what it cost and what it produced. A surplus nobody can point to will be quietly captured.
  • Keep the interfaces open, so the next town can run it without asking permission.
  • Deploy where the benefit is obvious to people who did not fund it, and let them see it working before anyone asks them to vote on it.
