European English: a proposal for a shared European standard

How the EU could develop a clear, neutral and testable English standard without replacing national languages or prescribing one accent.

European English: a proposal for a shared European standard

TLDR

English is already the most useful common second language in continental Europe. The practical opportunity is not to invent a new dialect, but to build a European English Standard (EES): a shared layer for administration, education, science, business and cross-border public life.

EES would prefer clear and widely understood forms, document terminology decisions, test them with speakers from different EU language groups, and accept variation in accents and everyday speech. It would add capacity to Europe’s multilingual system rather than replace national languages.

The first deliverable should be a tested 60-item vocabulary and usage pilot. Only after measuring frequency, comprehension and the cost of misunderstanding should the project scale to 200, then 2,000+ decisions.

The proposal

The original idea was to make English an additional official language across the EU and create a European English council able to settle questions such as chips or fries. This article turns that idea into a buildable proposal.

The council should not decide which accent is correct. It should maintain a public interoperability standard for situations in which Europeans with different first languages need to understand one another:

  • public forms, notices and digital government interfaces;
  • cross-border contracts, procurement and company operations;
  • European research, education and media;
  • travel, emergency information and consumer products;
  • political communication between European institutions and citizens.

The national language remains the primary language of each country. EES is an additional shared capability and a common drafting target where an EU-wide audience matters.

Why this is plausible

The 2024 Eurobarometer reported that 47% of Europeans speak English as a foreign language, around seven in ten young Europeans can converse in English, and 98.3% of lower-secondary students learn it. English is therefore the strongest candidate for a shared operational language, even though proficiency and access remain uneven. European Commission — language Eurobarometer

English is still one of the EU’s 24 official and working languages, including after the United Kingdom left the Union. The EU already has language rules, professional translation services and IATE, its shared terminology database. EES would build on that infrastructure. EU language policy, IATE

The European Commission’s English Style Guide offers a useful precedent: it combines a consistent institutional convention with a strong preference for clear, reader-friendly writing. It explicitly distinguishes EU-specific terminology from the idea that the EU speaks a separate natural language. European Commission English Style Guide

Research on English as a Lingua Franca describes European accents, grammatical transfer and mainland-European vocabulary as normal features of multilingual communication. It suggests that successful interaction and accommodation matter more than imitating a native speaker. Modiano, 2024

Architectural decisions

These are the decisions I would make before building the system.

DecisionProposalWhy
1. What is being built?A standard and resource system, not a new dialect.A dialect cannot be legislated into existence; a shared drafting standard can.
2. What is the default?Plain, contemporary international English.It reduces translation, teaching and comprehension costs.
3. Who owns it?An independent European English Board connected to EU language services and IATE.The system needs legitimacy, continuity and terminology expertise.
4. What does the Board regulate?Recommendations, terminology, style, tests and versioned decisions.It should guide usage without policing identity or accent.
5. What does it not regulate?Native-language varieties, accents, informal speech or literary style.Variation is an asset and cannot be removed by decree.
6. How are decisions made?Evidence first: corpus frequency, comprehension testing and risk assessment.The most frequent form is not always the safest form.
7. How are conflicts resolved?Prefer a transparent compound when a short word is ambiguous: potato crisps, public holiday.Explicit meaning beats symbolic victory in a BrE/AmE dispute.
8. How is law handled?Separate EES Plain, EES Legal, EES Technical and EES Spoken profiles.One register cannot serve a menu, a statute and a scientific paper.
9. How is change handled?Every decision has evidence, confidence, owner, date and review date.Language changes; the standard must be reversible.
10. How is fairness handled?Publish uncertainty and country-level results; do not treat native-speaker intuition as the sole authority.The users of EES are predominantly multilingual Europeans.

How I would test it

Phase 0 — define the target

Write a one-page specification: audience, domains, risk levels and what “easy” means. “Popular in Europe” is not precise enough. For each item, distinguish:

  • frequency in EU institutional writing;
  • frequency in everyday communication;
  • comprehension without context;
  • ability to paraphrase or explain the term;
  • consequences of misunderstanding.

Phase 1 — the 60-item pilot

Use the initial 60 decisions as hypotheses, not conclusions. Recruit at least five participants from each of twelve EU countries, balanced across major language families and age groups. Include competent non-native speakers, not only English teachers or linguists.

Give participants four tasks:

  1. choose the meaning of a word or phrase shown without context;
  2. select the clearest option for a public notice;
  3. paraphrase the chosen expression in their own language or in English;
  4. repeat the comprehension task after 24 hours.

Record frequency, comprehensibility, risk, confidence and country. A decision is a strong EES candidate when it is sufficiently common, broadly understood and low-risk. If no short word wins, recommend an explicit compound.

Phase 2 — corpus validation

Compare the pilot answers with four types of data: EUR-Lex and DGT Translation Memory for legal language; IATE for EU terminology; ELF/VOICE-style spoken interaction for multilingual conversations; and consumer-facing text from at least ten continental EU countries. Report results by country and genre. An EU institutional corpus is not a proxy for ordinary speech.

Phase 3 — live prototypes

Test the draft standard in three real products: a bilingual public-service form, a travel or emergency notice, and a cross-border business workflow. Measure task completion, clarification requests, time to comprehension and serious misunderstandings. A good standard should make people complete tasks faster, not merely make editors feel that the prose looks tidy.

The first decision set

The pilot recommends fries for fried potato strips and potato crisps for the packaged snack. It prefers forms such as elevator, apartment, mobile phone, postcode, pharmacy, football, petrol, motorway, autumn, film and CV, while allowing regional variants when context is safe.

It avoids false friends and opaque calques: actual should not mean “current”; eventually should not mean “possibly”; make a control should become carry out a check; advices and informations should remain advice and information. It prefers will over unnecessary shall, active voice where possible, explicit dates such as 27 August 2026, and short sentences.

Governance and safeguards

The European English Board should publish a dictionary, style guide, terminology graph, evidence register, consultation process and changelog. It should include EU translators, lawyer-linguists, teachers, accessibility specialists, domain experts and users from different language communities.

It should not impose one national accent, treat native-speaker intuition as the only authority, confuse EU institutional frequency with everyday frequency, or use language integration to remove national languages. The political promise is enhancing, not replacing linguistic diversity.

First concrete actions

  1. Approve the EES charter: scope, principles and safeguards.
  2. Convert the 60-item table into a small open dataset with evidence fields.
  3. Recruit the pilot panel and run the comprehension tests.
  4. Compare results with EU corpora and IATE.
  5. Publish EES-0.1 with confidence scores and unresolved items.
  6. Test it in one public form, one travel notice and one business workflow.
  7. Decide whether the evidence supports scaling to 200 items.
