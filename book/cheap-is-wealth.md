Wealth is when things are cheap, not when they are expensive. When something is
expensive, nobody can afford it and the people who supply it take the gain.
When it is cheap, you can buy it, and everybody ends up better off: the people
at the bottom live well, and the people above them live better still.

That sentence sounds obvious and almost nothing in our industrial policy acts on
it. We subsidise producers, protect margins, and count success in the size of a
sector rather than in the price its customers pay. I want to turn that around.
**Making things cheap is the goal, not a side effect of chasing some other
goal.** European transport has to be cheap and automated. If the machines
arrive and prices stay where they are, we built them for somebody else.

[Who It Earns For](/book/who-it-earns-for) argues that the system we build must
produce a surplus that reaches society. This chapter is the other half: the
surplus reaches people mostly through the price, and the price is decided by
whether we deploy at scale or keep the thing rare.

## Rare means they win, cheap means we win

Take any new capability and follow it two ways.

Wealth in a society is mostly that accumulated difference — the long list of
things that used to be a luxury and are now unremarkable. When it goes the other
way, that is not a moral failure by anybody; it is what scarcity does. So
"democratising the benefits of technology" is not a slogan about ownership
papers. In the first instance it is a decision about volume and price. Build
for scale, keep the standards open, refuse to let the thing be enclosed, and the
distribution happens through the till.

</SideNote>

## Technological unemployment: build what we never offered

A lot of people are going to lose work to AI. I am not going to pretend
otherwise.

But I am a positivist about the technological sciences, and I mean something
specific by it. **Do not put the effort into how work is being taken away. Put
it into producing value we never offered before.** That is where the employment
is. So the policy question is not "how do we protect this occupation". It is
"what could people have, that they cannot have today at any sane price, and
what would it take to deliver it at scale". Search for that. Fund the search.
That is a better use of a ministry than another retraining scheme aimed at the
jobs that are leaving.

## A worked example I am building myself

I built an application that generates made-to-measure furniture through a chat.
You describe what you want, it designs it, and you order it online.
Made-to-measure furniture has always been rare and expensive: hard to find
someone good, hard to explain what you wanted, and the design work cost more
than the wood. Most people gave up and bought a box that nearly fitted.

I expect the startup to create **more** work for the people who assemble
furniture, not less. Once the design step is nearly free, made-to-measure stops
being a luxury and becomes an ordinary thing to order, and there will be far
more orders than there ever were. The value could not be delivered at scale
before. That is the pattern: a cheap thing that did not exist, paid for by
volume, with hands needed at the end of it.

I should say the other half, because it is true. **This is also the uberisation
of work.** The fitter becomes a contractor taking jobs off a platform, and the
platform holds the customer relationship, the pricing and the ratings. Both
things are happening at once: more work, on worse terms, unless we decide the
terms.

## Share the gains from capital, and start now

A technological revolution is when the division of the gains gets decided. It
gets decided early, quietly, by whoever is in the room, and then it holds for a
generation. We are in that room now, which is why this belongs in a political
programme rather than in a paper about the future of work.

What I want is not one new ownership law. It is **experiments in how people
organise, funded properly**: cooperatives, mutuals, employee-owned firms,
private capital, and mixed forms nobody has tried yet. Give them support and
grants, let most of them fail, and look at what the survivors do to prices and
to the people who work in them.

I think it is unfair that we subsidise private capital so heavily and put
almost nothing into cooperative forms. That is my claim, not a finding: the
research behind this chapter found no clean euro-for-euro comparison of public
support to cooperatives against public support to private firms in the EU, and
none is invented here. What is documented is narrower and points the same way.
The Commission's own [study on state aid and access to finance for social
enterprises](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/study-state-aid-access-finance-social-enterprises-and-recruitment-disadvantaged-workers-form-wage_en)
finds that the rules — age limits on eligible firms, the requirement to match
public money with private investment — create hurdles for social-economy
enterprises, that they lean on small *de minimis* schemes which cap their
growth, and that national authorities often avoid state-aid routes because the
rules are complex. A rule that pays out in proportion to the private investment
you can attract is a rule written for firms with shareholders; a cooperative
does not have them, by construction.

<EvidenceBox>
  Total public support — grants, tax expenditure, guarantees, subsidised credit
  — per euro of turnover and per employee, split by legal form, across a few
  member states. If the numbers show no tilt, the claim above goes. Until
  somebody runs them, it is the author's argument and not a finding.
</EvidenceBox>

The obvious question — is this communism? — gets one answer on this site, in
[Who It Earns For](/book/who-it-earns-for#is-this-communism), and it is not
repeated here.

## Write the proposal

[The Shorter Week](/book/the-shorter-week) is the other answer to the same
pressure: take the productivity as time rather than as unemployment. This
chapter is the first answer — take it as cheapness, and keep hunting for the
value nobody has been able to deliver yet. They are not alternatives. Fewer
hours only feel like freedom if the things you buy with the rest of your life
are cheap.

I think a better vision is enough to take power honestly and democratically.
That is what an election is supposed to be: whoever has the better proposal for
the voters. Nobody has one for the decade we are walking into, which is an
opening, not a complaint. We have AI. We have time, because the work is ending.
So let us write the proposal.
