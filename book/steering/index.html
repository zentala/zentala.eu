Steering

How we get there · Argument

By Paweł Żentała · 25 September 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

A state that decides slowly falls over. Our decision loop is too slow; the fix is faster correction: frequent citizens' votes and a state-economy channel.

Watch anyone ride a bicycle. They are never upright. They lean left, correct, lean right, correct, and the correcting never stops; that is what holds them up. Stand the same bicycle still and it falls over in two seconds.

Balance is not walking in a straight line. It is leaning back, then forward, and still moving ahead. You do not stop. You go forward, lean one way, lean the other, and catch your balance. Keeping balance means being in motion and catching the middle again and again, not standing in it, because the middle cannot be held. A state that refuses to lean is not stable. It is stalled, and a stalled bicycle is a falling bicycle.

We do not fall because we are wrong. We fall because we are slow.

Here is the pattern, and once you see it you will see it everywhere.

A direction gets set. It is held for years, past the point where everyone involved can see it is not working. Then it is reversed at enormous cost, after the money is spent, after the factories have retooled, after the competitors have taken the market. And often enough, the thing the public said at the start turns out to be roughly what happens in the end.

Notice what is broken there. It is not that a mistake was made. Every system that does anything makes mistakes. The failure is in how long it took to correct one. The cost is not in the wrong turn; the cost is in the distance travelled before the wheel moved.

The cars

The clearest example we have is the car industry, and the dates are the argument.

WhenWhat happened
March 2023The Union adopts Regulation (EU) 2023/851: new cars and vans sold from 2035 must emit no CO2, in effect the end of the combustion engine in new cars. Germany holds up the final vote for about a month and extracts a carve-out for e-fuels, agreed in the last week before adoption.
2023–2025The industry spends against the announced direction.
December 2025The Commission proposes replacing the 100 % cut with 90 %, so plug-in hybrids, range extenders and combustion cars can go on being sold after 2035, the last tenth offset through EU-made low-carbon steel, e-fuels and biofuels (Euronews, 16 December 2025). Bulgaria, Czechia, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Poland and Slovakia had pressed for it, and the carmakers harder.

A law that has to be renegotiated in the last week before it passes is a law that was never settled. One qualification, because accuracy matters more than rhetoric: the December 2025 text is a proposal, not yet law. It still needs the member state governments and the European Parliament, and the final version may differ. If you read this later than it was written, check where it landed.

Everything else about the episode is on the record, and it is bad enough. We set a direction, told an entire industry to spend against it, held it for nearly three years, and then softened it so that European manufacturers would not go under. That is not a policy. That is a swerve. It is an embarrassment in front of the whole world, an embarrassment for the Union’s administration, that it could not carry the thing through, and the embarrassment is not that they changed their mind. Changing your mind is the correct response to new information. It is that it took this long and cost this much, and that in the end it came out the way the citizens wanted from the start.

What this chapter cannot show

”The citizens were against it from the start” is my reading, not a measured fact. No poll is quoted here and none will be invented. What can be said without inventing anything: the reversal happened, it happened under pressure from industry and national governments, and nobody asked the public directly whether this was the transition they wanted, at that speed. What would settle it is a polling series on the 2035 rule from 2022 to 2025, by member state, which somebody may already hold.

And the cost was paid twice. Once by the companies that invested in the direction we announced, and again by the ones that hesitated because they suspected we would not hold it. Both bets turned out to be reasonable. That is the real damage of a slow system: it stops being rational to trust it, and after that nobody plans on anything it says.

A controller must correct at least as fast as the thing it steers

There is a name for this problem, and it is older than the European Union. It was offered to me while this chapter was being edited; I did not arrive at it myself, and it is set out here as the nearest prior art, not as my claim.

Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety says that a controller must have at least as much variety in its responses as the system it is trying to control throws at it; the usual short form is that only variety can absorb variety. Stafford Beer spent his career applying this to organisations and to states, and built the Viable System Model around it.

The part that matters here is simple enough to say in ordinary words. A steering system that corrects once every four years can only steer a world that changes once every four years. Ours does not. Technology, energy prices, supply chains and wars move faster than that, and a four-year loop against a six-month world is not governance; it is hoping.

Direct democracy is the faster loop

This is why direct democracy is not a matter of taste for me. Those movements, the lean and the correction, are what it is. We fall over because we walk too slowly: too slow to decide, too slow to set a direction, and then we set one and back out of it, and that costs us a great deal. The instrument is the correction mechanism.

Frequent votes on specific questions do two things at once. They make corrections more often, and because they happen more often, they make each correction smaller. A wheel you touch every few seconds needs a nudge. A wheel you touch once every four years needs a yank, and a yank at speed is how you come off. The Swiss hold federal popular votes three to four times a year. That is not, as people assume, a country in permanent crisis from constant voting. It is a country whose steering never has to move very far, because it never goes very long without moving.

Speed is the point. Not participation for its own sake, not fairness in the abstract: error correction, fast enough to matter.

The other loop: who talks to whom

Voting is the loop between citizens and the state. There is a second loop that is just as broken, and it runs between the state and the people who build things.

Entrepreneurs feel they cannot get through to the government with what they need for growth: which rule is strangling a sector, which permit takes nine months, what an investment decision is waiting on. And from the other side, there is no agreement between entrepreneurs and everybody else about how they should be taxed and how the burden should be divided. Each group suspects the other of freeloading, and neither has a room to say so in.

What I want instead runs in both directions. Business sends someone to talk to the state: not the state planting a spy in a firm, but the firm sending a person to speak with the state. And the state sends someone to talk to business. So that there is communication, so that information flows. The state has to move further into a communicating role: a negotiator, or perhaps not even a negotiator, a system of checks and balances.

What to learn from China, stated carefully

I will say the uncomfortable part plainly, and then bound it.

I think we can learn a lot from China about running a state, and the thing I want to learn is not the party. Not one-party rule, not the absence of a free press, not what happens to people who become inconvenient. None of that is on offer here and none of it is what I am pointing at. I want more power in the hands of citizens, not less.

China has its group of representatives and it works well for them; we should have a group of representatives too, only ours should be democratic. Theirs are, in a sense, also democratic: the party is somewhat more like a mafia, and people get cleaned out, but apart from that the representatives are relatively democratic. The part worth taking is not how those people are chosen. It is the question of where representatives come from, from which social classes, and what they represent.

Our answer is almost entirely geography. You represent a district. That is a real thing to represent, but it is one axis, and a modern economy has several. Nobody in our parliaments sits there as the person the machine-tool sector sent, or the care workers, or the small logistics firms, with a mandate from them and a duty to report back. Those people exist in the building, but they arrive as lobbyists, paid by whoever pays them, visible to nobody. The shape of the question is one we have stopped asking, and we should ask it again in a democratic form: elected, transparent, revocable, answerable to the people who sent them. I do not have a designed mechanism to hand you.

Except we already built it, and left it toothless

Here the research behind this chapter turned something up that I had not named: Europe already has institutions of exactly this shape. They sit in our capitals, and almost nobody can name them. What follows is what was found, offered with its sources.

The European Economic and Social Committee (EESC) is a Union body of representatives of employers, workers and civil society that gives opinions on EU legislation; it has existed since the Treaty of Rome, and its opinions are advisory, with no veto and no formal power to stop anything. In March 2026 the European Court of Auditors reported that the Committee delivered 84 % of its opinions on time in 2019–2024 and that neither it nor the Committee of the Regions systematically measures whether an opinion changed the final law (Special report 12/2026). If a body cannot tell you whether it changed anything, that is usually because it did not.

In Poland, the Rada Dialogu Społecznego, the Council for Social Dialogue, is the national forum where unions, employers and the government are supposed to meet. The idea is right. The practice is the complaint from every side of the table: draft laws never reach it, or reach it too late to matter; bills are routed around it; government seats sit empty. Union and employer representatives alike have described it as stuck, operating but producing no results and no shared vision.

So the conclusion is not “Europe needs a new institution for this”. It is worse and more useful than that: the institution exists, and it has no teeth. We built the channel and then made sure nothing has to come out of the other end. What these bodies lack is consequence. Four things that would give them some, none of which needs a treaty change, proposed here and not yet adopted as a demand:

What would give the channel consequence

  1. 1

    A duty to answer.

    If a draft law goes to these bodies and they object, the legislator publishes a reasoned reply before proceeding. Not obedience: a written answer, on the record, that a journalist can quote back later.

  2. 2

    A duty to ask.

    A law touching a sector does not move without that sector's representatives having had a real chance to see it. Bypassing the council becomes a defect in the process, not a tactic.

  3. 3

    Publication by default.

    Every meeting, every position, every name. Most of what lobbying buys is privacy.

  4. 4

    Visible people.

    If a sector sends a representative, the public knows who they are and can read what they asked for. A lobbyist nobody can name is worth more to his employer than a representative everybody can.

Whether that is best done by reforming the EESC and the national councils, or by wiring the same duties into the ordinary legislative procedure, is open. A body with decades of institutional memory and no power is a better starting point than a new body with neither.

Why the two loops need each other

The votes decide direction: what kind of country this is going to be, who the gains go to, what we will not accept. The dialogue supplies information: what a decision will actually do once it hits a factory, a ward, a haulage firm. A state with fast votes and no information corrects quickly towards the wrong place. A state with good information and no votes knows exactly what to do and does what the best-connected interest wants instead, which is a fair description of the car file. You need the speed and you need the sensors.

If you take one thing from this chapter, take the question. Of any proposed reform, ask not only whether it points the right way but: when this turns out to be partly wrong, how long will it take us to find out, and how expensive will it be to correct? That question kills a lot of grand plans and rescues a lot of modest ones. It is the reason this site argues for instruments rather than for programmes, and the reason The Total State insists that the correction mechanism ships alongside the digital machinery rather than after it. A state that can see everything and cannot be corrected is a fast bicycle with no handlebars.
