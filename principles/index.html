Principles

Every proposal on this site is tested against the same few rules. This page states them, in the order the frame sets — a working language, then a digital state, then robotics — and points at the chapter where each one is argued.

Nothing here is the argument itself. It is the ruler the arguments are measured with.

The frame

Why now

The demographic case for acting now — the shared condition, the window of roughly thirty years we still have to build the systems that will carry us, and the shift from relying on more workers to relying on automation — is laid out in full in Designing Our Retirement. What follows is not that argument again; it is the rules we apply once we accept it.

How we decide

The four principles

Each one is a sentence from the frame, and each has a chapter that carries it.

  1. Everyone wins, or it does not pass. A proposal built so that everyone wins can earn the unanimity that treaty change requires; treaty change is then a question of political will, which follows conviction. The way to find such proposals is to start from what almost all of us already agree on and work that out properly, instead of hunting for the line that divides. The Political Path argues the first half; the manifesto names the second as a method.

  2. Pay once, together. Every member state funds its own digitalisation separately. Doing it together is not a new budget; it is a less wasteful use of the one we have. Who pays for it, in the frame chapter.

  3. One working language makes one public. European political consciousness cannot exist without a shared language: the awareness people build working together in business becomes, over time, the awareness they bring to European politics. A shared interest is what keeps us from turning on each other; the Union was built so there would be no more war, and a common venture that stays worth more than any quarrel is how that works in this century. One Working Language is the floor of the whole plan.

  4. Automate old-age provision so the few young people left are not crushed carrying it. Robotics is the top of the stack, not the starting point; it needs the layers beneath it, and the targets come in the order they are ready — municipal services, food production and delivery, predictive medicine. Robotic Reindustrialisation argues where the machines go first.

Runs through every proposal

Who captures the gains

Private capital, or everyone. Every proposal on this site has to answer it.

If automation replaces the labour our pensions depend on and the returns go to a handful of owners, we have solved production and destroyed the distribution that made it worth solving. Settling ownership first is the wrong order; the models will be mixed. What matters more is that the technology stays cheap enough that it cannot be enclosed:

  • cheap to buy and run;
  • available to every municipality, farm and clinic;
  • built on open standards;
  • hard to monopolise.

Why cheapness, not ownership, is the goal is argued in Cheap Is Wealth. Who the surplus has to reach is argued in Who It Earns For.

Where this stands

Pro-European, not attached to the present shape

The current administration is rejected by a large part of the public, and it is not the point. What is worth defending is the project underneath: free nations with enough shared interest that war stays unthinkable. If the institutions have to change form to do that, they change form — the shape proposed here is a Union of Nations, rebuilt from the inside.

This is a manifesto and a programme, not a party platform, and it does not need to be politically correct. It needs to be worth reading. The demand it makes of you is on the manifesto; the full plan is on the vision page.
