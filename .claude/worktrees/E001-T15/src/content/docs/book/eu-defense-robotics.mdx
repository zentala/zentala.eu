# EU Defense Robotics: Autonomous Systems as Strategic Independence

I grew up in Poland, roughly 200 kilometers from Kaliningrad — a Russian military exclave that
hosts Iskander missiles capable of reaching Warsaw in under three minutes. I did not grow up
afraid of this. I grew up understanding it as a fixed variable in the environment, the way
you understand that a legacy system has a known vulnerability that nobody has patched yet.

The last four years have changed the calculus. The US administration has made explicit what
was previously politely left unsaid: Europe must take "primary responsibility" for its own
conventional defense. Defense Secretary Hegseth said it directly in February 2025. The Trump
administration questioned the automaticity of Article 5 repeatedly. No formal withdrawal
from NATO — but a clear signal that the operating assumption of the last 75 years is being
renegotiated.

I am not catastrophizing. I am reading the architecture diagram and noting where the single
points of failure are.

## The Economics of Autonomous War

Ukraine settled a question that military theorists had been debating for a decade: can cheap,
autonomous systems compete with expensive conventional platforms? The answer is not in dispute.

An FPV drone costs $400–$700. A main battle tank — Leopard 2A6, M1A2 Abrams — costs $4–7
million. When a drone successfully disables a tank, the cost-exchange ratio is approximately
6,000:1 in the attacker's favor. Ukraine is producing these drones at 4 million units per
year as of 2025 — a production rate that was considered impossible for a country at war until
it happened.

This is not a niche battlefield effect. It is a fundamental repricing of military capability.
The relevant industrial base is no longer Rheinmetall and KMW building tanks. It is the
ecosystem of drone frame manufacturers, battery suppliers, fiber-optic guidance engineers, and
software teams writing AI-assisted target recognition. Ukraine built that ecosystem from
commercial suppliers, not from defense factories, because that is the only way to reach
4 million units annually.

The KARGU-2 autonomous loitering munition — documented by a UN Panel of Experts report as
potentially having engaged combatants without a human operator in Libya in 2020 — tells the
second part of the story. Autonomous lethal engagement is not a future scenario requiring UN
consensus. It is a present reality that has already happened, in a contested legal void, while
the CCW Group of Governmental Experts has been holding annual sessions in Geneva since 2014
without producing a binding treaty.

This is where Europe needs to be honest about where it stands.

## The 7-Cent Problem

The EU spends approximately €10.5 billion per year on defense R&D across all 27 member states.
The US spends $145 billion. That is 7 cents of defense R&D for every US dollar.

ReArm Europe — the €800 billion framework announced in March 2025 — is primarily about
procurement, not R&D. It buys more artillery ammunition, more air defense interceptors, more
conventional platforms. These are necessary. They are not sufficient. Procurement without
R&D means Europe buys yesterday's systems while autonomous warfare evolves without European
input.

I am not arguing that Europe needs to match the US dollar for dollar. I am arguing that 7
cents is not a strategy — it is a dependency. And dependencies on partners who are actively
reconsidering the partnership are what engineers call a single point of failure.

## The Argument I Am Actually Making

Here is the argument I want to make clearly, because I think it is underappreciated in the
current European defense debate.

The same industrial capacity that builds care robots for an aging European population can build
defense drones. This is not a coincidence. It is the central insight.

Autonomous mobile robots navigating hospital corridors, optimizing routes, avoiding obstacles,
operating in GPS-uncertain indoor environments — that is the same sensing, planning, and
actuation stack as autonomous logistics for military supply. Agricultural drone operators in
Poland's eastern provinces, using precision farming systems to monitor crop health over
hundreds of hectares — they are operating the same sensor fusion and autonomous navigation
systems that unmanned ISR platforms use. The EU's precision manufacturing base — the same
base that builds automotive robotics systems, medical device robotics, industrial automation
cells — is the base from which drone hardware can be produced at scale.

Ukraine proved this in the other direction: it turned DJI agricultural drones into weapons.
Europe can make this work constructively. Invest in civilian robotics. Fund the automation of
eldercare logistics, precision agriculture, industrial maintenance. Build the engineering
ecosystem, the software talent, the hardware supply chains, the sensor manufacturing capacity.
That ecosystem, invested once for civilian purposes, doubles as the defense production base
when it is needed.

This is not a roundabout argument for defense spending. It is a roundabout argument for a
unified robotics policy — one that stops treating civilian automation and defense autonomy as
separate ministries with separate budgets and separate supply chains, when they share the
same underlying technology stack.

## What This Means for ReArm Europe

The ReArm Europe framework authorizes up to €800 billion. It explicitly mentions autonomous
systems and drone capability alongside conventional procurement. But the risk is that
"autonomous systems" gets interpreted as buying existing platforms rather than building
European capacity.

Buying US MALE drones to fill the ISR gap is a patch. It increases European dependency on a
US that is reconsidering the partnership. Building MILREM THeMIS-equivalent programs across
EU member states — deploying Estonian ground robotics expertise at EU scale — is a refactor.
Building Poland's FPV drone production capacity (WB Electronics currently produces WARMATE
at hundreds of units per year — Ukraine produces equivalent systems at hundreds of thousands
per month) is closing a gap that the war has made visible.

I propose that ReArm Europe explicitly earmark a meaningful percentage — I would argue 15–20%
of the EU-funded SAFE instrument — for autonomous systems R&D and industrial capacity,
not procurement of existing foreign platforms. Not because conventional procurement is wrong.
Because Europe building its own autonomous defense capability is not the same as Europe
buying its autonomous defense capability from abroad. One creates strategic independence.
The other extends the dependency.

## The View from 200 Kilometers

When I think about what "Europe builds its own autonomous defense capability" means for
someone who grew up near Kaliningrad, I think about it as a systems question.

The current architecture: a 75-year-old mutual defense commitment, backed by US nuclear
guarantees and C4ISR infrastructure, increasingly contingent on US domestic politics. A
dependency that is being renegotiated in real time.

The proposed architecture: European autonomous systems capacity, built on a dual-use civilian
robotics base, that does not require a phone call to Washington before it can operate.

Poland is already showing what political will looks like: 4.12% of GDP in defense spending
in 2024, the highest in NATO, higher than the US itself. Estonia built MILREM and put a
ground combat robot in Ukrainian service. These are not small countries with small ambitions.
They are countries that understand, from geography, what strategic autonomy actually means.

The question is whether the EU as a whole — Germany, France, Italy, Spain, whose economies
are 2–10 times larger — will make the same calculation before the dependency becomes
irreversible.

I think they should. I think the argument for doing it is not primarily a defense argument.
It is a robotics argument. It is a demographics argument. It is the argument that investing in
automation for an aging society and investing in autonomous capability for a contested security
environment are the same investment, pointed at two problems at once.

Build autonomous logistics for the economy. It doubles as autonomous supply in conflict. Invest
once. Benefit everywhere.

That is the refactoring Europe needs. Not a new arms race. A unified architecture for
autonomous systems, serving demographic and defense goals simultaneously — designed by
engineers who have read the specifications of both problems and noticed they share most of
the same requirements.

For someone who grew up 200 kilometers from Kaliningrad, that architecture feels
overdue.
