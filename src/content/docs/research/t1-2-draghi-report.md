# Draghi Report: EU Competitiveness Crisis and Policy Recommendations

## Summary

Released on September 9, 2024, Mario Draghi's 400-page report "The Future of European Competitiveness" delivers the most comprehensive diagnosis of Europe's economic decline in decades. Commissioned by European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, the report argues that without radical reform — including €750–800 billion in additional annual investment (roughly 4.4% of EU GDP) — Europe faces what Draghi calls "slow agony": a gradual, irreversible slide in living standards, geopolitical relevance, and technological capacity. The central problem is a productivity gap that has widened since the mid-1990s: EU labour productivity, which had converged from 22% of US levels in 1945 to 95% by 1995, has since fallen back below 80%, and the gap grew by 12% in 2023 alone. Around 70% of the EU's GDP per capita gap with the US is explained by lower productivity. If current trends continue, EU GDP will merely stagnate by 2050 — at precisely the moment when Europe needs to fund decarbonization, digitalization, and defense simultaneously. For the zentala.eu manifest, the Draghi Report is the most authoritative external validation of the case for structural EU reform, though it frames the problem primarily in economic rather than civilizational terms, and is largely silent on language as a competitiveness factor.

---

## Key Findings

- EU labour productivity was 95% of US levels in 1995; it has since fallen back below 80%, with the gap growing 12% in 2023, 72% of which is explained by the tech sector gap.
- Around 70% of the EU-US GDP per capita gap is explained by lower productivity growth since 2000.
- Real disposable income per capita grew "almost twice as much in the US as in the EU since 2000."
- No EU company exceeding €100 billion in market value has been created from scratch in the last 50 years; the US produced six companies above €1 trillion in the same period.
- EU companies spent €270 billion less than US counterparts on R&I in 2021; EU R&I is roughly half the US share of GDP.
- 70% of foundational AI models since 2017 have been developed in the US; three US "hyperscalers" control over 65% of the global cloud market, including Europe's.
- By 2040, the EU workforce will shrink by close to 2 million workers per year; without productivity gains, current growth rates are only sufficient to keep GDP constant until 2050.
- Closing the gap requires investment to increase from approximately 22% of EU GDP to 27% — a 5-percentage-point jump not seen since the 1960s.
- EU energy costs are 2–3 times higher for companies than in the US; natural gas prices are 4–5 times higher.
- Only 43 of 383 Draghi Report recommendations (11.2%) were fully implemented as of September 2025.

## 1. Draghi's Main Thesis

Draghi's main thesis, stated without euphemism: **Europe is entering the first period in its modern history where growth will not be supported by a rising population, and unless it radically improves productivity — particularly in technology — it will be forced to abandon its ambitions for technological leadership, climate action, and geopolitical independence, and accept irreversible decline.**

The underlying argument runs as follows. For 50 years, Europe's growth model relied on three external props: cheap Russian energy, open access to Chinese markets, and the US security umbrella. All three have ended or are ending. Simultaneously, Europe missed the digital revolution: since the mid-1990s, EU productivity gains from information technology have been far smaller than in the US, creating a structural divergence Draghi calls the "middle technology trap" — Europe is stuck between low-cost emerging market producers and high-technology US/Chinese innovators, unable to compete at either end.

The report is not a general anti-EU critique but a technical argument that the EU's existing institutional model — highly fragmented, rule-by-rule, nation-by-nation — is structurally incapable of producing the coordination and investment scale needed to compete. The call is for more Europe, not less, but a different kind of Europe: faster, more centralized in strategic priorities, and willing to borrow collectively.

## 2. Why EU GDP Shrinks in Absolute Terms by 2050

The Draghi Report does not explicitly project EU GDP decline in absolute terms by 2050 as a point estimate. What it demonstrates is a convergence of three trends that collectively make GDP stagnation — and thus relative decline — virtually guaranteed under the status quo:

**Demographic contraction.** By 2040, the EU workforce will shrink by close to 2 million workers each year. The EU is "entering the first period in its recent history in which growth will not be supported by rising populations." With the old-age dependency ratio rising from 37% today to approximately 57% by 2050 (see T1.1 Demographic Crisis research), labour input will subtract from rather than add to GDP growth.

**The productivity flatline.** If the EU maintains its average productivity growth rate since 2015, this will only be enough to keep GDP constant until 2050. GDP stagnation is the *optimistic* scenario — it assumes no deterioration in current productivity trends. But it also assumes no additional investment demands. In reality, decarbonization, digitalization, and defense require investment increases of approximately 5 percentage points of GDP. Without growth above the stagnation baseline, these cannot be funded without cutting something else: welfare systems, public services, or investment itself.

**The compound divergence.** EU growth of real income per capita has been approximately 0.1% lower annually than in the US since 2000. That sounds trivial but compounds to roughly 2.5% over 25 years. Sustained over another 25 years, EU living standards will fall progressively further behind the US, China, and other fast-growing economies. The absolute decline is not GDP turning negative in 2050; it is the purchasing power, technological capacity, and geopolitical weight of Europe becoming a fraction of what it could be — the "slow agony" of a continent that chose inertia.

**The innovation desert.** Only 4 of the world's top 50 technology companies are European. No EU company above €100 billion has been created from scratch in 50 years. Europe's most successful startups leave: nearly 30% of European unicorns founded between 2008 and 2021 relocated their headquarters abroad, the vast majority to the US. The EU's internal barriers function as "45% tariff on goods and 110% on services" (IMF estimate). A continent that cannot retain or scale its own innovations cannot generate the productivity growth needed to offset demographic shrinkage.

## 3. Top 5 Policy Recommendations (Ranked by Impact)

The Draghi Report contains 383 specific recommendations across all sectors. The five highest-impact structural reforms, based on economic scale and systemic effect, are:

### 1. Massive Joint Investment via Common Debt (€750–800 Billion/Year)

The single most impactful recommendation. Draghi argues the EU must mobilize €750–800 billion in additional investment annually — about 4.4% of EU GDP — to fund decarbonization (approximately €450 billion), digitalization (approximately €150 billion), breakthrough innovation (€100–150 billion), and defense (approximately €50 billion). This exceeds the annual scale of the Marshall Plan (which provided 1–2% of recipient GDP per year from 1948–51). The primary mechanism is common EU debt issuance, building on the NGEU model. Without EU-level borrowing, this scale cannot be achieved because national fiscal constraints (particularly in France, Italy, and Spain) prevent the required investment surge.

*Political obstacle*: Germany and "frugal" member states (Netherlands, Austria, Nordics) remain opposed to institutionalizing common debt. This is the central political barrier to the entire Draghi agenda.

### 2. Close the Digital and Innovation Gap

Draghi identifies the technology sector as the root cause of the EU-US productivity divergence. Concrete recommendations include: create a European Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) for high-risk breakthrough research; reform Horizon Europe's successor (10th Framework Programme) by doubling the budget and concentrating it on fewer, larger bets; establish a "28th regime" allowing startups to incorporate under a single EU-wide legal and tax framework rather than 27 national ones; reform competition policy to allow European tech champions to scale; expand EU-level AI investment to €20 billion annually by 2030; and reserve capacity in the EU's High Performance Computing network for AI applications.

The "28th regime" and startup framework reforms are the most actionable near-term levers because they require regulatory change rather than new money.

### 3. Reform Capital Markets (Capital Markets / Savings and Investment Union)

EU household savings rates are higher than in the US, but those savings fund US innovation rather than European growth because EU capital markets are deeply fragmented. Draghi proposes: upgrade ESMA to a single EU securities regulator (equivalent to the US SEC); complete Banking Union; create long-term savings products that channel household savings into productive EU investments; reform prudential rules on securitization to expand bank credit; and develop a Pan-EU equity listing environment. The EU currently relies on bank-dominated finance, which is inherently risk-averse; closing the innovation gap requires US-style venture and growth capital.

### 4. Energy Cost Reduction and Decarbonization Without Deindustrialization

EU energy costs — 2–3 times higher for electricity and 4–5 times higher for gas than US levels — are a direct competitiveness tax on European industry. Draghi recommends: expand EU-level gas collective purchasing platforms; accelerate electricity grid interconnections and cross-border power market integration; pursue technology-neutral decarbonization (renewables, nuclear, hydrogen, carbon capture) rather than excluding low-carbon technologies; and create industrial decarbonization accelerator programs for energy-intensive sectors (steel, chemicals, cement). The report frames decarbonization and competitiveness as compatible goals — but only if pursued at EU scale with coordinated policy, not through 27 separate national energy transitions.

### 5. Governance Reform and Regulatory Simplification

The EU's legislative process averages 19 months from Commission proposal to adoption; over 13,000 pieces of EU legislation have been passed (vs. approximately 3,000 US congressional resolutions). Over 60% of EU companies cite regulation as an investment obstacle; 55% of SMEs name regulatory burden as their greatest challenge. Draghi recommends: expand qualified-majority voting in economic and industrial policy (reducing individual member-state veto power); create a "Competitiveness Coordination Tool" to align national policies with EU strategic priorities; and conduct an EU-wide regulatory simplification exercise targeting a 25% reduction in administrative burden. Faster governance is prerequisite to all other reforms — if the EU cannot decide and act quickly, the remaining recommendations cannot be implemented in the time available.

## 4. Alignment with zentala.eu Three Pillars

The Draghi Report was not designed with zentala.eu's framework in mind, but its economic analysis maps closely onto two of the three pillars and partially onto the third.

| zentala.eu Pillar | Draghi Report Alignment | Key Draghi Recommendations |
|---|---|---|
| **Pillar 1: Language (English as EU lingua franca)** | Indirect / Not explicitly addressed | Skills gap closure, Erasmus+ 5x expansion, cross-border qualifications recognition, 'Union of Skills' for mobility — but English-as-working-language not named |
| **Pillar 2: Digitalization (Estonia e-government model)** | Strong direct alignment | '28th regime' pan-EU startup framework, digital single market completion, EU Cloud/AI infrastructure, data sharing between industries, cross-border digital services, GDPR reform signals |
| **Pillar 3: Robotization (care robots, logistics automation)** | Moderate alignment (implicit) | AI adoption across 10 strategic sectors including healthcare, advanced manufacturing; 'Apply AI' strategy; labor shortage addressed through productivity technology rather than immigration alone |

### Pillar 1: Language

Draghi does not mention English as a working language or propose any EU-wide language standardization. However, the report's structural diagnosis makes the language argument by implication: startup founders relocate to the US partly because the English-language market is uniform and accessible; the EU's 24 official languages fragment markets, complicate cross-border scaling, and raise transaction costs. Draghi's 'Union of Skills' proposal — focused on cross-border mobility, qualification recognition, and lifelong learning — is a precondition for any language-neutral EU labor market, but the report does not name language barriers explicitly. The Erasmus+ 5x budget increase would substantially increase English-language exposure among young Europeans, supporting the manifest's goal even if unintentionally.

### Pillar 2: Digitalization

This is the strongest alignment. Draghi's entire diagnosis rests on Europe's failure to capture digital-age productivity gains. His recommendations for a '28th regime' (single EU startup registration), digital single market completion, AI gigafactories, cloud infrastructure investment, data sharing frameworks, and cross-border digital services harmonization are a structural blueprint for the kind of digitalized, borderless EU the zentala.eu manifest envisions. The report cites e-government and digital public services as areas where single-market fragmentation imposes the highest costs. Estonia's model is not named explicitly, but the '28th regime' concept is essentially the Estonia approach applied EU-wide: a jurisdiction-neutral digital layer on top of member-state governance.

### Pillar 3: Robotization

Draghi addresses this indirectly. The report's 'Apply AI' strategy explicitly covers healthcare and advanced manufacturing — the sectors where care and logistics robots are most relevant. The workforce shrinkage projection (2 million workers lost per year by 2040) makes automation not optional but arithmetically necessary: the EU cannot maintain current levels of elder care, manufacturing output, or logistics without substituting technology for human labour. Draghi frames this as an AI productivity challenge rather than a robotics one, but the economic logic is identical. The report proposes €20 billion annual EU AI investment by 2030 and identifies healthcare as one of ten strategic AI priority sectors.

## 5. Where Draghi Diverges from zentala.eu Vision

### 5.1 Language is Invisible

The most notable divergence: the Draghi Report is entirely silent on language as a competitiveness factor. This is a significant gap. The zentala.eu argument is that language fragmentation is a *structural* barrier to EU market unification — not just a cultural nicety. A startup founder in Warsaw cannot easily access the German market without German; a skilled worker in Romania cannot move to Sweden without Swedish. Draghi acknowledges that 30% of unicorns leave Europe but does not analyze language as a contributing factor. The manifest fills this gap by making the language-market connection explicit.

### 5.2 Technocratic, Not Civilizational

Draghi's report is fundamentally a technocratic document. Its logic is economic: Europe is falling behind on productivity metrics; here is the investment required to close the gap. The zentala.eu manifest argues something different: that Europe needs a new self-concept, a shared identity built around shared tools (language, digital infrastructure, automation), not just optimized economic mechanisms. Draghi does not engage with the cultural or political dimensions of European integration; the manifest does. Both are arguing for more Europe, but from different philosophical premises.

### 5.3 National Sovereignty as Context, Not Obstacle

Draghi acknowledges member-state resistance to common debt and centralized industrial policy but treats it as a political obstacle to work around. The zentala.eu vision implies a more direct confrontation: the nation-state architecture itself is the bug, not a feature to be accommodated. The manifest's "refactoring Europe" metaphor suggests replacing legacy code (national fragmentation) with a new operating system (pan-European infrastructure). Draghi proposes patching the existing system; the manifest argues for an OS-level rewrite.

### 5.4 Defense and Geopolitics vs. Domestic Reform

Draghi dedicates significant attention to defense industrial policy, reducing dependency on the US security umbrella, and building European strategic autonomy. The zentala.eu manifest focuses on internal transformation — language, e-government, automation — rather than external security. The two are not contradictory, but they represent different priorities: Draghi is writing for EU policymakers facing immediate geopolitical pressures; the manifest addresses the underlying structural weaknesses that make Europe vulnerable in the first place.

## 6. Cost-Benefit Analysis

The Draghi Report is primarily diagnostic rather than cost-benefit analytic, but several quantified estimates are embedded in the analysis:

**Investment requirement:** €750–800 billion annually (4.4% of EU GDP), sustained from approximately 2025–2030 and beyond. This is the cost of the entire agenda. It breaks down roughly as:
- Decarbonization: approximately €450 billion/year
- Digitalization: approximately €150 billion/year
- Breakthrough innovation (R&I): approximately €100–150 billion/year
- Defense: approximately €50 billion/year

**Productivity gain potential:** If successful reforms closed the EU-US productivity gap, the return would be enormous. The productivity gap "grew by 12% in 2023, 72% explained by the tech sector." Even partial convergence — capturing half the gap — would translate to trillions of euros in cumulative GDP gains over a decade. The CER analysis suggests that public investment subsidies reducing private funding costs by 2.5% could unlock 4% of GDP in additional private investment — a 1.6x leverage ratio on public money.

**GDP stagnation cost:** If no reform occurs, Draghi projects GDP essentially flat by 2050 against a backdrop of rising investment needs. The implicit cost is 25 years of foregone growth: at 2% annual growth (roughly what the US achieves), the EU economy by 2050 would be approximately 60% larger than today; at stagnation, it is no larger. The wealth gap — already 2.5% larger than 2000 in favour of the US — would compound by another 2.5% or more.

**Workforce replacement arithmetic:** With 2 million fewer workers per year by 2040, automation and productivity improvements must compensate. A 1% annual productivity improvement on the existing workforce base offsets roughly 1–1.5 million worker-equivalents in output terms. The €800 billion annual investment agenda is therefore, in part, a workforce replacement investment.

**Regulatory simplification return:** Administrative burden reduction is cited as a quick-win category. Simplification measures adopted through 2025 have generated approximately €8.4 billion in compliance savings. Draghi estimates a 25% reduction in administrative burden could release significant further capital into productive activity, though an aggregate EU-wide figure is not provided.

**Counterpoint — cost of debt:** Common debt issuance at the scale Draghi proposes would add materially to EU-level financial obligations. Northern member states' objection is partly fiscal: they worry about moral hazard and contingent liabilities. No EU-level debt sustainability analysis is included in the report.

## 7. Intended Audience

The Draghi Report is explicitly addressed to **EU institutional leadership** — primarily the incoming European Commission (2024–2029), the European Council (heads of government), and the European Parliament. Draghi was commissioned by Commission President von der Leyen; the report was timed to coincide with the new Commission's mandate beginning in late 2024.

Secondary audiences:
- **EU finance ministers and central banks** — the Capital Markets Union proposals and joint debt recommendations require Council decisions.
- **Member-state governments** — particularly Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and Poland, whose consent is required for treaty changes and major budget shifts.
- **European industrial sector** — the report explicitly addresses energy-intensive industries, automotive, aerospace, pharmaceuticals, and technology companies as stakeholders in the reform agenda.
- **International investors** — the capital markets and investment environment sections are designed to signal EU-level commitment to creating a more attractive investment destination.

The report's language and structure make it inaccessible to a general European public. It assumes familiarity with EU institutions, fiscal policy, and industrial economics. There is no "popular" version. This is a document for insiders arguing to other insiders. The zentala.eu manifest, by contrast, targets a broader educated audience and makes an argument that should be intuitive to anyone who has navigated EU bureaucracy or tried to start a business across member-state borders.

## 8. EU Policy Response Since Publication

### Immediate Reception (September–December 2024)

The report was received with broad rhetorical endorsement and limited immediate legislative action. Von der Leyen incorporated many Draghi themes into her Political Guidelines for the 2024–2029 Commission and issued mission letters to commissioners referencing the report. All EU member states and the European Parliament endorsed the report's analysis by September 2025. Draghi himself expressed frustration, urging decision-makers to "do what I told you."

### Competitiveness Compass (January 2025)

The Commission's primary operational response was the **Competitiveness Compass**, published January 29, 2025. The Commission claims 90% of its flagship initiatives are "directly inspired" by Draghi's most pressing recommendations. The Compass adopts Draghi's three-pillar structure (innovation/digital, clean economy, security/strategic autonomy) and adds "horizontal enablers" (regulatory simplification, capital markets, skills). Key adopted elements include:
- €200 billion mobilized for AI investment, including €20 billion for AI gigafactories
- €150 billion in defense funding (SAFE initiative)
- €100+ billion for Clean Industrial Deal
- €70 billion for innovative companies via TechEU
- €8.4 billion in regulatory simplification savings
- Start-up and scale-up strategy, "28th regime" consultation
- Savings and Investment Union strategy

### What Was Not Adopted

- **Common debt (Eurobonds)**: Rejected by Germany and northern member states. The Compass relies on member-state financing and redirected existing funds rather than new EU-level borrowing.
- **WTO-bending subsidies**: Draghi suggested local-content subsidy programs that would violate WTO rules; the Commission stopped short of endorsing these.
- **Budget reallocation away from agriculture/cohesion**: Draghi implicitly required redirecting EU budget from legacy spending toward strategic priorities; farm lobby and regional fund recipients blocked this.
- **Full GDPR reform**: Draghi signaled AI rules need reassessment; no substantive GDPR changes were adopted.

### Implementation Gap (September 2025 Assessment)

The European Policy Innovation Council (EPIC) launched the **Draghi Observatory & Implementation Index** in September 2025, one year after publication. First audit findings:
- 43 of 383 recommendations (11.2%) fully implemented
- 77 (20.1%) partially implemented
- 176 (46.0%) in progress
- 87 (22.7%) not yet addressed
- Best progress: critical raw materials (>33% implemented)
- Worst progress: energy (0% of proposed reforms enacted)

The European Parliament passed a resolution on September 8, 2025 (407 for, 161 against, 97 abstaining) endorsing the Draghi agenda — a non-binding but politically significant signal.

The gap between rhetorical endorsement and actual implementation reflects the structural problem Draghi himself identifies: the EU's decision-making architecture is too slow and too fragmented to execute the reform agenda at the required speed.

## 9. Implications for EU Reform Argument

The Draghi Report is the strongest institutional validation of the zentala.eu manifest's core premise: Europe's current architecture is not fit for purpose and requires structural transformation, not incremental adjustment.

**Where it strengthens the manifest:**
- Provides authoritative economic backing for the urgency argument — stagnation by 2050 under the status quo is not alarmism but economic modeling.
- Confirms digitalization as the root cause of EU-US divergence, not a secondary policy priority.
- Demonstrates the scale of investment required and the impossibility of funding it through national budgets alone — validating the case for deeper integration.
- The workforce shrinkage projections (2 million/year by 2040) create the arithmetic case for automation/robotization that the manifest makes explicitly.

**Where the manifest goes further:**
- Language as infrastructure: Draghi misses the most obvious single-market barrier. The zentala.eu argument that English as a working language would directly reduce market fragmentation, improve labor mobility, and reduce transaction costs is not addressed anywhere in the 400-page report. This is the manifest's original contribution.
- Civilizational framing: Draghi argues for European competitiveness; the manifest argues for European identity. The economic case alone is necessary but not sufficient — Europe also needs a story about who it is and where it is going, not just an investment plan.
- The manifest's "refactoring" metaphor captures what Draghi proves with data: the EU is a legacy system running on 1950s architecture in a 2020s competitive environment. Draghi describes the bugs; the manifest proposes the refactor.
