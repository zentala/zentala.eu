# Defense Robotics and EU Strategic Autonomy

## Summary

The war in Ukraine has done what decades of military theory could not: it has made autonomous
systems and drone warfare a lived, documented reality rather than a projection. First-person-view
(FPV) drones costing $400–$700 are disabling tanks worth $4–7 million. Ukraine is producing
4 million drones per year as of 2025. Loitering munitions — "kamikaze drones" — are making
fixed artillery positions untenable. Ground unmanned vehicles are beginning to serve logistics
and reconnaissance roles. NATO is taking detailed notes. The EU, simultaneously, is in the
middle of a rearmament surge it has not seen since the Cold War: the ReArm Europe plan
announced in March 2025 authorizes up to €800 billion in additional defense spending over four
years, with explicit goals around industrial base development, autonomous systems, and
interoperability. But the EU's dependence on US capabilities — from C4ISR (command, control,
communications, computers, intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance) to satellite guidance
to nuclear deterrence — remains structural. European defense industrial output is fragmented
across 27 national programs. PESCO's autonomous systems projects are early stage. Lethal
autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) remain in a UN-level political deadlock. The question for
EU reform is not whether autonomous systems will define future warfare — they already do —
but whether Europe will build them, buy them from the US, or import them from China.

---

## Key Findings

- **FPV drone cost vs. main battle tank cost**: $400–700 per drone vs. $4–7 million per tank
  (10:1 to 17,500:1 asymmetry). A $40,000 drone equipped with a fiber-optic guidance cable
  resists electronic jamming and has successfully disabled multiple armored platforms.
- **Ukraine drone output 2025**: approximately 4 million FPV drones per year domestic production;
  Ukraine's stated 2024 target was 1 million drones — that target was exceeded by 4x in 2025.
- **ReArm Europe**: March 2025 EU plan — up to €800 billion in combined EU and member state
  spending through 2029; removes defense spending from EU fiscal deficit calculations.
- **MILREM Robotics (Estonia)**: the most advanced EU ground UGV producer; THeMIS UGV deployed
  with Ukrainian and French forces; ADDER loitering munition in development.
- **EU defense R&D gap**: EU total defense R&D spending ~€10.5 billion/year (2023); US defense
  R&D budget ~$145 billion/year (FY2024). The EU spends roughly 7 cents of defense R&D for
  every dollar the US spends.
- **LAWS status**: no binding international treaty exists as of April 2026. The Convention on
  Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) Group of Governmental Experts has met since 2014 without
  reaching a legally binding instrument. Political commitments only.
- **Baltic + Poland defense spending**: Poland reached 4.12% of GDP in defense spending in 2024
  — highest in NATO and the first time any NATO member exceeded 4%. Estonia: 3.43%. Latvia: 3.15%.
- **US NATO commitment signals**: The US political environment 2024–2026 has generated significant
  uncertainty. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth stated in February 2025 that Europe must take
  "primary responsibility" for its own security. No formal withdrawal from NATO, but operational
  burden-shift signaling is explicit.

## Ukraine: Live Laboratory for Autonomous Systems

### Drone Warfare at Industrial Scale

Ukraine is the first conventional conflict where drone warfare has reached true industrial scale
on both sides. As of early 2025:

- **FPV drones**: First-person-view attack drones, operator-guided via video link, have become
  the dominant anti-armor weapon. Ukrainian forces deploy hundreds daily along a 1,000-km front.
  Unit cost: $400–700 assembled from commercial components (Geprc, iFlight frames; DJI
  components until Chinese export restrictions; battery packs from consumer market).
- **Fiber-optic guided FPVs**: A significant evolution emerging 2024–2025. Optical cable replaces
  radio link, making the drone immune to radio-frequency jamming. These systems have demonstrated
  ability to defeat electronic warfare countermeasures fielded by both sides. Unit cost premium:
  roughly 2–4x standard FPV.
- **Loitering munitions**: Ukraine uses Switchblade 300 (US, $6,000 per unit) and Switchblade
  600 (anti-armor, ~$50,000) from US deliveries. Russia uses Lancet-3 (estimated $35,000) and
  Iranian Shahed-136 ($20,000–$50,000 each) extensively. Loitering munitions have forced artillery
  units to relocate after every fire mission to avoid detection and destruction.
- **Long-range strike drones**: Ukraine's domestically produced Shahed-derivative (UJ-22, Bober,
  Beaver variants) and its own designs have struck oil refineries inside Russia at ranges of
  1,200–1,500 km, demonstrating indigenous capacity for strategic strike.
- **Maritime drones (USVs)**: Ukraine has effectively developed a new weapon category —
  uncrewed surface vessels. Sea Baby and Magura V5 USVs have destroyed or damaged multiple
  Russian Black Sea Fleet warships including the cruiser Moskva (though by missile) and the
  landing ship Novocherkassk. USV unit cost: estimated $250,000–$500,000 vs. warship replacement
  cost of $300 million+.

### Ground Unmanned Vehicles (UGVs)

Ground robotics in Ukraine remain far less mature than aerial:

- **MILREM THeMIS UGV** (Estonia): Used in Ukrainian theater for logistics and ISR roles.
  Wheeled platform, 750 kg payload, armed variant possible. Not yet used for direct combat.
- **Ukrainian-developed UGVs**: Several Ukrainian companies (Roboneers, others) are developing
  remote-operated ground platforms for logistics, mine clearance, and armed reconnaissance.
  These are prototype/early serial production as of 2025.
- **Assessment**: Ground autonomy has proven harder than aerial autonomy due to terrain
  complexity, obstacle negotiation, and GPS-denied operation requirements. Ukraine's front-line
  adoption of UGVs for direct combat is limited. Logistics use is growing.

### Lessons NATO is Drawing

NATO published an unclassified summary of its "Ukraine Lessons Learned" analysis in mid-2024:

1. **Drone attrition is persistent**: Both sides lose thousands of drones monthly. Industrial
   capacity to sustain drone production at war-scale requires civilian supply chain integration.
2. **Electronic warfare is decisive**: The electromagnetic spectrum is contested at every layer.
   Systems relying on GPS, radio control, or commercial data links are vulnerable. Optical
   fiber guidance and AI-assisted navigation are responses.
3. **ISR-to-strike loop compression**: The time from target identification to strike authorization
   has compressed from hours (Gulf War model) to minutes. Human-in-the-loop authorization is
   becoming the bottleneck, not the technology.
4. **Logistics robotics is underinvested**: Forward supply is a critical vulnerability. Crewed
   vehicles delivering ammunition face drone threats. Robotic logistics is a high-priority gap.
5. **Software is a weapon**: Ukraine's Delta battlefield management system integrates drone feeds,
   artillery targeting, and logistics tracking. Software update speed matters as much as hardware.

Source: [NATO Allied Command Transformation — Innovation Hub, 2024](https://act.nato.int);
[RUSI Special Report: Lessons from the Ukraine War, 2024](https://rusi.org); multiple open-source
analysis from Oryx, ISW, and Warmonger.

## Asymmetric Economics: Drone Cost vs. Tank Cost

The cost asymmetry is the defining economic feature of contemporary warfare:

| System | Unit Cost | Manufacturer |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| FPV Attack Drone | $400–$700 | Ukraine, China (commercial components) |
| Fiber-optic FPV Drone | $1,500–$2,500 | Ukraine domestics |
| Lancet-3 Loitering Munition | ~$35,000 | Russia (ZALA Aero) |
| Switchblade 300 | ~$6,000 | AeroVironment (US) |
| Switchblade 600 | ~$50,000 | AeroVironment (US) |
| Shahed-136 | $20,000–$50,000 | Iran (Shahed Aviation) |
| T-72B3 Main Battle Tank | ~$2–3 million | Russia (UralVagonZavod) |
| Leopard 2A6 | ~$4–5 million | Germany (Rheinmetall/KMW) |
| M1A2 Abrams | ~$6–7 million | USA (General Dynamics) |

A single FPV drone that successfully disables a tank delivers a cost-exchange ratio of
approximately 6,000:1 to 17,500:1 in the attacker's favor. Even accounting for miss rates
and drone attrition, this ratio remains decisively favorable.

**Implications for EU defense industrial strategy:**
- Building tank and armored vehicle production capacity remains important but insufficient.
- Drone production capacity is the new mobilization metric.
- The relevant industrial base is not Airbus Defence — it is small drone manufacturers,
  electronics integrators, and battery suppliers.

Ukraine's 4 million drone/year output (2025) required building a national industrial ecosystem
from commercial suppliers, not establishing new defense factories. Poland, Germany, and France
are studying this model as a template.

Source: [Oryx — Equipment Losses Ukraine War, continuously updated](https://www.oryxspioenkop.com);
[CSIS Defense Costs Database 2024](https://csis.org); [Ukraine Defense Ministry statements,
Q1 2025](https://mil.gov.ua).

### EU Defense R&D Spending vs. US

- **EU combined defense R&D** (2023): approximately €10.5 billion across all member states.
  Of this, collaborative EU-funded R&D (EDF, EDIDP combined) was €2.1 billion.
- **US defense R&D** (FY2024): $145.4 billion (DARPA: $4.3B, service R&D: $90B+).
- **Gap ratio**: EU spends approximately 7.2% of what the US spends on defense R&D.
- **China defense R&D** (estimated): $30–40 billion/year (Stockholm International Peace
  Research Institute, 2024). China may surpass EU in defense R&D within 1–2 years.

Sources: [SIPRI Military Expenditure Database 2024](https://sipri.org/databases/milex);
[EDA — Defence Data 2023](https://eda.europa.eu); [US DoD FY2024 Budget](https://comptroller.defense.gov).

## ReArm Europe: What €800B Actually Funds

In March 2025, the European Commission announced the **ReArm Europe / SAFE** framework:

**Structure:**
- **€150 billion SAFE instrument** (Security Action for Europe): EU-backed loans to member
  states for defense procurement. Funds can only be spent on European-made defense goods.
- **€650 billion member state spending**: Achieved by removing defense spending from EU
  deficit calculations under the Stability and Growth Pact — freeing countries to borrow more
  without Excessive Deficit Procedure penalties.
- **Total headline figure**: €800 billion, though most is member state sovereign spending,
  not a central EU budget allocation.

**What it specifically targets:**
- Artillery ammunition production ramp-up (EU was producing 300,000 shells/year pre-war;
  target is 2 million shells/year by 2025 — still not achieved as of early 2026)
- Air defense systems: Patriot, IRIS-T SLM, SAMP/T production increases
- Drone and counter-drone capability (explicitly mentioned in Commission communication)
- Defense industrial base consolidation and cross-border procurement
- Critical raw materials for defense supply chains (lithium, rare earths)

**What it does NOT fund:**
- A European nuclear deterrent (excluded from discussion)
- New EU military force structure (NATO command authority unchanged)
- US replacement for NATO's C4ISR infrastructure

Source: [European Commission — ReArm Europe / SAFE Communication, March 2025](https://ec.europa.eu);
[European Defence Agency press releases, 2025](https://eda.europa.eu).

## Autonomous Weapons Systems: TRL and Status

### Current Technology Readiness (TRL 7+)

| System | Country | Type | TRL | Notes |
|--------|---------|------|-----|-------|
| **KARGU-2** | Turkey (STM) | Loitering munition, swarm-capable | TRL 8–9 | First documented autonomous lethal use in Libya (2020, UN Panel report) |
| **Harop** | Israel (IAI) | Loitering munition, anti-radiation | TRL 9 | Operational since 2000s; used in Nagorno-Karabakh 2020 |
| **Harpy** | Israel (IAI) | Anti-radiation loitering munition | TRL 9 | Operational; passive radar homing |
| **Predator/Reaper MQ-9** | USA (GA-ASI) | MALE strike drone | TRL 9 | Human-in-loop for lethal strike; highly autonomous for flight/ISR |
| **MQ-1C Gray Eagle** | USA (GA-ASI) | MALE armed drone | TRL 9 | Widely deployed US Army |
| **Lancet-3** | Russia (ZALA Aero) | Loitering munition | TRL 8–9 | Optical homing with AI target selection claimed |
| **THeMIS** | Estonia (MILREM) | Ground UGV (logistics, armed) | TRL 7–8 | Combat use limited; armed variant tested |
| **MUTT (EMAV)** | USA (General Dynamics) | Ground UGV, logistics | TRL 7–8 | Used by US Army for logistics; autonomous follow-me mode |
| **Ghost Shark** | Australia (Anduril+) | Autonomous submarine (UUV) | TRL 6–7 | Contract awarded 2024; operational target 2025–2026 |

### US Autonomous Systems: Deployed and Planned

- **MQ-9 Reaper**: The primary deployed US MALE (medium altitude long endurance) armed drone.
  Human-in-the-loop for strike authorization. Highly autonomous for flight, ISR, and return.
  Fleet size: approximately 300 aircraft across Air Force and allies.
- **MUTT (Modular Unmanned Transport Technology)**: Army UGV for logistics. "Follow-me" mode
  tested in exercises. Not yet combat-deployed as primary logistics platform.
- **Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA)**: DARPA/Air Force program. AI-controlled loyal wingman
  aircraft to accompany crewed fighters. XQ-58A Valkyrie demonstrator has flown. Autonomy
  level: AI mission execution with human authority override.
- **REMUS 300/600/6000**: Naval autonomous underwater vehicles, used for mine countermeasures
  and ISR. TRL 9 for non-lethal roles.
- **MQ-25 Stingray**: Autonomous carrier-based refueling drone. Boeing contract. Not yet
  operational as of early 2026.

### Russia Autonomous Systems

- **KUB-BLA / Lancet**: Russia's most-used lethal loitering munition in Ukraine. Lancet-3 uses
  optical homing and reportedly AI-assisted target selection. Unknown level of human oversight
  for target engagement.
- **S-70 Okhotnik**: Heavy stealth UCAV (unmanned combat air vehicle). First flight 2019.
  Pair-operation with Su-57 crewed fighters. Claimed AI-pilot capability. Development status
  uncertain under sanctions pressure.
- **Marker UGV**: Ground combat robot developed by Kalashnikov and Android Techniques. Tested
  in exercises; limited deployed use in Ukraine. Russia's ground autonomous systems program
  is significantly behind Ukraine's improvised capability.
- **Assessment**: Russia's autonomous systems development has been disrupted by sanctions
  (component shortages, microelectronics access). Iran-supplied Shahed-136 filled the gap for
  long-range strike. Russia's indigenous program is slower than pre-2022 trajectories.

### LAWS: UN Debates and Status

The Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) Group of Governmental Experts on LAWS:

- **Meeting since**: 2014 (annual sessions in Geneva)
- **Status as of 2026**: No binding treaty. No agreed definition of "meaningful human control."
  Multiple competing proposals: comprehensive ban (Austria, New Zealand), preemptive regulation
  (US), case-by-case national review (Russia, China, India).
- **Key blocking dynamic**: China and Russia oppose binding regulation but support "political
  declarations." The US supports human control requirements but opposes a blanket ban. Small
  states pushing for a ban lack enforcement leverage.
- **Practical reality**: KARGU-2 is cited in a 2021 UN Panel of Experts report as potentially
  having operated in autonomous lethal mode against combatants in Libya. If accurate, LAWS have
  already been used in combat. The legal and ethical framework has not caught up.

Source: [UN CCW GGE on LAWS — Reports 2024](https://www.unog.ch); [SIPRI — Autonomous Weapons 2024](https://sipri.org).

## US Withdrawal Risk: EU Dependency Analysis

### What Has Changed Since 2024

The US political environment shifted substantially after January 2025:

- **Defense Secretary Hegseth (Feb 2025)**: Stated Europe must take "primary responsibility" for
  its own conventional defense. Suggested US focus would shift to Asia-Pacific and the southern
  border. No formal withdrawal from NATO, but a clear operational signal.
- **EUCOM funding uncertainty**: US European Command (EUCOM) budget for pre-positioned equipment
  and troop levels in Europe has come under congressional scrutiny. Specific cuts proposed but
  not enacted as of early 2026.
- **Article 5 ambiguity**: Multiple statements from the Trump administration questioned the
  automaticity of Article 5 NATO mutual defense commitments, though no formal withdrawal
  notification was filed.

### EU Dependency on US Capabilities

Current structural dependencies:

| Capability Area | US Dependency Level | EU Alternatives |
|-----------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Nuclear deterrence | Near-total (US B61 bombs in Europe; UK/France independent) | France (independent), UK (NATO-aligned) |
| C4ISR infrastructure | Very high (NSA SIGINT, satellite imagery, AWACS fleet) | AIRBUS Pléiades (commercial), EU SatCom (limited) |
| Air defense interceptors | High (Patriot PAC-3, THAAD forward-deployed) | IRIS-T SLM (Germany), SAMP/T (France/Italy) — insufficient scale |
| Long-range precision strike | Very high (JASSM, Tomahawk; EU SCALP/Taurus limited) | SCALP (France), Taurus (Germany — not yet cleared for Ukraine) |
| Secure satellite communications | High (US military SATCOM for NATO ops) | IRIS2 constellation planned (2027+) |
| Autonomous ISR drone platforms | High (US MALE platforms dominate NATO ISR) | Limited EU alternatives at scale |

**Quantified dependency**: The European Parliamentary Research Service (2024) estimates that
approximately 70–80% of NATO's "enabling capabilities" — AWACS, ISR satellites, logistics
coordination, precision strike — are US-provided. In a scenario where US forces fully withdrew
from NATO operations, Europe could sustain current conventional defense with difficulty but
would lose most strategic strike, satellite-based ISR, and missile defense capability within
6–12 months without replacement.

Source: [EPRS — European Defence 2024](https://europarl.europa.eu/thinktank); [IISS — Military Balance 2025](https://iiss.org).

## Path to EU Strategic Autonomy in Defense

### Realistic Timeline Assessment

**Near-term (2025–2027):**
- ReArm Europe / SAFE funds begin flowing; artillery production ramp-up continues
- MILREM THeMIS enters wider European military service (Estonia, France, UK tests)
- First EU collaborative drone procurement programs under EDF 2025–2027 period
- IRIS2 satellite constellation awarded to Airbus/Thales/Leonardo/etc. consortium (contracts
  signed 2024; operational target 2027–2029)

**Medium-term (2028–2032):**
- EU Collaborative Combat Aircraft feasibility studies conclude; development decision expected
- European MALE drone (EURODRONE / Euromale by Airbus Defence) — production deliveries planned
  2028–2030; program repeatedly delayed
- Poland and Germany reach ~4-5% GDP defense spending; more European combined capability
- Possible EU Rapid Deployment Capacity: up to 5,000 troops; first exercises underway

**Long-term (2033–2040):**
- EU strategic autonomy in conventional defense theoretically achievable if current spending
  trajectories hold and industrial base consolidation occurs
- Nuclear deterrence gap remains: France has independent deterrent; EU-level nuclear sharing
  outside NATO framework has no political consensus and would require treaty changes
- C4ISR gap narrows only if IRIS2, EU military SATCOM, and collaborative ISR programs
  fully materialize — currently uncertain

**Honest assessment**: Full EU strategic autonomy — able to operate at NATO Article 5
equivalent capability without US forces — is not achievable before 2035 at the earliest, and
only if ReArm Europe spending is sustained and collaborative programs do not fracture along
national interest lines (as they historically do in EU defense cooperation).

## Dual-Use: Civilian Robotics in Defense Context

### Can Civilian Robots Become Defense Assets?

**Logistics robots → military supply:**
- Yes, with modifications. Boston Dynamics Spot has been tested by multiple military forces
  (US Marines, UK, France) for forward reconnaissance and payload carrying. Not yet operationally
  deployed for combat logistics.
- Warehouse AMRs (Autonomous Mobile Robots) — used by Amazon Robotics — could in principle
  supply forward operating bases but lack hardening for contested environments.
- Practical barrier: civilian logistics robots assume GPS availability, non-adversarial
  electromagnetic environment, and paved surfaces. Military forward supply requires none of these.

**Agricultural drones → surveillance/attack:**
- Already happening in Ukraine. DJI Agras M30 crop-spraying drones, designed for agricultural
  use, have been modified to drop grenades and small munitions. Ukraine and Russia both use
  modified commercial drones extensively.
- DJI responded by implementing geofencing near conflict zones (2022–2023) and cutting
  off repair support for Ukraine. China subsequently tightened export controls on drone
  components (2024), affecting Ukrainian production chains.
- EU agricultural drone fleet (used under EU Farm to Fork precision farming support) includes
  similar platforms. The dual-use potential is not hypothetical — it is proven at industrial scale.

### EU's Position on Dual-Use Technology Development

The EU's **Dual-Use Regulation** (2021/821), which came into force in September 2021, governs:
- Export controls for dual-use items (technology with both civilian and military applications)
- End-user certificates required for controlled exports
- "Catch-all" clause: member states can restrict exports of items not on the control list if
  there is reason to believe they will be used for military purposes in embargoed destinations

The regulation does NOT restrict EU companies from developing dual-use technologies for the
EU market. The tension is at the export layer. A Polish FPV drone manufacturer can sell to EU
member states' militaries; export to non-EU destinations requires licensing.

The **European Defence Industrial Strategy (EDIS)** announced March 2024 explicitly acknowledges
the civilian-military technology transfer gap and calls for closer cooperation between the
European Defence Fund and Horizon Europe (civilian R&D). This is the first time the Commission
has formally endorsed dual-use R&D integration at EU level.

Source: [EU Dual-Use Regulation 2021/821](https://eur-lex.europa.eu); [EDIS Communication, March 2024](https://ec.europa.eu).

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

The defense dimension connects to the broader zentala.eu argument as follows:

1. **Sovereignty requires defense autonomy**: A digitally reformed, economically integrated EU
   that remains militarily dependent on a US administration willing to question Article 5 is not
   sovereign. Digital integration without security integration is incomplete.

2. **The Ukraine lesson is about industrial capacity, not just technology**: Ukraine did not win
   the drone war because it had better technology. It won because it built industrial capacity
   faster than Russia. The EU's comparative advantage — a large industrial base, advanced
   manufacturing, electronics supply chains — is directly applicable.

3. **Robotization of the military is the same trend as robotization of the economy**: The forces
   driving automation in manufacturing, logistics, and agriculture are the same forces making
   drone warfare dominant. EU policies for civilian robotics R&D (Horizon Europe, EDF, EDIS)
   are not separate from defense policy — they are the upstream of it.

4. **The 7% defense R&D ratio is unsustainable**: Europe cannot claim strategic autonomy while
   spending 7 cents of defense R&D for every US dollar. ReArm Europe addresses procurement;
   it must also address R&D if Europe intends to lead rather than follow in autonomous systems.

5. **Poland is showing what political will looks like**: At 4.12% GDP, Poland is demonstrating
   that EU countries can make the investment if the political will exists. The question is
   whether Germany, France, Italy, and Spain — whose economies are 2–10x larger — will follow
   at the same intensity.
