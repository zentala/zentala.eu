# Autonomous Vehicles in EU Logistics: Technology, Jobs, and Transition

## Summary

Autonomous vehicles are no longer a speculative technology — they are a commercial reality in the United States and an imminent industrial force in Europe. Aurora Innovation launched the world's first commercial driverless heavy-truck service on Texas highways in April 2025; Waymo's robotaxi fleet logged 450,000+ weekly rides by end-2025 across five U.S. cities; Gatik is running fully driverless medium-duty trucks at scale for Fortune 50 retailers. Europe lags by roughly three to five years on regulatory approval but is closing the gap fast: the European Commission's March 2025 Automotive Action Plan commits to cross-border testbeds in 2026 and a unified AV certification system by 2027. The financial case is already clear — autonomous long-haul trucks can cut cost-per-mile by 42% (McKinsey) and deliver ROI in under 3.5 years on simple corridors. The jobs case is equally clear, and far more politically charged: the EU employs roughly 3.5 million professional truck drivers, 600,000+ bus drivers, and hundreds of thousands in taxi/courier work — a total driving workforce of roughly 5–6 million. These are not abstract statistics; they are predominantly male, 40–55 years old, concentrated in Central and Eastern Europe, with limited transferable skills and historically low engagement with retraining programs. The technology timeline and the labor displacement timeline are converging faster than EU policy is moving. That gap is the core problem this research surfaces.

## Key Findings

- Aurora Innovation launched commercial SAE Level 4 driverless trucking on U.S. public highways in April 2025, logging 250,000+ incident-free miles by early 2026. EU equivalent expected 2028–2030 under current regulatory trajectory.
- Goldman Sachs forecasts autonomous truck cost-per-mile falling from $6.15 (2025) to $1.89 (2030) — a 69% reduction driven by labor, fuel, and insurance savings.
- McKinsey estimates autonomous trucking reduces total cost per mile by 42%. Payback period: 2.5–3.5 years for long-haul corridors.
- EU employs approximately 3.5 million heavy truck drivers (Eurostat/CEDEFOP 2023), of whom 35%+ are over 55. The sector already faces a 500,000-driver shortage — the near-term risk is not unemployment but a structural transition gap.
- Eastern European countries — Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary — supply a disproportionate share of EU road transport labor. Romania and Poland together account for an estimated 25–30% of EU international road haulage drivers.
- Automated trucks could make 50–70% of European truck driver positions redundant by 2035 under aggressive deployment scenarios (ITF/OECD). Even conservative models indicate 2+ million displacements across the US and Europe combined.
- Singapore is set to trial six driverless public buses in Marina Bay from mid-2026 — the world's first public road deployment of full-size autonomous buses. EU trains are advancing under ATO GoA2–GoA4 standards; series production of autonomous solutions targeted for 2030.
- Retraining conversion is proving harder than expected: surveys show truckers, especially long-haul drivers, have low willingness to engage with professional retraining programs, even facing automation.

## SAE Automation Levels by Vehicle Class

The SAE J3016 scale runs from Level 0 (no automation) to Level 5 (full automation in all conditions). Key distinctions: L3 = conditional automation (human must be available to take over); L4 = high automation within a defined Operational Design Domain (ODD) — no human required inside that domain; L5 = full automation everywhere.

| Vehicle Class | Current SAE Level (2026) | Nearest L4 Target | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long-haul trucks (highway) | L4 (commercial, US); L2–L3 (EU pilots) | L4 on defined corridors | 2025 (US operational); 2028–2030 (EU) |
| Medium-duty trucks (B2B fixed routes) | L4 (commercial, Gatik, US) | L4 fixed routes | 2025 (US); 2027–2029 (EU) |
| Last-mile delivery robots (sidewalk/low-speed) | L4 (Starship, limited ODD) | L4 within campus/suburb zones | Already deployed (2,700+ units) |
| Last-mile vans/pods (urban) | L2–L3 | L4 urban ODD | 2028–2032 |
| City buses (scheduled fixed route) | L2 (most); L3 pilot (Singapore, NTU/Volvo) | L4 pilot fixed route | 2026 (Singapore trial); 2030+ (EU series) |
| Rail/metro (urban, controlled environment) | GoA3–GoA4 (many urban metros) | GoA4 fully automated | Metro: already operational (Paris M1, Copenhagen, Dubai); mainline: 2030+ via R2DATO |

**What this means:** Long-haul trucking on highways is the first major commercial casualty. The ODD for highway driving — consistent road markings, no pedestrians, predictable speeds — is the easiest to automate. Urban buses and delivery vans face a longer path because their ODD includes pedestrians, cyclists, weather, and complex intersections.

## Leading Companies by Segment

### Long-Haul Trucking (L4 Highway)

**Aurora Innovation** (NASDAQ: AUR) — the current commercial leader. Launched the first commercial driverless trucking service between Dallas and Houston in April 2025. By Q1 2026: 250,000+ incident-free driverless miles, 10 route lanes across the US Sun Belt, partnerships with FedEx, Schneider, Hirschbach, Uber Freight. Q3 2025 revenue: $1 million (early commercial stage). Targets 200+ driverless trucks by end-2026, with volume manufacturing through Volvo Trucks and PACCAR partners starting 2027.

**Kodiak Robotics** — first to deploy commercial driverless on private roads (December 2024, Atlas Energy Solutions, West Texas). Highway driverless launch targeted H2 2026. Autonomous Readiness Metric: 78% (November 2025).

**Waymo Via** — Waymo's freight arm, partnered with Daimler Trucks to develop SAE L4 on Freightliner Cascadia platform. No commercial launch date announced as of early 2026; likely 2027–2028 for commercial service.

**Volvo Autonomous Solutions / Einride (EU)** — Einride's autonomous electric trucks have been running commercial routes in Sweden since December 2024, moving cargo for Apoteket (largest Swedish pharmacy retailer). Hub-to-hub, within ODD.

**Traton/Scania/MAN (EU)** — TRATON Group (Volkswagen's truck division) partnered with TuSimple to test driverless fleets in Sweden, Germany. Series production target: 2030.

### Medium-Duty Fixed-Route (B2B)

**Gatik AI** — as of January 2026, the first company in North America with fully driverless medium-duty trucks at commercial scale. Operates for Walmart across Texas, Arkansas, Arizona, Nebraska, and Ontario (Canada). No driver, no safety observer, 26–30 ft trucks.

### Last-Mile Delivery Robots

**Starship Technologies** (Estonian company, founded by Skype co-founders) — the global leader by unit count. 2,700 robots across 270 locations in 7 countries (US, UK, Germany, Estonia, Finland, others). 9 million deliveries completed, 19 million km autonomous. Raised €50M EIB loan for EU expansion. SAE L4 within defined campus/suburb zones.

**Nuro** (US) — L4 delivery pods; announced in July 2025 a partnership with Uber and Lucid Motors for 20,000 robotaxi/delivery pods by 2031.

**Amazon** — shut down Amazon Scout robot program in 2022 after mixed results. Currently investing in autonomous delivery via acquisitions and Zoox.

### Autonomous Buses

**NTU Singapore / Volvo Buses** — first full-size autonomous electric bus (40-ft, 80 passengers) trialed at NTU Smart Campus; mid-2026 launch on public roads in Marina Bay/Shenton Way.

**EU incumbents** — Mercedes-Benz, Volvo, Daimler working on CCAM (Connected Cooperative and Automated Mobility) programs; no commercial autonomous bus launch before 2030 in EU.

### Rail (ATO)

**Europe's Rail FP2 R2DATO** — EU-funded flagship project delivering ATO demonstrators by 2025/2026. Regulations for ATO deployment on EU railways entered into force in 2023. Full implementation targets 2030 (ETCS Hybrid Level 3 Moving Block, TRL6 by 2028).

Urban metros (Paris M14, Copenhagen Metro, Dubai Metro) already operate at GoA4 (fully driverless, no staff onboard). Mainline rail automation is 5–10 years behind.

## Infrastructure Requirements

Deploying L4 vehicles at scale requires three interlocking infrastructure layers, none of which are universally ready in the EU today.

**1. Connectivity: 5G and V2X**

Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication requires end-to-end latency under 10 milliseconds for safety-critical messages. 5G C-V2X (Cellular Vehicle-to-Everything) can achieve this; legacy 4G cannot reliably. Current EU 5G coverage: approximately 73% of populated areas as of 2024, but rural and cross-border corridor coverage is patchy. The EU's ITS Directive 2010/40/EU mandated ITS interoperability, but V2X protocol standards between member states remain fragmented. The 5G Automotive Association (5GAA) is pushing global C-V2X standards; without adoption, cross-border AV operations on highways remain legally and technically complicated.

**2. Physical Infrastructure: Sensors, HD Maps**

Roadside sensors (LiDAR, radar, cameras) are required at intersections and complex junctions for infrastructure-assisted perception — especially in low-visibility conditions. HD maps with centimeter accuracy require continuous crowd-sourced updates; Mobileye's Road Experience Management (REM) system is the EU's most mature solution, gathering map data from 200+ million ADAS-equipped vehicles. Major highways in Western Europe have adequate map coverage; Eastern European secondary roads are underserved.

**3. Regulatory Framework**

The EU's March 2025 Automotive Action Plan is the most significant regulatory shift to date:
- Unlimited series approval for automated parking systems: 2025
- Hub-to-hub freight additional use cases: 2026
- At least 3 large-scale cross-border testbeds: starting 2026
- Harmonized testing procedures: early 2026
- Single AV certification valid EU-wide (replaces patchwork national rules): 2027
- European Connected and Automated Vehicle Alliance established: 2025

Without the 2027 unified certification, each member state effectively has veto power over cross-border deployment. Poland, Romania, and Bulgaria have basic national AV testing frameworks (Romania via CMS review; Poland via amendments to the Road Traffic Act) but lack the infrastructure and institutional capacity for rapid regulatory approval. This creates a paradox: the countries most exposed to driver job displacement are also slowest to establish the regulatory conditions for retraining pipeline creation.

## ROI Analysis per Vehicle

The financial case for autonomous trucks is already compelling at scale. The barriers are capital cost, regulatory approval, and fleet transition logistics — not the underlying economics.

**Labor savings**
- Average EU truck driver cost (wages + social contributions): €40,000–€65,000/year depending on country (Western EU on the high end, Eastern EU at €20,000–€30,000). US equivalent: $65,000–$85,000 base + 25–35% benefits.
- Per autonomous truck: eliminates 1.5–2.0 driver-years annually (one driver per shift; autonomous trucks can run 22+ hours/day vs 9 hours for a human driver due to HOS regulations).
- Annual labor saving per truck (EU, conservative): €40,000–€80,000.

**Fuel savings**
- Autonomous trucks optimize acceleration, braking, and speed for fuel efficiency. Estimated saving: 32% fuel reduction (NACFE/Goldman Sachs). At €70,000/year diesel cost for a long-haul truck, this translates to ~€22,000/year saved per vehicle.

**Accident reduction**
- 94% of truck accidents involve human error (NHTSA). Reducing accident frequency lowers insurance premiums and liability costs. Each $1 invested in ADAS yields ~$5.09 in crash-related savings (NACFE study). Full L4 multiplies this further.

**Asset utilization**
- Human drivers are legally limited to 9 hours/day driving time (EU HOS regulations). An autonomous truck can operate 22 hours/day (maintenance window: 2 hours). This doubles the revenue-generating utilization of a €150,000–€200,000 asset.

**Total ROI summary (long-haul corridor)**

| Cost Item | Annual Value (per truck) |
|---|---|
| Driver labor eliminated | €40,000–€80,000 |
| Fuel savings (32%) | ~€22,000 |
| Insurance/accident reduction | ~€10,000–€20,000 |
| Extended operating hours (utilization) | €30,000–€60,000 (revenue equivalent) |
| **Total annual benefit** | **€100,000–€180,000** |
| AV system incremental cost | €30,000–€80,000 (Frost & Sullivan estimates vary widely) |
| **Payback period (long-haul)** | **2.5–3.5 years** |
| **Payback period (urban/mixed)** | **6–8+ years** |

Goldman Sachs projects cost-per-mile for autonomous trucks dropping from $6.15 in 2025 to $1.89 in 2030 as scale increases. At $1.89/mile, autonomous trucking costs less than half the current human-driven rate. At that point, the transition is not optional for carriers that want to remain competitive.

## EU Driving Jobs: Scale and Exposure

As of the most recent EU data (CEDEFOP 2023, Eurostat):

- **Heavy truck and lorry drivers**: approximately 3.5 million in the EU-27. This represents roughly 35% of all transport occupation employment.
- **Bus and coach drivers**: approximately 600,000–800,000 in the EU-27. CEDEFOP notes 53% of current bus/tram drivers are expected to retire within 15 years.
- **Taxi, rideshare, courier drivers**: approximately 500,000–700,000 (highly variable; gig economy workers often unregistered in traditional employment statistics).
- **Light commercial vehicle (delivery van) drivers**: approximately 1.5–2 million, covering parcel, grocery, and B2B delivery.
- **Total professional driving workforce (EU-27 estimate)**: 6–7 million, representing approximately 3–4% of EU total employment.

The sector already faces structural stress before automation arrives: Europe has a shortage of over 500,000 professional truck drivers as of 2025 (IRU). More than one in three truck drivers is over 55 years old. The EU projects 5.2 million job openings for drivers between 2022 and 2035 — mostly replacement demand from retirements, not growth.

**The near-term demographic paradox**: the existing driver shortage creates an illusion of job security. In reality, the shortage and the automation wave are on parallel tracks. Retirement will remove many drivers before automation does — but automation will prevent those positions from being refilled, fundamentally changing the long-term structure of the labor market.

## Job Displacement Timeline

Displacement will not be linear. It follows three phases:

**Phase 1 (2025–2030): Highway corridors, hub-to-hub, private roads**
- Affected roles: long-haul highway drivers on well-mapped, high-volume corridors (Rotterdam-Hamburg, A4 Warsaw-Berlin, Lisbon-Madrid).
- Scale of displacement (EU): limited — perhaps 50,000–100,000 directly affected positions by 2030 as EU regulatory approval lags US by 3–5 years. Most impact in this phase is in the US (Aurora, Kodiak, Gatik scaling fast).
- Mitigating factor: driver shortage means many automated positions simply won't be filled by humans to begin with.

**Phase 2 (2030–2035): Commercial scale, urban-capable systems, bus pilots**
- EU cross-border testbeds operational (2026+) scale into commercial service. Three large-scale corridors become twenty, then hundreds.
- TRATON/Scania/Volvo target series production by 2030. Once vehicles are type-approved at scale, adoption will be rapid for cost reasons alone.
- Bus automation: EU city bus pilots become commercial deployments; first driverless bus service in Singapore (2026) provides the regulatory/operational template.
- Scale of displacement (EU): 500,000–1,000,000 truck driver positions effectively automated or non-replaced across the decade. Bus driver exposure begins.

**Phase 3 (2035–2045): Near-total highway automation, urban expansion**
- ITF/OECD scenario: 50–70% of European truck driver positions automated or structurally non-replaced. Absolute displacement (not just non-replacement) of 1.5–2.5 million in EU.
- Last-mile delivery vans: urban L4 systems reach cost-effective maturity. 500,000–1,000,000 additional courier/van driver positions at risk.
- Critical mass threshold: once >30% of long-haul freight moves autonomously, insurance rates for human-driven trucks may rise (statistical accident comparison), accelerating the transition further.

**Conservative vs aggressive scenario**:
- Conservative (regulatory delays, public resistance): 1.5 million EU driving job displacements by 2040.
- Aggressive (smooth regulation, rapid scale): 3+ million by 2040, plus 1–2 million non-replaced attrition positions.

## Geographic Concentration: Who Bears the Impact?

Driving employment is not evenly distributed across the EU. Central and Eastern European countries are disproportionately exposed for two reasons: (1) they have a higher share of transport employment in their labor mix, and (2) their workers are more concentrated in international long-haul trucking — precisely the segment that automates first.

**Poland** is the EU's largest road freight operator by ton-kilometer. Polish trucking companies (Raben, Rohlig, Geis, hundreds of smaller operators) dominate intra-EU international haulage. Poland is estimated to have 500,000–600,000 professional truck drivers — one of the largest concentrations in the EU. The Polish Movers' Association estimates truck driving is among the top-10 occupations by employment in Poland.

**Romania** is the second-largest supplier of EU international road haulage drivers. Romanian truckers are typically hired by Western EU companies but based in Romania — a labor arbitrage model. Romania's truck driver population: estimated 250,000–350,000. Romania has already lost millions of workers to Western European emigration; trucking is one of the few domestic occupations that pays close to Western wages via posted-worker contracts.

**Bulgaria, Hungary, Lithuania, Latvia** — similarly exposed. Lithuania and Latvia have among the highest truck-driver-to-population ratios in the EU, given their role as transit corridors between Western Europe and the CIS/Baltic states.

**Germany** and **Netherlands** are the largest employers in absolute terms but have more diversified labor markets. The social impact per worker is higher in Eastern Europe because few alternative formal-sector jobs offer equivalent wages.

**The political risk** is concrete: automation of long-haul trucking will hit Poland, Romania, and Bulgaria harder than Germany or France in relative terms — simultaneously with their ongoing demographic decline and emigration losses. Without coordinated EU-level support, this creates a political crisis, not just an economic one.

## Retraining and Transition Options

The honest answer is that large-scale driver retraining is harder than policy documents suggest, for reasons that are structural rather than motivational.

**What skills do truck drivers have?**
- Long-distance navigation, fatigue management, load securing, basic vehicle maintenance, customer-facing delivery (for courier roles).
- Transferable to: fleet supervisor/dispatcher roles, logistics operations, remote operations center (monitoring autonomous trucks), freight forwarding, warehouse management.
- Less transferable to: software, healthcare, construction (physical requirements), finance.

**What is realistically achievable?**
Studies on alternative occupations for truck drivers (ScienceDirect, 2023) identify the following viable paths ranked by accessibility:

1. **Remote vehicle operations supervisor** — monitoring fleets of autonomous trucks, intervening when systems flag exceptions. Estimated 1 remote operator per 5–10 autonomous vehicles during early deployment. As reliability improves, ratios drop to 1:30–1:50. This is a temporary buffer job, not a permanent transition.
2. **Logistics dispatcher / freight coordinator** — leverages routing and logistics knowledge; requires digital literacy upgrade. Moderate transition; already exists as a career path for older drivers.
3. **Warehouse operations** — physical, accessible without significant retraining. Pay is lower than long-haul trucking. Many existing warehouse roles are also being automated (Amazon-style robotics).
4. **Vehicle technician / maintenance** — autonomous vehicles have more complex sensor and compute systems, creating some new demand. However, volume is insufficient to absorb large numbers.
5. **Construction and trades** — structural labor shortage in EU construction; physically demanding but accessible without advanced education.

**What research shows about retraining willingness**:
A critical finding from CEDEFOP and ITF research: long-haul truck drivers, specifically, show the lowest willingness to engage in professional retraining programs of any at-risk occupational group studied. Factors include: age (35+ is the median affected cohort), geographic isolation (long-haul truckers spend weeks away from their home region), distrust of institutional programs, and the fact that current wages are relatively high — the perceived urgency is low until automation actually arrives.

**What programs exist or are needed?**
- Germany has expanded its Bildungsgutschein (education voucher) system to include drivers facing structural displacement.
- EU Just Transition Fund (JTF) provides financing for regions undergoing structural industrial change — but was designed primarily for coal/industrial transitions, not yet systematically applied to transport automation.
- No EU member state as of 2026 has a specific, scaled retraining program for truck drivers facing automation. This is a policy gap.
- Estimated cost of adequate transition support (EU-wide, over 10 years): Unknown. No public comprehensive estimate available as of 2026. Comparable programs (coal transition) cost €5,000–€15,000 per worker for retraining; at 2 million displaced drivers, this implies €10–30 billion — not including income support during transition, which dwarfs the training cost.

## Case Studies: Phoenix, Singapore, and European Pilots

### Phoenix (Waymo One) — the most mature L4 robotaxi deployment globally

Phoenix is the only city in the world with a multi-year, multi-hundred-vehicle, commercially operating L4 robotaxi service accessible to the general public (no waitlist as of 2025). Key metrics:

- Service area: 315 square miles (the largest robotaxi territory in the US), including freeway operations at up to 65 mph.
- Fleet: part of Waymo's 2,500-vehicle US fleet; Phoenix is the oldest and most dense market.
- Volume: Phoenix Sky Harbor Airport alone handles approximately 1,000–1,200 daily robotaxi trips; overall Phoenix volume undisclosed but estimated at 5,000–8,000 trips/day.
- Safety record: Waymo logged 100 million fully autonomous miles by July 2025, doubling from 50 million in just six months. Independent studies (Swiss Re, NHTSA data) show Waymo's airbag deployment rate is approximately 2.1 per million miles vs 4.5 for human-driven vehicles in comparable conditions.
- Expansion: DoorDash partnership launched in October 2025 for autonomous grocery/restaurant delivery across all 315 square miles of Phoenix.

**Lesson for EU**: The Phoenix deployment demonstrates that L4 robotaxis can operate safely and commercially in a Sun Belt city with clear weather and well-mapped roads. The transfer to EU conditions — denser urban cores, narrower streets, greater pedestrian/cyclist mix, variable weather — is non-trivial but not impossible. The operational model (remote teleoperations center, 24/7 monitoring, real-time map updates) is the template EU cities should be studying now.

### Singapore Autonomous Bus Trials

Singapore is arguably the most advanced country globally in preparing its public transport system for automation, driven by a combination of labor scarcity, high wages, and strong state capacity.

- **NTU / Volvo program (2019–2025)**: World's first full-size (12-meter, 80-passenger) autonomous electric bus trialed on NTU Smart Campus and later on public roads with safety drivers present. The bus is equipped with LiDAR, radar, cameras, and NTU-developed AI navigating fixed routes at L3/L4 within the campus ODD.
- **Mid-2026 public launch**: Singapore's Land Transport Authority (LTA) has approved six driverless buses for commercial passenger service in the Marina Bay / Shenton Way financial district. These will be the world's first driverless full-size buses operating on public roads without a safety driver. Initial service alongside manned buses for three years.
- **Key lesson**: Singapore proves that urban bus autonomy is technically achievable on well-defined fixed routes in controlled environments. The regulatory path — years of shadow-mode testing, then safety-driver mode, then gradual driverless expansion — is the model Europe's regulatory bodies (especially UNECE WP.29) are following. The Singapore timeline suggests European cities could realistically have driverless bus pilots on selected routes by 2030–2032 if regulatory frameworks are in place.

### European Pilots: MODI, Einride, TuSimple/Scania

**MODI project (EU Horizon-funded, 2023–2026)**:
Testing autonomous transport integration across three key European hubs:
- Rotterdam (busy port with mixed traffic)
- Hamburg (motorway-to-urban road transitions)
- Gothenburg (hub-to-hub automated charging and loading)

MODI's focus is not just vehicle autonomy but the systemic integration: border crossings, automated refueling, loading dock operations. The findings will feed directly into the European Commission's 2026 cross-border testbed requirements.

**Einride (Sweden, commercial since December 2024)**:
Einride's autonomous electric trucks (the "Pod" — a cab-less, purpose-built L4 design) have been running commercial freight between Apoteket warehouses in Sweden since December 2024. This makes Einride the first company with commercially operating autonomous trucks in Europe. The vehicles are remote-operated from Einride's operations center; the system is conservative (low speeds, well-mapped routes) but real and revenue-generating.

**TuSimple / TRATON / Scania (Sweden, Germany testing)**:
TuSimple's autonomous driving technology has been integrated with Scania S500 trucks for testing in Sweden and Germany. TRATON's stated target is series production of autonomous trucks by 2030 — matching the EU Commission's regulatory roadmap. The testing phase involves safety drivers; driverless trials are contingent on Germany's updated Road Traffic Act (StVG) autonomous provisions.

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

Autonomous vehicles sit at the intersection of all three reform pillars the zentala.eu project argues for, but they most directly address Pillar 3: automation and robotics as the EU's demographic response.

**The demographic arithmetic**: The EU faces a 500,000 truck driver shortage today, which will grow to 745,000 by 2028 (IRU). More than a third of current drivers are over 55 and approaching retirement. Without automation, EU freight capacity shrinks as the driver pool ages out. Autonomous trucks are not primarily a job-destroying technology in the EU context — they are a structural replacement for workers who are retiring faster than they can be replaced. The political framing matters enormously: this is not "robots taking jobs," it is "robots filling the jobs that young Europeans don't want and aging Europeans are leaving."

**The competitiveness arithmetic**: US carriers that adopt Aurora-class autonomous trucks in 2026–2028 will operate at ~$1.89/mile by 2030. EU carriers operating with human drivers at €0.50–€0.70/km (equivalent range) will face a structural cost disadvantage of 30–50% on long-haul freight — unless the EU accelerates its own deployment timeline. European OEMs (Volvo, Scania, Daimler) are already developing the technology; the chokepoint is regulatory approval and infrastructure, both of which are EU-level problems requiring EU-level coordination.

**The reform imperative**: The EU's March 2025 Automotive Action Plan is the right direction but insufficient in speed. Single AV certification by 2027 is necessary but not sufficient — it needs to be accompanied by a Just Transition Fund deployment specifically targeting truck driver communities in Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, and the Baltic states, funded at the scale the coal transition received. The window for proactive transition management is 2026–2032. By 2035, the displacement will largely have happened, and the political cost of delayed action will be concentrated in the countries least able to absorb it.
