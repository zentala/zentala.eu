# English Language Adoption in Europe: Proficiency, Barriers, and Policy Models

## Summary

Europe is already far along the path toward English as a common working language — but the distribution is deeply uneven. As of 2024, 50% of EU citizens speak English as a foreign language (Eurobarometer 540, 2024), up from 45% in 2012. Among those aged 15–24, the figure rises to 70%. Northern and Northwestern countries have achieved near-universal functional English; Southern and Eastern countries lag by 20–40 percentage points. The evidence suggests the manifest's language integration proposal is realistic for a 20–30 year horizon, but would require targeted investment in adult re-skilling, rural access, and political reframing — particularly in France, Italy, and Hungary, where national language identity is constitutionally embedded. The economic case for this investment is strong: common language increases bilateral trade by 40–55%, and English proficiency yields wage premiums of 5–19% depending on sector.

---

## Key Findings

1. **50% of EU adults speak English** as a second language (Eurobarometer 540, 2023 survey). Among 15–24 year-olds: 70%.
2. **Northern Europe dominates**: Netherlands (EF EPI score 624, ranked #1 globally), Norway (613), Denmark (611), Sweden (609). Southern Europe lags: Italy (513), France (539), Spain (540).
3. **The generational gap is the single biggest predictor**: 18–24 year-olds are ~12–15 percentage points more likely to speak a foreign language than 55–64 year-olds. This gap is self-correcting over time as older cohorts are replaced.
4. **Three-quarters of young Europeans (15–24) engage with English weekly**, up from 62% in 2014 — driven by streaming platforms, social media, and gaming.
5. **Netherlands model works**: 20% of Dutch secondary schools deliver 30–50% of curriculum in English (CLIL). Students show measurably higher oral and reading proficiency versus non-CLIL peers.
6. **Language barriers suppress trade by approximately 40–55%**: sharing a common language increases bilateral trade by that margin (meta-analysis of gravity model studies).
7. **English proficiency yields 5–19% wage premium** in European labor markets; for Polish workers the premium is approximately 11%.
8. **Political resistance is real but fragile**: France, Hungary, and Italy have constitutional language protections; post-Brexit, the legal status of English in EU institutions is contested but practically unchanged.

- 50% of EU adults speak English as a foreign language (up from 45% in 2012)

- Belgium: 49.0%

## 2. The Adoption Trend: Accelerating or Plateauing?

### The Generational Acceleration Is Real

The trend is strongly positive for young cohorts and decelerating for adults over 40.

- **2012–2024**: EU-wide English speakers rose from 45% to 50% (+5 pp in 12 years)
- **Among 15–24 year-olds**: 79% can have a conversation in at least one foreign language (+5 pp vs 2012)
- **Weekly English contact**: 74% of 15–24 year-olds engage with English weekly, up from 62% in 2014 (+12 pp in 10 years)
- **Preference for subtitles over dubbing**: 65% of young Europeans prefer subtitled over dubbed content — this is a major driver of passive English acquisition in Northern and Eastern Europe

*Sources: Eurobarometer 540 (2024); EF EPI historical data.*

### The Global Plateau Warning

The EF EPI 2024 press release noted that global English proficiency "fell back to 2011 levels after years of pandemic-era decline." However, this global decline is driven heavily by non-European regions. European countries have gained +39 to +59 percentage points in higher proficiency bands between 2011 and 2024. The global average does not reflect European trajectories.

### The Generational Replacement Dynamic

The single most powerful force is demographic: cohorts educated since 1990 in most EU countries studied English from age 8–10, and in many Northern European countries from age 6. As they replace workers educated before 1980 (who largely did not study English), overall proficiency will rise without additional policy intervention.

**Projected trajectory (conservative)**: If current generational trends hold, EU-wide English proficiency could reach 60–65% by 2035 purely through cohort replacement, before any new policy.

## 3. Who Speaks English and Who Doesn't: Demographic Breakdown

### Age

The age gap is the dominant demographic divide:

- **18–24 year-olds**: 87.3% know at least 1 foreign language (predominantly English)
- **25–64 year-olds**: 74.7% (+12.6 pp gap vs youth cohort)
- **55–64 year-olds**: approximately 20.4% report proficiency in their strongest foreign language (vs 34.9% for 25–34 year-olds)

Practical implication: a 60-year-old civil servant in Warsaw or Bucharest who was educated in the 1970s–1980s under Soviet-era schooling had almost no English instruction. A 25-year-old colleague likely has B1–B2.

*Source: Eurostat, 2022 data. EF EPI notes: young professionals (25–34) are more proficient than students and adults over 40.*

### Education Level

Education level is the second-strongest predictor:

- **Tertiary-educated adults**: 90% know at least one foreign language
- **Medium education**: significantly lower, no precise EU-wide figure available in public data
- **Low education**: substantially lower; Eurostat cites the highest-educated showing 34.9% achieving C1/C2 versus roughly 5–8% for those with primary education only

The education-language correlation creates a structural inequality: professional-class workers benefit from language skills in the labor market while lower-educated workers cannot.

### Urban vs. Rural

Direct EU-wide data on urban-rural English proficiency gap is not published separately by Eurostat. The following proxies are available:

- Rural youth early school-leaving rate: 9.9% vs 8.6% in cities (Eurostat 2023) — indicating less total education, which correlates with lower language proficiency
- Rural areas have systematically weaker English teaching due to teacher supply constraints
- Rural median income is approximately 20% lower than urban median (Eurostat 2023), correlating with lower access to private tutoring, travel, and English-language media consumption

*Direct EU rural-urban English proficiency gap: Unknown. No dedicated Eurostat data available as of 2026.*

### Socioeconomic Status

Research from Asia and developing contexts documents a clear correlation between household income and English proficiency. For Europe specifically:

- Private English tutoring is more accessible to higher-income households
- Business travel and international exposure reinforce adult English skills among knowledge workers
- Lower-income workers in domestic service industries have less English-language job demand

*Precise income-stratified EU English proficiency data: Unknown. No public Eurostat dataset available as of 2026. The correlation is structurally implied but not quantified for EU specifically.*

### Gender

The EF EPI 2024 report flagged "weaker skills in women and young adults" as a notable finding. This may reflect differences in STEM track enrollment and types of international work exposure, but the gender gap in Europe is smaller than in Middle Eastern and Asian contexts. Gender as a major barrier is not well-supported for EU member states.

## 4. Successful Bilingual Models: Belgium, Luxembourg, Netherlands

### The Netherlands — The Gold Standard

The Netherlands ranks #1 globally in the EF EPI 2025 with a score of 624. The Netherlands holds this position for the sixth consecutive year as of the 2024 index.

**Policy specifics:**
- Bilingual secondary education (TTO — Tweetalig Onderwijs) began in 1989 as a teacher- and parent-driven initiative
- 20% of secondary schools are now bilingual (as of 2023); 132 schools in the Dutch Network of Bilingual Schools, 115 with official accreditation
- **CLIL model**: 30–50% of curriculum delivered in English, not just English-as-subject
- Quality enforced by the Standard for Bilingual Education — teacher minimum proficiency requirements, regular school inspections
- English instruction starts in primary school; bilingual track begins at age 12

**What produces the result:**
1. Early start: English from age 6–8 in regular schools, bilingual track from age 12
2. Media exposure: Dutch television and film historically subtitled, not dubbed — creating passive immersion
3. High linguistic aptitude: Dutch is a Germanic language closely related to English, lowering acquisition cost
4. Cultural openness: Netherlands is a small, export-dependent economy; English fluency is economically rational

**Measured outcomes**: CLIL students show significantly higher oral proficiency and reading comprehension versus non-CLIL peers in the same schools; bilingual graduates reach C1–C2 levels more frequently.

### Luxembourg — Mandatory Trilingualism

Luxembourg is unique: the national school system mandates instruction in three languages.

- **Primary school**: German as main language of instruction; French introduced progressively
- **Secondary school**: French becomes main instructional language; English introduced
- All students are expected to achieve functional literacy in Luxembourgish, French, and German, with English as a fourth
- European Schools (tuition-free) offer English, French, or German as primary language of instruction
- As of 2023/2024, fewer than one-third of primary school students have Luxembourgish as first language — Luxembourg's school system is already managing a de facto multilingual immigrant-heavy population

**Why Luxembourg works**: its economy is structurally international (EU institutions, financial services, cross-border commuters). Language acquisition is an economic necessity.

### Belgium — Divergent Models

Belgium's language policy is federated and complex. No single Belgian model exists:

- Flanders (Dutch-speaking): schools follow the Netherlands CLIL model; French mandated, English widely taught
- Wallonia (French-speaking): French primary; Dutch and English as secondary languages
- Brussels: officially bilingual (French-Dutch), with English as a major working language in EU/NATO institutions

The Brussels environment produces high English proficiency among the professional class but limited penetration in working-class communities.

*Specific Belgian per-region proficiency data: detailed breakdown not available in public datasets reviewed.*

### Key Lessons for Policy

Three factors distinguish high-proficiency countries from low-proficiency ones:
1. **Early start**: before age 10, when acquisition is fastest
2. **CLIL over subject-English**: learning history in English is more effective than studying English as a subject
3. **Passive exposure infrastructure**: non-dubbing policy (subtitles) creates millions of informal learning hours per year that cost the state nothing

## 5. The Cost of English at Scale

### Education Budget Context

EU member states spend an average of 4.6% of GDP on education (Eurostat 2022). Variation:
- Highest: Sweden (6.9%), Belgium (6.2%), Finland (5.9%)
- Lowest: Romania (2.9%), Croatia (3.1%), Greece (3.4%)

OECD average per-student spending: ~€10,000–11,000/year at primary level, ~€12,000/year at secondary.

### Cost of English Language Instruction

The incremental cost of English instruction within existing schooling is low because:
- English is already a mandated subject in all EU secondary curricula
- The CLIL model repurposes existing teaching hours rather than adding new ones

**CLIL teacher premium**: approximately 10–20% salary premium for bilingual-qualified teachers; the Netherlands' bilingual school accreditation requires teachers to hold minimum C1 English certification.

**Adult re-skilling cost**: This is the expensive part. For adults already in the workforce:
- Intensive language courses to B1 (from A0): approximately 375–500 guided hours
- At market rates for group instruction in Europe: approximately €15–25/hour
- **Estimated per-adult cost to reach B1**: €5,600–12,500 (375 hours × €15–33 group course pricing)
- For state-subsidized programs (e.g., government employee re-skilling at scale): marginal cost significantly lower via digital-first delivery

*No EU-wide budget figure for language instruction specifically is published. The above is a cost estimate derived from CEFR learning-hour standards and European adult education market pricing.*

### Timeline to B1 Proficiency

From absolute beginner (A0) to B1 ("threshold" — able to handle most everyday situations):
- **Cambridge English and British Council standard**: approximately 200 guided learning hours per CEFR level
- **Reaching B1 from A0**: requires passing through A1, A2, and lower-B1 — approximately 375–500 hours of guided instruction
- **Realistic adult schedule**: 2 hours/week of formal instruction + informal exposure = approximately 3–4 years to functional B1
- With intensive instruction (10 hours/week): 9–12 months to B1 from zero
- **For speakers of related European languages** (Dutch, German, Swedish, Norwegian): significant head start — may reach B1 in 150–250 hours due to cognate vocabulary

For speakers of linguistically distant languages (Hungarian, Finnish, Estonian, which are non-Indo-European), the timeline is longer — these learners face higher acquisition costs.

*Sources: Cambridge English guided learning hours; Pearson GSE Learning Hours report; British Council CEFR level descriptions.*

### What % of Public Service Jobs Would Require Bilingual Staff

*Unknown. No EU-wide study has modeled the workforce share requiring English in a hypothetical EU bilingual public administration system. Estimate based on analogous situations:*

- EU institutions currently operate with approximately 60–70% of internal documents in English; staff are expected to function in at least English and one other working language
- In practical terms, front-facing public services would require bilingual capacity for 20–40% of roles (border services, healthcare where international patients appear, tourism-adjacent municipalities, digital government services)
- Back-office roles in IT, procurement, and research would require higher rates (~60–80%) to interact with EU procurement and funding systems

## 6. The Economic ROI: Why English Pays

### Trade Facilitation

The economic literature on language and trade is consistent: shared language is one of the strongest determinants of bilateral trade.

- **Meta-analysis of 701 gravity model estimates** (Egger & Lassmann, 2012, Economics Letters): a common language increases bilateral trade by approximately **40–55%**
- **Melitz & Toubal (2014)**: common native language biases import values toward shared-language partners by **18 percentage points** and transaction counts by **20 percentage points**
- **Anderson & van Wincoop (2004)**: language barriers carry a tax equivalent of approximately 7% of trade value
- A 2025 study using panel ARDL models across 105 countries (2011–2023) confirms that English proficiency has a positive and significant long-run effect on trade volumes, though effects on trade balance are more mixed regionally

For the EU specifically: the EU single market eliminates tariff barriers but language fragmentation continues to suppress intra-EU trade and investment, particularly for SMEs that cannot afford professional translators.

### Wage Premium

Research on European labor markets documents consistent but varied premiums for English proficiency:

- **Average foreign language premium**: 11% (Springer/Empirica, "The wage premium from foreign language skills")
- **Poland-specific English premium**: approximately 11% — comparable to German; Spanish, French, and Italian command higher premiums there (32%, 22%, 15% respectively) due to scarcity
- **Multi-language premium in 2026 estimates**: bilingual workers earning up to 19% more on average (Talkio AI, citing labor market data)
- **Range across EU**: 3–5% in most Western European countries where English is already widely spoken; 10–15%+ in Southern and Eastern European countries where English-proficiency is differentiating
- **UK-specific historical data**: English-proficient roles in London 5–10% premium; highly specialist technical English: up to 20%

**Diminishing returns logic**: English proficiency commands a higher salary premium where it is rare (Italy, Spain, Eastern Europe) than where it is universal (Netherlands, Denmark). As EU-wide proficiency rises, the individual salary premium shrinks — but the systemic benefit increases.

### Labor Mobility and Human Capital

- 70% of young Europeans report learning English to improve job prospects (Eurobarometer 540)
- English-proficient workers are significantly more mobile within the EU — they can apply to jobs in any member state
- This functions as a free-trade agreement for human capital; language barriers are to labor mobility what tariffs are to goods

## 7. Barriers: What Actually Stops People

### Age as a Structural Barrier

Adults over 50 who did not learn English in school face the highest barriers. For a 55-year-old Polish factory worker or a 60-year-old French civil servant, reaching B1 English requires 375–500 hours of instruction — approximately 3–4 years at part-time pace. The motivation is often low unless tied to job requirements or economic incentives.

This is not an insurmountable barrier — it is a policy design challenge requiring incentives (pay raises tied to certification), accessible delivery (digital, blended, at work), and realistic timeline expectations.

### Political and Cultural Resistance

**France**: French language identity is constitutionally protected and politically charged. The Académie française has historically resisted Anglicisms. However, French companies increasingly require English for professional roles, creating a pragmatic override.

**Hungary**: Hungarian is constitutionally identified as the state's official language. PM Orbán's government has used cultural nationalism that includes linguistic identity as a political marker.

**Italy**: English proficiency is among the EU's lowest (EF EPI 513, rank 59). Italy's low proficiency reflects a combination of strong dubbing culture (eliminates passive English exposure), late English introduction in schooling, and strong Italian-language media ecosystem.

**Post-Brexit legal ambiguity**: After the UK left the EU, English technically lost its primary national sponsor (Ireland nominated Irish, Malta nominated Maltese as their primary EU languages). This has created legal uncertainty about English's formal EU status — while its practical dominance as EU working language is unaltered. A European Parliament question in 2022 challenged whether English could remain an official working language. No resolution has forced change.

**The Euro-English argument**: Some linguists argue that post-Brexit is actually the right moment to formalize "Euro-English" as a supranational linguistic identity — owned by no one nation, therefore less politically charged. This could reduce French and German resistance to English adoption.

### Dubbing Culture Correlates with Low Proficiency

There is a striking correlation between countries that dub foreign content and those with low English proficiency:

- **Non-dubbing countries** (subtitles): Netherlands, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Portugal, Romania → consistently top EF EPI rankings
- **Dubbing countries**: Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Austria, Hungary → systematically lower EF EPI scores despite comparable education investments

The Netherlands produces several hundred hours of passive English exposure annually per citizen through subtitled television. Italy produces zero. This is a major policy lever that costs governments nothing to change.

### Access Inequality as a Systemic Risk

The risk of a mandatory English policy is that it advantages already-advantaged populations:
- Urban, higher-educated, higher-income workers already speak English
- Rural, lower-educated, lower-income workers who do not speak English would face a job market that increasingly requires it
- A poorly designed "make English the EU working language" policy could function as a regressive labor market filter, excluding the most vulnerable

This is the strongest practical argument for investing in access before mandating English requirements.

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

### Is the Manifest's Proposal Realistic?

**Yes, but on a 20–30 year horizon, not 5–10 years.**

The evidence supports a conditional optimism:

1. **The trajectory is already set**: the generational replacement of low-English-proficiency adults with high-proficiency young people will raise EU-wide English rates to approximately 60–65% by 2035 even without new policy. The policy question is whether to accelerate this, not whether to start from zero.

2. **The Netherlands proves the ceiling is high**: a country with a Germanic language, subtitling culture, CLIL education, and small-economy pragmatism has achieved near-universal functional English among working-age adults. This is a replicable model.

3. **The cost is front-loaded**: the expensive phase is adult re-skilling (the current 45–65 age cohort). Once this cohort retires, English proficiency self-sustains through schooling. A 15-year focused program targeting 200 million working-age low-proficiency adults would be expensive but finite.

4. **The political path is through economic framing, not cultural imposition**: resistance is highest when English is framed as replacing national languages. Resistance drops sharply when it is framed as a professional second layer — "keep your language, add English alongside it." Ireland, Luxembourg, and the Netherlands all demonstrate that strong national identity and high English proficiency coexist without contradiction.

5. **The strongest case is bureaucratic friction cost**: the EU currently maintains 24 official languages, requiring translation/interpretation services that cost approximately €1 billion/year. For 27 member states to conduct EU business bilingually (national language + English) would eliminate this overhead while preserving national linguistic sovereignty.

### What It Would Actually Take

- **Education**: Accelerate CLIL adoption to 30–50% of secondary schools across all EU member states; set minimum teacher English proficiency standards; phase out mandatory dubbing in favor of subtitles for state broadcasters
- **Adult workforce**: EU-funded English re-skilling program for 45–60 year-olds currently in public service roles; certify to B2 minimum for any EU-facing function
- **Regulatory**: English as official additional working language in EU institutions — requiring only Irish and Maltese to formally designate English as their national EU language (both already use it de facto)
- **Timeline**: 10 years to reach 65% EU English proficiency; 20–25 years to reach 80%; functional EU bilingual administration achievable within one generation
