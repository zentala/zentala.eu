# Digital State Models: US, China, Russia, India, and the EU's Third Way

## Summary

The EU is not designing its digital future in a vacuum. Four models already exist — and each
represents a fundamentally different answer to the same question: who controls the digital
infrastructure that modern societies run on? The US chose the market: private companies built
the stack, the state never unified around a national digital identity, and fragmentation is
the default. China chose the state: centralized digital ID, surveillance infrastructure
covering hundreds of millions of cameras, a social credit framework for 33 million+ businesses,
and a digital yuan designed to give the CCP real-time visibility into every financial transaction.
Russia chose paranoid isolation: a $660 million "sovereign internet" investment to build a
killswitch for the global internet, combined with a domestic DNS and DPI equipment on every ISP.
India chose pragmatic scale: 1.38 billion Aadhaar numbers issued, 150+ billion authentication
transactions, universal coverage — but persistent data breaches and a privacy framework still
being built out. The EU is attempting something genuinely different: a rights-based, federated,
regulation-first approach. Whether that constitutes a viable "third way" or a slow-motion
compromise depends entirely on whether the regulatory architecture finally acquires an
execution layer. For the zentala.eu manifest, this comparison provides the most important
context: the EU's path to competitive digital statehood cannot copy any of these models, but
it must match their ambition.

## Key Findings

1. **US digital identity is a patchwork**: As of late 2024, only 13 US states had mobile
   driver's licenses. Login.gov failed NIST identity proofing standards until October 2024.
   Federal agencies spent $210 million on commercial ID verification (FY2020–2023) versus
   just $32.5 million on Login.gov — a 6:1 ratio in favor of private vendors over the
   public option.
2. **US AI investment dominance**: Cumulative private AI investment in the US, 2013–2024,
   exceeded $470 billion — compared with roughly $50 billion across all EU countries. In 2024
   alone, US institutions produced 40 notable AI models vs. Europe's three.
3. **China's surveillance scale**: An estimated 200–400 million CCTV cameras in China,
   approximately 54% of the world's total. SenseTime and Megvii facial recognition platforms
   serve 40+ police bureaus. Traffic cameras issue 6.8 million jaywalking tickets daily.
   Over 1 million Uyghurs estimated in Xinjiang re-education camps connected to the
   Integrated Joint Operations Platform (IJOP).
4. **China digital yuan**: 225 million personal e-CNY wallets as of 2025; cumulative
   transactions reached approximately 7 trillion RMB ($986 billion) by mid-2024. Expanding
   to Belt and Road Initiative countries to challenge dollar hegemony.
5. **Russia's internet isolation cost**: Russian government allocated 600 billion rubles
   ($660 million) over five years for TSPU filtering infrastructure. Equipment costs for
   ISPs estimated at 134 billion RUB/year. As of March 2026, new decree gives Roskomnadzor
   authority to switch Runet into full isolation mode in real time.
6. **India Aadhaar scale**: 1.38+ billion IDs issued (96%+ population). 2.5 billion
   monthly authentications. But in October 2023, data including Aadhaar numbers of ~850
   million Indians leaked onto the dark web.
7. **EU regulatory corpus is complete; execution is not**: The EU has enacted GDPR, eIDAS 2.0,
   AI Act, DSA, DMA, and the Digital Decade targets — the most comprehensive digital
   governance framework on the planet. Yet 45% of cross-border EU services still do not
   accept IDs from other member states, and the EUDI wallet mandatory deadline has already
   slipped to 2026.
8. **At least 80 countries** have adopted Chinese police and/or surveillance technology.
   Experts project over 100 countries will deploy behavioral scoring systems inspired by
   China's model by 2027.

## The US Model: Market-Driven Fragmentation

### Why US Digital Infrastructure Is Fragmented

The United States has no unified government digital stack. This is not an accident — it is
a design philosophy. The federal system delegates enormous authority to individual states,
and the political culture has consistently favored private-sector solutions over federal
digital platforms. The result: every agency, every state, and every department built its own
approach.

**Login.gov**, the federal effort to provide a single sign-on for government services, is the
closest thing to a national digital identity system. It failed to meet NIST identity proofing
standards (IAL2) until October 2024. From fiscal years 2020 to 2023, federal agencies spent
nearly **$210 million on commercial identity verification solutions** — more than six times
the $32.5 million spent on Login.gov itself. The IRS declined to adopt Login.gov due to
ongoing compliance concerns. By September 2024, only 13 states had mobile driver's licenses
(mDLs), and these were not interoperable between states.

The identity landscape is highly fragmented across individual holders, issuers, and verifiers,
with multiple standards — both technological and regulatory — existing across states, agencies,
and private issuers. The White House stalled on a digital identity mandate in early 2024
despite documented fraud losses in the billions running through fragmented verification.

Structural reasons this persists:
- **Constitutional structure**: No federal mandate can force uniform state identity systems.
- **Private sector capture**: Lobbying by identity verification vendors (LexisNexis, ID.me,
  Experian) keeps commercial alternatives entrenched.
- **Political resistance**: National ID proposals routinely die due to civil liberties concerns.
- **No crisis forcing convergence**: The US has no EU-wide internal market pressure that
  makes cross-border identity interoperability an economic necessity.

### How the US Maintains Tech Dominance

Despite governmental fragmentation, the US maintains overwhelming tech leadership through
three mechanisms:

**Capital concentration.** Silicon Valley venture capital firms deployed over $28 billion
in 2024 alone. Cumulative private AI investment in the US from 2013 to 2024 exceeded **$470
billion** — roughly 9.4x the ~$50 billion across EU countries over the same period. The US
accounts for over 75% of reported global funding in generative AI ventures.

**Talent gravity.** Stanford and UC Berkeley graduate approximately 15,000 engineering and
computer science students annually. Critically, the EU trains AI researchers at competitive
levels — but those researchers emigrate: EU-trained talent flows to the US, not the other
way around. In 2024, US institutions produced **40 notable AI models**; Europe produced
three.

**Infrastructure scale.** The US hosted an estimated 4,049 data centers as of 2024 (vs.
~2,250 in the EU). In 2024, the US added **5.8 gigawatts** of new data center capacity —
vs. 1.6 GW in the EU. This infrastructure advantage compounds: AI training requires
proximity to infrastructure, and US companies can iterate faster as a result.

### Advantages and Disadvantages of the US Model

**Advantages**: Speed. The market-driven model produced Amazon, Google, Apple, Meta, and
Microsoft — the infrastructure layer that now underlies much of the global internet —
without waiting for political consensus. Regulatory lightness (Section 230's near-absolute
liability shield for platforms) permitted experiments that centralized systems would have
blocked.

**Disadvantages**: Exclusion, fragmentation, and dependency. Roughly 15% of US households
lack broadband access. The elderly, poor, and rural populations are systematically
disadvantaged by market provision. Federal agencies run on legacy systems, some dating to
the 1980s, that were not designed for modern integration. Meanwhile, 87% of federal agency
leaders agree their agency would benefit from IT modernization, but only 43% say it is a
priority — indicating political economy barriers, not technical ones.

The US model exports its standards through market dominance (US cloud = global cloud),
not through governance frameworks. The EU depends on US cloud providers for approximately
65% of its cloud market — meaning European digital sovereignty is structurally compromised
by the American market-driven model's success.

## The Chinese Model: State-Built Digital Surveillance

### How China Is Building Its Digital State

China's approach inverts the US model: the state defines the architecture, private companies
implement it within CCP guidelines, and the resulting infrastructure is designed for control
as much as for service.

The foundation is the **Resident Identity Card system**: every Chinese citizen carries an
18-digit national ID number linked to their name, address, gender, ethnicity, birthday,
photo, and blood type. Updated cards include a microchip. This ID is linked to phone numbers,
social media accounts, and bank accounts — creating a unified identity anchor that no Western
democracy has implemented or legally could implement in its current form.

### The Surveillance Infrastructure

China's surveillance infrastructure is the most extensive ever built:

- **Camera network**: Estimated 200–400 million CCTV cameras, constituting approximately
  54% of the world's total. AI facial recognition software operates in tandem with over
  200 million of these cameras.
- **Facial recognition accuracy**: Platforms from SenseTime and Megvii achieve 98.5%
  identification accuracy. These platforms serve 40+ police bureaus nationwide, scanning
  millions of faces daily.
- **Gait and voice recognition**: Supplementing facial recognition with non-facial
  biometric identification methods that cannot be defeated by masks.
- **Traffic enforcement at scale**: Cameras in major cities issue **6.8 million jaywalking
  tickets daily** — not through police, but through automated AI-driven systems.
- **Xinjiang's IJOP (Integrated Joint Operations Platform)**: Gathers data via iris
  scanners, CCTV, face and voice recognition, and DNA sampling, cross-referenced with
  banking, phone calls, and text messages. Estimates place over 1 million Uyghurs in
  "re-education camps" connected to automated detention workflows from IJOP.
- **Predictive algorithms**: Systems claiming 95% accuracy in forecasting social unrest,
  enabling pre-crime detention.

### The Social Credit System

The social credit system is frequently mischaracterized as a single unified national score.
The reality is more complex and more dangerous: it is a collection of overlapping systems
at corporate, municipal, and national levels.

By 2022, approximately **80% of China's provinces, regions, and cities** had introduced
some version of the system. **More than 33 million businesses** have been assigned scores
under a corporate social credit framework. For individuals, the national blacklist system
tracks "untrustworthy" persons — by 2019, blacklisted individuals had been blocked from
approximately 23 million flights and 5.5 million high-speed train tickets.

The system is not purely punitive: it also rewards compliance. High scores can unlock
access to premium services, reduced deposits, and faster approvals for government services.
This carrot-and-stick architecture makes it more effective — and more deeply entrenched —
than a purely coercive system would be.

### The Digital Yuan (e-CNY)

China launched the world's most advanced Central Bank Digital Currency, the **e-CNY (digital
yuan)**. As of 2025, there are over **225 million personal digital wallets** and cumulative
transactions of approximately **7 trillion RMB ($986 billion)** as of mid-2024. The pilot has
expanded to 17 provinces.

The surveillance implications are structural, not hypothetical: the e-CNY provides the CCP
with real-time financial intelligence on every transaction run through the system. Cross-
referencing transaction patterns with behavioral data — social media, travel, associations —
enables financial profiling at a scale that no Western institution has attempted. The US
National Security Council has formally identified the e-CNY as a national security risk
because it enables surveillance of US entities operating in China.

Geopolitically, China is expanding e-CNY adoption through Belt and Road Initiative countries
and Southeast Asian financial corridors, positioning the digital yuan as a dollar alternative
for bilateral trade — removing reliance on SWIFT and creating financial dependency on Chinese
infrastructure.

### Economic Benefits of the Chinese Model

For the CCP, the surveillance state delivers concrete economic and political outputs:
- **Fraud reduction**: The corporate social credit system reduces counterfeiting, tax fraud,
  and contract default through automated scoring.
- **Behavioral modification at scale**: Automated nudging systems (traffic cameras, social
  scoring) produce compliance without requiring human enforcement officers.
- **Data monetization**: Chinese tech companies — Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, Huawei — operate
  at the intersection of state surveillance and commercial services, with blurred lines
  between private data and state intelligence.
- **State control maintenance**: The ultimate economic benefit, from the CCP's perspective,
  is political stability. The surveillance infrastructure prevented the scale of civil
  unrest that might otherwise accompany rapid industrialization, urbanization, and
  inequality growth.

### Costs and International Backlash

The costs of the Chinese model are severe, unevenly distributed, and increasingly
internationally visible:

**Privacy**: Chinese citizens have no enforceable privacy rights against state surveillance.
The surveillance infrastructure generates comprehensive behavioral dossiers on hundreds of
millions of people without consent, judicial oversight, or right of appeal.

**Dissent suppression**: Xinjiang is the most documented case — but the infrastructure is not
unique to Xinjiang. The same camera networks, face-recognition databases, and predictive
policing tools operate across China. Artists, lawyers, journalists, and activists are tracked,
pre-emptively detained, and silenced. The ICIJ documented China's mechanisms for repressing
dissent across borders, including monitoring Chinese diaspora communities in dozens of countries.

**International backlash**: In 2022, a rights group documented Chinese business associations
in dozens of cities worldwide operating as surveillance hubs. The European Parliament called
for harmonized responses to transnational repression. The US, UK, and EU have imposed
sanctions on Chinese surveillance companies (Hikvision, Dahua, SenseTime, DJI). The EU
has restricted public procurement of Chinese surveillance equipment in sensitive installations.

**Export liability**: Chinese companies have sold AI surveillance systems to at least 80
countries, including states with poor human rights records. This creates reputational and
legal exposure as importing countries face domestic and international criticism.

### Can China's Model Be Replicated Elsewhere?

Technically, yes. Governance-wise, no — unless the destination is already authoritarian.

The hardware and software (cameras, facial recognition, social scoring platforms) are
commercially available and actively exported. China has sold or financed surveillance
technology to at least **80 countries**, with 22 African nations adopting Chinese public
security systems. Russia has already borrowed surveillance tactics pioneered in China.
Experts project **100+ countries** deploying behavioral scoring inspired by the Chinese model
by 2027.

But full replication requires two non-exportable conditions: **centralized state control
over the identity substrate** (the 18-digit national ID linked to everything) and **absence
of independent judiciary or press** to challenge surveillance overreach. Democratic states
can purchase Chinese cameras; they cannot purchase China's governance conditions. This is
why the "China model" spreads most effectively in already-authoritarian states — it
amplifies existing control mechanisms rather than creating them from scratch.

## The Russian Model: Sovereign Internet as State Survival Tool

### What Is Russia's "Sovereign Internet"?

Russia's approach is distinct from China's: China built surveillance into its digital
infrastructure from the ground up. Russia is trying to retrofit isolation onto infrastructure
it did not originally control. The goal is not primarily surveillance but **killswitch
capability**: the ability to disconnect Runet from the global internet in a crisis while
maintaining internal connectivity.

The legal foundation is **Federal Law No. 90-FZ ("Sovereign Internet Law")**, signed May
2019. Its official rationale was protecting Russia from foreign cyberattacks or "external
threats." The actual function is control: the state installed filtering infrastructure on
every major ISP and built a domestic DNS that does not depend on ICANN.

### How It Works Technically

The infrastructure has three layers:

1. **TSPU (Technical Means of Counteracting Threats)**: Deep packet inspection (DPI)
   equipment mandated on all Russian ISP networks. This equipment allows Roskomnadzor to
   monitor, filter, slow, or block any traffic at the packet level. Previously, companies
   spent more than **20 billion rubles ($327 million)** installing TSPU equipment between
   2019 and 2022. By 2030, TSPU capacity is planned to reach 954 Tbps, with 83.7 billion
   rubles allocated to the expansion.

2. **Domestic DNS and routing**: Russia has been building a national DNS infrastructure
   that routes traffic internally without requiring global DNS resolution. In November 2024,
   Russian authorities announced plans to build domestic internet routing validation
   infrastructure, enabling traffic routing that does not cross Russia's borders.

3. **Isolation mode**: Government Decree No. 1667, entering into force March 1, 2026,
   expands the 2019 law: Roskomnadzor, together with the FSB and Ministry of Digital
   Development, now has legal authority to reroute internet traffic in real time and switch
   Runet into full isolation mode — removing external connectivity while maintaining
   internal.

In December 2024, regions in southern Russia (Dagestan, Chechnya, Ingushetia) were
disconnected from the global internet for 24 hours in tests conducted by Roskomnadzor.
The tests demonstrated the infrastructure works; they also demonstrated the civilian
disruption cost.

### Financial Investment

Russia has invested substantially despite economic pressure from sanctions:
- 600 billion rubles (**$660 million**) allocated over five years for the TSPU filtering
  system, per January 2023 Ministry of Digital Development plans.
- Industry cost estimate for TSPU maintenance: **134 billion RUB/year**.
- ISP costs passed to consumers: telecom companies planned 10–15% price increases for
  home internet and mobile service in 2025, citing TSPU compliance costs alongside
  sanctions and the departure of foreign firms.

### Benefits for the Russian State

From the Kremlin's perspective, the RuNet project provides three things:
- **Information control**: DPI enables throttling of platforms (YouTube was throttled to
  below 40 Kbps in 2024 before being partially restored) and complete blocking of VPN
  protocols. Between July and September 2024, approximately 60 VPN apps disappeared from
  the Russian Apple App Store; total unavailable apps reached 98. In 2024–2025, Russia
  made advertising VPN services a criminal offense.
- **Wartime resilience**: The ability to operate communications infrastructure independently
  of global internet during military conflict, foreign cyberattacks, or domestic unrest.
- **Sanctions evasion infrastructure**: Domestic routing reduces dependency on Western
  routing infrastructure and payment systems.

### Costs for Russia

The project has significant unintended costs:
- **Economic damage**: Internet outages and throttling directly harm businesses. The
  70,000+ websites blocked by Roskomnadzor include legitimate foreign services that
  Russian businesses depend on.
- **Innovation suppression**: Isolation from global digital ecosystems reduces access to
  research tools, international collaboration platforms, and cloud services. The departure
  of major Western tech companies accelerated this damage.
- **Technical inefficacy**: Freedom House rated Russia "Not Free" in its 2024 Internet
  Freedom report. Yet VPN usage in Russia has skyrocketed despite restrictions — an
  estimated 20–30% of Russian internet users regularly use VPNs, suggesting the censorship
  creates workaround pressure rather than compliance.
- **Dependency on Chinese substitutes**: Russia has replaced many Western platforms with
  Chinese alternatives, creating a new form of dependency. Experts suggest Russia could
  achieve full digital sovereignty by 2028 — but only by becoming significantly dependent
  on Chinese technology.

## India's Aadhaar: A Middle Path

### What Aadhaar Is

India's Aadhaar system, launched in 2009 and managed by the Unique Identification Authority
of India (UIDAI), is the world's largest biometric digital identity system:

- **1.38+ billion Aadhaar numbers** issued as of July 2024 — covering **96%+ of the
  population**, including some of the world's poorest and most rural populations.
- **2.5 billion monthly authentication transactions** as of 2025.
- **150+ billion cumulative authentications** completed as of April 2025.
- **1.02 billion face authentication transactions** cumulative by early 2025, with 780
  million occurring within the past year alone.

Aadhaar is not just an ID — it is a platform. It underpins Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT),
eliminating middlemen from welfare disbursements and claiming massive corruption reduction.
It enables bank account opening (Jan Dhan), SIM card registration, income tax filing,
passport applications, and increasingly private-sector services.

### Is Aadhaar a Compromise?

Structurally, yes. India chose to build large-scale biometric identity infrastructure without
China's surveillance intent — but also without adequate legal protection infrastructure.

**Where it succeeds**: Coverage and accessibility. Aadhaar reached populations that no
previous identification scheme reached: migrant workers, rural poor, elderly citizens without
prior documentation. The biometric model (iris scans + fingerprints + face recognition)
works for populations with limited literacy. The 150 billion authentication transactions
demonstrate genuine utility at scale.

**Where it fails**: Privacy architecture. The system centralizes biometric data at UIDAI
with limited judicial oversight. In October 2023, Aadhaar and passport numbers of
approximately **850 million Indians leaked** onto the dark web — one of the largest biometric
data breaches in history. A 2018 case demonstrated that a journalist could access Aadhaar
data of over 1 billion Indians for ₹500 (about $6). India's Personal Data Protection law is
still being implemented; comprehensive privacy enforcement did not precede Aadhaar's expansion.

**The January 2025 expansion**: The Indian government began allowing private companies
access to Aadhaar's face recognition infrastructure — extending the surveillance surface to
commercial actors. Civil society warned this creates Aadhaar-as-surveillance-platform risks
that the original design, focused on state welfare delivery, did not contemplate.

### The Middle Path Assessment

Aadhaar demonstrates that universal digital identity coverage is achievable in large,
poor, diverse populations without Chinese-style authoritarian control — but also without
adequate privacy safeguards. It is a pragmatic infrastructure achievement with a governance
deficit. For the EU, the lesson is not "copy Aadhaar" but "universal coverage is technically
achievable; the hard part is the legal architecture around it."

## The EU's Regulatory Approach: Building a Third Way

### The Regulatory Corpus

The EU has built a comprehensive digital governance framework over 2016–2025:

| Regulation | Year | What It Does |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| GDPR | 2018 | Data subject rights, processing obligations, cross-border enforcement |
| eIDAS 2.0 | 2024 | Mandatory EU-wide digital identity wallet by 2026 |
| Digital Services Act | 2023 | Platform liability, content moderation transparency, algorithmic audits |
| Digital Markets Act | 2022 | Gatekeeper obligations, interoperability, self-preferencing prohibition |
| AI Act | 2024 | Risk-based AI classification: prohibited, high-risk, limited-risk |

This corpus exists nowhere else. No other jurisdiction has simultaneously enacted legally
binding cross-border digital identity, mandatory platform risk assessments, and a
comprehensive AI risk classification framework with enforcement teeth.

### The AI Act as the Central Third-Way Instrument

The **AI Act (Regulation EU 2024/1689)**, in force August 2024, is the world's first
comprehensive AI governance law. Its logic is directly opposed to both the US and Chinese
approaches:

**Prohibited AI systems (applicable February 2025)**: The Act bans things China has deployed
at scale and the US has deployed in limited contexts:
- Real-time remote biometric identification of individuals in public spaces by law
  enforcement (with narrow exceptions)
- Untargeted scraping of facial images to create recognition databases — directly
  targeting the Clearview AI model and Chinese-style face-database construction
- Emotion recognition in workplaces and educational institutions
- AI that manipulates persons through subliminal techniques
- Biometric categorization to deduce protected characteristics (race, sexual orientation,
  political opinions, religion)
- Social scoring of natural persons by public authorities — a direct, explicit prohibition
  of the Chinese social credit model

**High-risk systems (applicable August 2026)**: Critical infrastructure, education, employment,
essential services, law enforcement, border management, administration of justice — all
require conformity assessments, human oversight, transparency documentation, and ongoing
monitoring.

**General-Purpose AI model obligations (applicable August 2025)**: Model providers must
document training data, comply with copyright law, and publish summaries of training
content. Systemic-risk models face additional obligations including adversarial testing
and reporting of serious incidents.

### How the EU Approach Differs from US and China

**From the US model**: The EU imposes affirmative obligations on both platforms and AI
systems — something US law does not do. Section 230 gives US platforms near-absolute
immunity; the DSA requires proactive risk management. The US has no federal AI law; the
EU AI Act creates binding obligations across all 27 member states. The "Brussels Effect" —
where EU regulation becomes de facto global standard because companies cannot maintain
separate product lines for EU and non-EU markets — means EU rules export to the world
even without enforcement outside EU borders.

**From the Chinese model**: The EU AI Act explicitly prohibits the surveillance tools China
has deployed nationally. GDPR's data minimization principle is architecturally incompatible
with the comprehensive behavioral profiling China's social credit system requires. The
EUDI Wallet's selective disclosure design (proving "over 18" without revealing birthdate)
is technically designed to minimize, not maximize, data collection — the inverse of Aadhaar's
centralized biometric model and China's identity-linked surveillance.

### Is the EU Building a Genuine Third Way?

The honest answer: partially, and not yet fully.

**What the third way looks like at maturity**: Universal digital identity (EUDI wallet, 80%
EU citizen target by 2030) that is privacy-preserving by design. AI used for efficiency and
public services, not behavioral control. Cross-border data exchange that maintains data
sovereignty. Digital infrastructure that does not depend on US hyperscalers for 65% of cloud
market share. Regulation that forces US and Chinese platforms to meet EU standards when
operating in EU markets.

**What is missing**: Execution infrastructure. The EU has the laws; it does not yet have
the shared services layer. 45% of cross-border EU government services still do not accept
credentials from other member states — a decade after eIDAS was first enacted. The EUDI
wallet deadline has already slipped once. EU cloud dependence remains structurally unchanged.

**The November 2025 "Digital Omnibus" reform** signaled an awareness of this tension: the
Commission proposed amendments to the AI Act to reduce regulatory burden, cutting projected
compliance costs by up to €5 billion by 2029, while broadening regulatory sandboxes. This
is calibration, not retreat — but it indicates that the third-way framework is still being
balanced between rights protection and competitive capacity.

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

The four-model comparison directly validates the zentala.eu manifest's core premises — and
highlights where the argument needs strengthening:

**The US model proves that digital infrastructure built by markets serves markets, not
citizens.** The 6:1 spending ratio favoring private ID verification over Login.gov, the
absence of a federal digital identity 15 years after Estonia deployed one, and the 65%
EU cloud market captured by US companies — all demonstrate that market-driven digital
infrastructure produces winner-takes-all outcomes, not universal public goods. EU digital
transformation cannot be market-led in the US mode; it requires deliberate public investment
in shared infrastructure.

**The Chinese model proves that digital state power is achievable at scale — and defines
what the EU must not build.** Covering 96%+ of a 1.4 billion population with digital ID,
running 2.5 billion authentications monthly, deploying facial recognition at 40 police
bureaus — these are proof-of-concept numbers. The technology works. The question is who
controls it, for what purpose, and with what accountability. The AI Act's explicit
prohibition of social scoring and real-time mass biometric surveillance is not idealism —
it is a legally binding line distinguishing EU digital governance from the Chinese model.

**The Russian model proves that digital isolation is a legitimate state ambition and a
costly failure mode.** The $660 million investment in filtering infrastructure has not
prevented 20–30% of Russians from using VPNs. The attempt to build a sovereign internet
has made Russia more dependent on Chinese technology. For the EU, the lesson is that digital
sovereignty through isolation is a dead end — the alternative is digital sovereignty through
own-infrastructure: EU cloud providers, EU-hosted AI, EU-owned data exchange backbone.

**India's Aadhaar proves that universal coverage is technically achievable without
authoritarianism — but that governance architecture must precede scale.** The 850 million
biometric record breach was a governance failure, not a technical one. The EU's approach
of leading with GDPR (privacy framework), then eIDAS 2.0 (identity infrastructure), then
the AI Act (AI governance) is architecturally correct — but only if execution speed matches
governance ambition.

**The EU's third way is real but incomplete.** The regulatory corpus is in place. The
execution infrastructure is not. The zentala.eu argument — that EU reform requires treating
digital infrastructure as a public good equivalent to roads and railways — is the exact gap
this comparison exposes. Laws that prohibit surveillance are not the same as infrastructure
that enables services. Building the former without the latter leaves the EU with the world's
most sophisticated digital governance framework and an execution record of approximately
45% cross-border service interoperability. That is the gap the manifest's reform agenda
must close.
