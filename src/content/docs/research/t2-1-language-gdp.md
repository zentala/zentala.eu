# Language as Economic Infrastructure

## Summary

Language is not culture — it is infrastructure. When two parties share a language, transaction
costs drop, trust rises, and the scope of market exchange expands. Gravity model research across
81 academic studies consistently finds that a common language increases bilateral trade by 44–65%.
The EU's 24-language operating system costs an estimated €228 billion in foregone goods trade and
€279 billion in services trade every year — roughly €507 billion in total lost economic exchange.
By contrast, the US runs a continental market of 335 million people on a single linguistic stack
and generates startup funding rounds twice the size of Europe's. These findings form the economic
backbone of the manifest's first pillar: language integration is not a cultural concession, it is a
systems upgrade with a measurable ROI.

## Key Findings

1. **Common language boosts bilateral trade by 44%** on average across 81 academic studies
   (Egger & Lassmann, 2012, meta-analysis of 701 estimated effects). Some specifications reach 65%.
2. **EU language fragmentation costs ~€507 billion annually** in foregone trade: €228 billion in
   goods and €279 billion in services, according to EU single market analysis (ECIPE, 2025).
3. **Language barriers in EU e-commerce offset the distance advantage of online trade** — the
   home bias of European online trade is similar in magnitude to offline trade, per ScienceDirect
   research (2016).
4. **EU language technologies alone could boost intra-EU trade by up to €360 billion** — of which
   machine translation alone accounts for €300 billion for SMEs (European Commission study, 2025).
5. **English proficiency yields 21–23% higher wages** for immigrants in the UK (Dustmann & Fabbri,
   UCL). In Switzerland, English-language skills correlate with 12–30% wage premia.
6. **EU SMEs lose an estimated €100 billion in export contracts annually** due to inadequate
   language skills (ELAN report, European Commission, 2006 — the figure has not been revised
   upward in more recent official publications, though the underlying fragmentation has grown
   with EU enlargement).
7. **US Series A rounds average $20M; European Series A averages $10M** — half the ticket size,
   partly attributable to the structurally smaller addressable market per language zone.
8. **EU spends €1 billion/year** on translation and interpretation across 24 official languages —
   an operating cost with no US or Chinese equivalent at comparable scale.

## Trade Effects of Common Language

The gravity model of international trade is the closest thing economics has to a physics equation:
trade between two countries is proportional to their economic mass and inversely proportional to
the distance (and friction) between them. Language is one of the friction variables.

Egger and Lassmann (2012) conducted a meta-analysis of 701 estimated language effects from 81
academic papers. Their central estimate: **a common official or spoken language increases bilateral
trade flows by 44%**. This is not a marginal effect — it is larger than the trade boost from
sharing a land border in most specifications.

Melitz and Toubal (2014, CEPII Working Paper 2012-17) decompose language into three components:
common official language, common spoken language, and a direct communication measure (linguistic
proximity). On a 0–100 communication scale, a one-point increase in common language raises
bilateral trade by 1.15%. They find the total influence of all language channels is **at least
twice that of official language alone** — meaning the commonly reported 44% figure is likely a
lower bound for deeply integrated linguistic communities.

The WTO's own gravity model documentation confirms that language dummy variables are among the
most consistently significant trade predictors across specifications (WTO Advanced Guide to Trade
Policy Analysis, 2016).

**What this means for the EU:** The EU has 27 member states conducting trade across 24 official
languages — 552 language-pair combinations. Every non-shared language pair has a 44%+ trade
deficit baked in by structural linguistics. For goods trade alone, EU single market analysts
(ECIPE, 2025) estimate the shortfall at approximately €228 billion annually; for services €279
billion. The non-tariff barriers between EU member states reach around 44% for goods and 110%
for services according to IMF estimates (2025) — and language is among the stickiest of those
barriers.

## Labor Mobility and Language

Labor mobility is an economic stabilizer: when one region is in recession, workers move to where
jobs exist. The EU's single market legally guarantees free movement of people, but language
fragmentation turns that legal right into a practical near-impossibility for most workers.

**Wage gap by proficiency — UK data:**
Dustmann and Fabbri (UCL, published in IZA Discussion Paper 156) find that **English language
proficiency is associated with approximately 21–23% higher wages** among immigrants in the UK.
The study found that lack of English fluency leads to "substantial earnings losses" and that
English fluency "contributes considerably to reducing earnings differences between white natives
and ethnic minority immigrants."

**Germany data:**
The IMF's 2016 analysis of German labor market outcomes (WP/16/6) found an unconditional wage gap
of 15.3% between native Germans and immigrants — and that having good German writing skills is
necessary but not sufficient: **an immigrant needs to spend 25 years in Germany before earning
wages comparable to a native with equivalent credentials**. Language is not the only friction,
but it is the first one.

**OECD framing:**
The OECD's 2020 report "Skills Proficiency and the Labour Market Outcomes of Migrants" states
directly: "Proficiency in the dominant language in the host country is a key variable facilitating
skill transferability." Language deficiency can "motivate return migration or circular migration,
even of high-skilled migrants" — meaning Europe loses the very talent it legally invited in
because the linguistic stack is fragmented.

The counterfactual is visible in the US: a software engineer moving from Austin to Boston
encounters no language barrier, zero credentialing friction related to language, and can
immediately compete in the local labor market. A Polish software engineer moving from Warsaw to
Munich faces a years-long German learning curve even as their technical skills are immediately
transferable. The market has priced this: EU cross-border labor mobility rates remain well below
US interstate mobility rates.

## Tech Industry: English as Startup Advantage

The tech industry is the clearest case study because it has effectively self-selected a single
language stack: English. Programming languages, documentation, frameworks, GitHub issues, Stack
Overflow, conference talks, VC pitch decks — all run on English. This is not accidental. It is
the protocol decision that enabled a global software industry.

**Funding size gap:**
The average US Series A round in 2023 was $20 million. The average European Series A was $10
million (Sifted, European tech 2024 data). This is a structural gap, not purely a risk appetite
difference. A European startup pitching in its home language has an immediately smaller
addressable market: 40–90 million speakers instead of 1.5 billion English speakers globally.

**The Dutch founder test:**
Sifted's research on English in the European startup ecosystem directly quotes Dutch founders
noting that "operating in any language other than English would have severely limited the
company's ability to trade internationally." All programming metrics, most startup frameworks,
and investor-grade benchmarking already operate in English.

**Investor credibility signal:**
A Harvard study by Laura Huang and colleagues (2020) analyzed over 1,000 early-stage pitches
submitted to a VC firm and found that **the language used by founders had a powerful impact on
investment decisions**. Founders who lack English fluency face a compounded disadvantage: smaller
local market + reduced credibility signal to international capital + lower talent access from
global hiring pools.

**Structural consequence:**
Language barriers remain a key challenge for 38% of businesses hiring in new regions; employers
open to global recruitment are significantly more likely to consider candidates from high-English
hubs (New York, Berlin, San Francisco) than from emerging hubs like Helsinki or Bucharest —
even when technical skills are equivalent. This creates a talent gravity well toward
English-speaking or high-English-proficiency cities, further concentrating tech wealth and
startup activity away from much of Central and Eastern Europe.

## Network Effects and Scale

Language behaves like a communication network: its value is not linear in the number of speakers.
Metcalfe's Law states that the value of a network is proportional to the square of the number of
connected nodes (n²). Applied to language: if English has 1.5 billion speakers and German has 90
million, the *potential connection space* of English is roughly (1500/90)² ≈ 278 times larger.

This is not just theoretical. When the EU Commission study (2025) models the effect of broad
language technology deployment, they find that **machine translation alone could add €300 billion
in intra-EU trade for SMEs** — because it effectively lets every European SME operate in all 24
language markets simultaneously. The technology is a proxy for what common language would achieve
natively.

The network effect of language also applies to knowledge accumulation. English-language research
databases (Google Scholar, PubMed, arXiv, SSRN) contain vastly more indexed papers than any
single European language corpus. A researcher or engineer fluent in English has access to an
information network orders of magnitude larger than one operating only in Polish, Czech, or
Portuguese. This is a compounding productivity advantage: over 20 years, it produces measurably
different innovation outputs.

The EU recognizes this implicitly: 86% of European Commission internal documents are written in
English (European Commission translation statistics). The institution officially maintains 24
languages and internally runs on one. This is the gap the manifest is pointing at.

## Historical Precedent: The US Internal Market

The United States provides the most relevant natural experiment for what a large economy gains
from language unity — not because it was designed that way, but because it happened to consolidate
on English as the dominant working language during its critical industrialization period.

**19th century market integration:**
The NBER analysis of 19th-century US manufacturing shows that economies of scale required
"better communication to run nationwide operations." The expansion of railroad networks reduced
trade costs across the country, but those cost reductions compounded with a shared language that
let firms hire across regions, publish standard contracts, and run centralized accounting. Many
firms grew large by taking advantage of economies of scale enabled by seamless communication —
an advantage unavailable to any contemporary European nation-state of similar geographic size.

**Scale comparison:**
By the early 20th century, the US had built what amounted to a continental economic operating
system: one currency, one legal framework (federal level), and one working language. The EU
has achieved the first two in partial form (Eurozone for 20 members, single market for 27) but
has never attempted the third. The result, over a century, is visible in market capitalization:
the two largest US stock exchanges have combined market cap of $60 trillion (200%+ of US GDP),
while EU stock exchanges total ~$12 trillion (60% of EU GDP). Capital flows to depth, and depth
requires transactability, which requires a common communication protocol.

**The IMF framing:**
The IMF's 2025 analysis "Europe's Integration Imperative" notes that the US is "home to a much
higher number of businesses per capita than the EU — twice as high." Part of this is regulatory.
Part of it is that in the US, a software startup in Austin can instantly address a market of 335
million people with one pitch deck in one language, while a startup in Warsaw needs to build
separate go-to-market strategies for German, French, Spanish, Italian, and Scandinavian markets
before achieving the same addressable scale.

## Cost-Benefit: EU English Bilingualism

The straightforward cost-benefit question: what would it cost to achieve functional English
bilingualism across the EU adult population, and what would it return?

**Current baseline costs:**
- EU translation and interpretation: €1 billion/year (EU Parliament Research Service, 2019).
  This is a direct operating cost of linguistic fragmentation, with no US equivalent.
- ELAN study estimate: €100 billion/year lost in SME export contracts due to language skill
  deficits (European Commission, 2006).
- EU single market foregone goods trade: ~€228 billion/year; services: ~€279 billion/year
  (ECIPE/ECB estimates, 2025).

**Investment cost:**
The UK language skills study (RAND, 2022) found that even under conservative cost assumptions,
promoting a second-language investment program yields a **benefit-to-cost ratio of approximately
2:1 over a 30-year horizon**. That is: £1 invested in language education returns approximately
£2 in economic output over 30 years.

**Return signal from Switzerland:**
Switzerland attributes approximately 9–10% of its GDP to its multilingual heritage and workforce
(Grin, Sfreddo & Vaillancourt, 2010). Switzerland is not an EU member, but it provides evidence
that building functional bilingualism across a national workforce is economically positive at
scale — even in a small economy where the absolute effect is more visible.

**Post-Brexit note:**
After the UK left the EU in 2020, English officially lost its primary native-language sponsorship
within EU institutions (Ireland and Malta are the remaining English-speaking members, with small
populations). Despite this, English remains the dominant internal working language at the
Commission: 86% of internal documents, the overwhelming majority of inter-institutional
communication, and the primary language of the EU's digital single market interfaces. The de
facto integration has already happened. The de jure integration (formal EU-wide bilingualism
policy) would legitimize and accelerate what is already occurring at street level among
younger Europeans.

**Conservative estimate of net gain:**
If common English reduced the EU's intra-market trade friction by even 20% (well below what
gravity models predict for full language unity), the gain would exceed €100 billion annually —
enough to fund the EU's entire translation apparatus 100 times over, every year.

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

The data above builds a single coherent argument: **language fragmentation is a systems-level
inefficiency that compounds across every economic domain simultaneously.**

The manifest's claim that "Europe is a legacy OS" maps directly onto this data:

- **Trade kernel**: EU internal market runs with a 44–65% trade friction tax on every
  cross-language transaction. This is not a tariff — it is a latency built into the protocol.
- **Labor kernel**: EU free movement of workers is a legal API with no runtime support. Language
  barriers are the missing runtime. The wage gap persists for 25 years post-migration.
- **Capital kernel**: EU startup funding rounds are structurally half the size of US rounds
  because addressable market is denominated per language, not per continent.
- **Knowledge kernel**: EU researchers, engineers, and entrepreneurs accessing
  English-first knowledge networks compound their productivity advantage over non-English peers
  over years and decades.

The European Commission implicitly knows this: 86% of its documents are in English. The EU
language technology study acknowledges it: €360 billion in unrealized trade. The ELAN report
named it: €100 billion in lost SME export contracts.

What the manifest adds is the framing: this is not a language problem, it is an infrastructure
problem. The EU built a single physical market (goods movement), a partial monetary union
(Eurozone), and a partial regulatory union (single market directives) — but left the
communication protocol layer unaddressed. The refactoring cost is real. The ROI on doing it is
larger.
