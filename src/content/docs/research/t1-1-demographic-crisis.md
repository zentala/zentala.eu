# Europe's Demographic Crisis: Data, Projections, and Policy Implications

## Summary

Europe is aging faster than at any point in modern history, and the numbers are not projections — they are already locked in. As of 2024, the EU's old-age dependency ratio stands at 37.0%: fewer than three working-age adults support every person aged 65 or older. By 2050, this ratio will approach 57%, meaning roughly one-and-a-half workers per retiree. Fertility rates across the continent hit a historic low of 1.34 births per woman in 2024 — Spain reached 1.10, Poland 1.10 as well, Italy 1.18. Post-accession emigration has stripped Poland, Romania, and Bulgaria of millions of working-age citizens. Pension systems designed for postwar baby-boom demographics are structurally underfunded for this new reality. Japan and South Korea, roughly one to two decades ahead of Europe on the aging curve, show what inaction looks like: Japan's public social spending on the elderly ballooned from 6% of national income in the 1970s to an expected 28% in 2025; South Korea projects healthcare and pension costs reaching 17.4% of GDP by 2060. The EU's 2024 Ageing Report estimates that without reform, aging-related fiscal costs in the euro area will rise from 25.1% to 26.5% of GDP by 2070. The IMF calculates that reform — specifically linking retirement ages to life expectancy and improving labor market participation — could reduce fiscal pressure in 2060 by around 4 percentage points of GDP. The window for low-cost intervention is now; every decade of delay compounds the required adjustment.

---

## Key Findings

- The EU old-age dependency ratio rose from 26.8% in 2004 to 37.0% in 2024 — a 10.2 pp increase in 20 years. The EUROPOP2025 projection puts this at 56.7% by 2050.
- All five major EU economies analyzed (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Poland) record fertility rates below 1.65 in 2024. The EU average of 1.34 is the lowest on record.
- Poland's population may fall from 37.4 million today to 28.4–29.4 million by 2060 under low-fertility scenarios; its old-age dependency ratio is projected to reach 56 by mid-century and 63% by 2100 — the highest in the EU.
- Romania has lost over 4 million people since 1990 (a 17% decline); at least 2 million, roughly 20% of the pre-emigration labor force, now live abroad. Bulgaria's population has fallen 27.5% since 1987.
- Current statutory pension contribution rates range from 15.45% of wages in France to 33.5% in Italy — already high, with projected increases as the dependency ratio worsens.
- The European Commission's 2024 Ageing Report projects EU-wide pension expenditure reaching 12.1% of GDP in 2050; Spain alone faces a 3.4 pp increase above the EU average.
- Japan's trajectory is the most cautionary precedent: aging-related social spending tripled from 6% to 18% of national income between the early 1970s and 1992, and is projected at ~28% by 2025. Gross public debt reached 245% of GDP by 2022.
- The IMF finds that structural labor market and retirement reforms could offset 60–75% of the demographically-driven GDP growth slowdown.

## 1. Old-Age Dependency Ratio: Current State and Projections

### Definition

The old-age dependency ratio (OADR) measures people aged 65+ as a percentage of those aged 20–64 (working age). A ratio of 37% means there are 37 retirees for every 100 working-age adults, or roughly 2.7 workers per retiree.

### EU-wide baseline (2024–2025)

- **2004**: 26.8%
- **2015**: 29.0%
- **2024**: 37.0% (Eurostat, January 2024)
- **2025**: 34.5% — Eurostat uses a slightly different base calculation; the January 2024 figure of 37.0% reflects the 65+/20–64 ratio while the 34.5% figure reflects the 65+/15–64 definition. Both trend lines are identical.

Source: [Eurostat — Old-age dependency growing across EU regions (October 2025)](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251001-2); [Eurostat — Population structure and ageing](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Population_structure_and_ageing)

### Country-level data (2024)

| Country | OADR 2024 (approx.) | Notes |
|---------|---------------------|-------|
| Italy | ~39% | Highest in EU among large economies |
| Germany | ~35% | Set to rise sharply as baby boomers retire |
| France | ~34–35% | Relatively younger profile, higher fertility |
| Spain | ~33% | Late baby boom delays peak to 2045–2050 |
| Poland | ~33–34% | Rapid acceleration underway; 5.6 pp increase 2015–2025 (fastest in EU) |
| EU average | 37.0% | Across all 27 member states |

Source: [Eurostat data browser tps00198](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/tps00198/default/table?lang=en); [Germany data — European Pensions (2024)](https://www.europeanpensions.net/ep/Germanys-old-age-dependency-ratio-set-to-increase-to-51-per-cent-by-2050.php)

### Projections: 2030–2050

The EUROPOP2025 projections (released April 2026) and the earlier EUROPOP2023 series provide the following trajectory:

| Year | EU-wide OADR |
|------|-------------|
| 2022 | ~34% |
| 2025 | 34.5% |
| 2030 | ~40% (estimated) |
| 2050 | 56.7% |
| 2100 | 61.9% |

Source: [Eurostat — Population projections in the EU (EUROPOP2025)](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Population_projections_in_the_EU); [ECB Economic Bulletin focus box — EUROPOP2023 implications](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2023/html/ecb.ebbox202303_08~47bea3ce51.en.html)

**Germany specifically**: OADR of ~34% in 2024 is projected to reach **51%** by 2050. If contributor rates within the working-age population stagnate, the actual retiree-to-active-contributor ratio could reach 70% by 2050.
Source: [European Pensions — Germany's OADR to reach 51% by 2050](https://www.europeanpensions.net/ep/Germanys-old-age-dependency-ratio-set-to-increase-to-51-per-cent-by-2050.php)

**Spain specifically**: OADR of ~33 in 2022, projected to reach **64.5** by 2054 — nearly doubling in three decades.
Source: [CaixaBank Research — Impact of ageing on Spain's public finances](https://www.caixabankresearch.com/en/economics-markets/labour-market-demographics/impact-ageing-public-finances-major-challenge-spain)

**Poland specifically**: OADR projected to reach **56** by mid-century and **63%** by 2100 — the highest in the EU by end of century.
Source: [Notes From Poland — Poland's population may fall further than forecast (Nov 2025)](https://notesfrompoland.com/2025/11/07/polands-population-may-fall-further-than-forecast-as-fertility-rate-hits-record-low-warns-stats-agency/)

**Working-age population (EU-wide, 20–64):**
- 2009: 272 million (peak)
- 2022: 265 million
- 2030: ~258 million
- 2040: ~247 million
- 2050: ~236 million

That is a 29-million reduction between 2022 and 2050 — an 11% contraction of the EU's productive labor base.
Source: [EU Employment and Social Developments in Europe (ESDE) 2023](https://op.europa.eu/webpub/empl/esde-2023/chapters/chapter-2-4-1.html)

## 2. Fertility Collapse: Country-by-Country Data

### Replacement rate context

The replacement fertility rate — the level needed to keep a population stable without immigration — is 2.1 live births per woman. No EU member state currently meets this threshold. The EU average has been below 2.1 since the mid-1970s.

### 2024 fertility rates

| Country | TFR 2024 | Notes |
|---------|----------|-------|
| EU average | **1.34** | Historic low, per Eurostat March 2026 release |
| France | **1.61** | Highest among the five large economies analyzed |
| Germany | **~1.36** | Slightly above EU average |
| Italy | **1.18** | Down from 1.21 in 2023 |
| Poland | **1.10** | "Lowest level on record in Poland and one of the lowest in the world" (GUS) |
| Spain | **1.10** | Second-lowest in the EU; lowest among large economies |
| South Korea (for comparison) | **0.74** | Extreme outlier, but leading indicator of urban fertility collapse |

Source: [Eurostat — EU fertility rate at 1.34 in 2024 (March 2026)](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20260306-1); [Euronews — Europe's fertility rates keep falling (March 2026)](https://www.euronews.com/health/2026/03/14/europes-fertility-rates-keep-falling-which-countries-have-the-highest-rates-and-biggest-dr); [GUS — Poland's fertility hits record low (Nov 2025)](https://notesfrompoland.com/2025/11/07/polands-population-may-fall-further-than-forecast-as-fertility-rate-hits-record-low-warns-stats-agency/)

### Historical trend

EU live births declined from a peak of 6.8 million in 1964 to 3.55 million in 2024 — a halving of annual births over 60 years. The total fertility rate declined correspondingly from approximately 2.5+ in the 1960s to 1.34 today.

### Structural causes

Delayed first births are a consistent pattern across the continent. In Poland, the average age at first childbirth rose from 23 to 28 between 1995 and 2021. In Romania, mean maternal age at first birth increased from 22.3 years (1989) to 27.5 years (2023). Each year of delayed first birth reduces completed family size and compresses the generational renewal cycle.

## 3. Emigration Losses: Poland, Romania, Bulgaria

### Poland

Poland joined the EU in 2004. Post-accession emigration peaked between 2004 and 2007, when over 2 million Poles moved to the UK, Germany, Ireland, and elsewhere.

**Current diaspora (2024):** UN DESA estimates 4.57 million Polish citizens lived abroad in 2024, including over 1.8 million in Germany and 900,000+ in the UK. Statistics Poland tracks approximately 1.555 million in temporary residence abroad as of end-2023.

**Demographic impact:** Emigrants are disproportionately young and working-age. The birth rate in Poland fell by approximately 10% as a direct result of working-age emigration, with further decline of 10% projected to 2035. Roughly **9% of Poland's working-age population was abroad as of 2020** (up from 4% in 2011).

**Recent reversal:** Poland's booming tech and manufacturing sectors have attracted return migration since approximately 2015, partially reversing the brain drain. Net migration turned moderately positive in recent years. However, the fertility damage from the emigration wave is irreversible on a 20-year timescale.

Source: [Wikipedia — Migrations from Poland since EU accession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrations_from_Poland_since_accession_to_the_European_Union); [Migration Policy Institute — Poland country profile](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/country-resource/poland)

### Romania

Romania's demographic collapse is the most severe in the EU.

- **1990 population**: 23.2 million
- **2021 population**: ~19 million
- **Loss**: approximately 4.2 million people (17% of population), averaging 130,000 per year
- **2050 projection**: ~16 million (further 16% decline from today)

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs estimates up to **5.7 million Romanians live abroad** (approximately 29% of the 2024 resident population of 19.6 million). The World Bank's conservative estimate is 4 million. Over **2 million — roughly 20% of the pre-emigration labor force** — are considered long-term or permanent emigrants. The largest communities are in Italy (1.1 million+), Spain, the UK, and Germany.

Between 2011 and 2020, the share of Romanians living in other EU member states rose from 14% to 23% of the total population.

**Recovery stalled**: After a positive migration balance from 2012–2022, Romania returned to net emigration in 2023 and 2024. The fertility rate of 1.7, while above Poland's, remains well below replacement, and deaths exceed births annually.

Source: [OSW — Thirty years of crisis: Romania's demographic situation (March 2025)](https://www.osw.waw.pl/en/publikacje/osw-commentary/2025-03-21/thirty-years-crisis-romanias-demographic-situation); [Romania Insider — Romania's population drops by over 4 million in 35 years](https://www.romania-insider.com/romania-population-decline-35-years-2026); [Agenzia Nova — Romania: Record emigration in 2024](https://www.agenzianova.com/en/news/romania-nel-2024-record-di-emigrazioni-popolazione-in-calo-di-41-milioni-in-35-anni/)

### Bulgaria

Bulgaria's decline is proportionally the most extreme of the three.

- **1987 population**: 8.976 million (peak)
- **2024 population**: 6.44 million
- **Loss**: 2.54 million, a **27.5% reduction** over 37 years
- **2050 projection**: under 5.5 million

Approximately **1.7 million Bulgarians lived abroad in 2020** (UN DESA). The share of Bulgarians living in other EU member states rose from 7% to 11% between 2011 and 2020. Mass emigration is directed primarily to Germany (16.9% of emigrants), the UK (13.9%), and France (8.9%).

**Economic impact**: Bulgaria's demographic decline has reduced GDP growth by an average of approximately **0.9 percentage points per year since 2009**. Nearly all of Bulgaria and Romania are classified by the European Commission as in a "talent development trap" — territories unable to compensate for working-population loss through any internal mechanism.

Sectoral damage: Bulgaria lost approximately 12% of its trained doctors between 2000 and 2015 through emigration.

Source: [OSW — A dwindling nation: Bulgaria on the brink of demographic collapse (December 2024)](https://www.osw.waw.pl/en/publikacje/osw-commentary/2024-12-16/a-dwindling-nation-bulgaria-brink-a-demographic-collapse); [China-CEE Institute — Bulgaria: Socio-Economic Dimensions of Demographic Crisis (November 2025)](https://china-cee.eu/2025/11/19/bulgaria-monthly-briefing-socio-economic-dimensions-of-the-demographic-crisis-in-bulgaria/)

### Aggregate fiscal pressure

The dependency ratio effect alone will push pension costs up by **6.2 percentage points of GDP** across the euro area by 2070, partially offset by structural reforms. Without reform:

- EU average pension system deficit: 2.5% of GDP today → **4% of GDP** within three decades.
- Total aging-related expenditure (pensions + healthcare + long-term care): 25.1% of GDP in 2022 → **26.5% of GDP** by 2070 in the euro area.

Source: [OECD — Rapidly ageing populations will continue to put pressure on pension systems (November 2025)](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/11/rapidly-ageing-populations-will-continue-to-put-pressure-on-pension-systems.html); [ECB Economic Bulletin 2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2024/html/ecb.ebbox202405_08~5f9531042f.en.html)

## 5. Precedent: Japan and South Korea

### Why these comparisons matter

Japan's OADR today exceeds 50%. South Korea's fertility rate reached 0.74 in 2024 — the lowest ever recorded in any country. Both nations are roughly 10–25 years ahead of Europe's aging trajectory and provide the most detailed empirical data on what happens when demographic transition is not managed proactively.

### Japan

- **Current demographics (2025)**: ~29% of the population aged 65+; over 25.9% aged 65+; one-third projected to be 65+ by 2050.
- **Population trajectory**: 16% decline projected to 2050, to approximately 104.9 million from 125 million.
- **Aging-related social spending**: 6% of national income in the early 1970s → 18% in 1992 → projected ~28% by 2025. This near-tripling over 50 years was not a policy choice — it was the arithmetic of demographics meeting a fixed benefit structure.
- **Pension expenditure (2022)**: 9.3% of GDP, vs. 7.7% OECD average.
- **Fiscal consequence**: Gross public debt reached 245% of GDP in 2022, the highest in the world. It is projected to decline only slightly.
- **Labor supply**: Aging has reduced Japan's growth by an estimated 0.7% annually, with 0.9% annual drag projected going forward.
- **Long-term care**: Users of Japan's long-term care insurance tripled from 1.49 million (2000) to 4.74 million (2018).

Source: [IMF — Finance & Development: Japan's Fiscal Challenge (2001)](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2001/03/muhleise.htm); [Aging of Japan — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aging_of_Japan); [World Economic Forum — Japan's ageing society (September 2023)](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2023/09/elderly-oldest-population-world-japan/)

**Japan's policy lesson**: The "macroeconomic slide" pension reform (2004) introduced automatic benefit adjustments tied to demographic change. The mechanism has been triggered only five times since implementation, because each trigger is politically painful. Proactive demographic management outperforms reactive adjustment.

### South Korea

- **Fertility rate (2024)**: 0.74 — the lowest ever measured in a sovereign nation. This is not a projection. Seoul's TFR is even lower, at approximately 0.55.
- **Aging speed**: South Korea's OADR is rising faster than any other OECD country. By 2025, over 20% of the population is 65+. By 2050, this is projected at **40%** — potentially the highest share of any country in the world.
- **Population trajectory**: UN projections suggest Korea's population (currently ~52 million) could decline by 17 million (33%) by 2070.
- **Economic cost of aging**: Healthcare, long-term care, and pension costs are projected to more than double to **17.4% of GDP by 2060** (from roughly 8% today).
- **GDP risk**: South Korea's GDP growth rate of ~2% is at risk of contraction by 2040 as the working-age population shrinks. BCG and OECD models indicate the shrinking workforce directly reduces productivity-adjusted output.
- **Pension system stress**: Studies project required pension contribution rates rising from 9.0% to **31.3%** if benefits remain unchanged — a 22-percentage-point increase that would consume nearly a third of gross wages.

Source: [BCG — The Economic Impact of South Korea's Low Birth Rate (2024)](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/economic-impact-of-south-koreas-low-birth-rate); [KDI — Addressing the Demographic Decline in South Korea](https://kdijep.org/v.48/1/5/Addressing+the+Demographic+Decline+in+South+Korea); [OECD Ecoscope — Addressing Korea's Fertility Crisis (October 2024)](https://oecdecoscope.blog/2024/10/18/addressing-koreas-fertility-crisis/); [CNBC — South Korea's birth rate collapse (September 2025)](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/27/south-koreas-birth-rate-collapse-threatens-growth.html)

**European mirror**: Europe's fertility rates are generally higher than South Korea's but are tracking the same direction. Spain at 1.10 and Poland at 1.10 in 2024 are approaching Korean levels of the early 2000s. Without intervention, Europe enters the same trajectory with a 15-year lag.

## 6. Cost of Inaction vs. ROI of Reform

### Macro cost of aging without reform

The IMF's April 2025 World Economic Outlook (Chapter 2: "The Rise of the Silver Economy") provides the most recent comprehensive modeling:

- **Aggregate fiscal pressure**: Fiscal pressure will increase in the average OECD country by nearly **6.25 percentage points of GDP** between 2024 and 2060; demographic aging accounts for more than 40% of that increase.
- **GDP growth drag**: Europe's working-age population will fall by over 20% between 2015 and 2055, with a direct corresponding decline in GDP growth trajectory.
- **Pension deficit**: Average EU pension system deficit moves from 2.5% of GDP today to 4% of GDP within three decades without reform.
- **Potential output**: EU potential GDP growth is projected at 1.1% per year on average (2022–2070) in a baseline scenario; a less dynamic productivity scenario brings this to 0.8% by the early 2030s.

Source: [IMF F&D — Sustaining Growth in an Aging World (June 2025)](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2025/06/sustaining-growth-in-an-aging-world-bertrand-gruss); [OECD ECOSCOPE — The fiscal impact of population ageing (November 2025)](https://oecdecoscope.blog/2025/11/07/the-fiscal-impact-of-population-ageing-how-can-we-afford-getting-older/)

### Reform ROI: what works and what it buys

The IMF and OECD modeling literature converges on several reform levers with quantified returns:

**1. Retirement age reform (link to life expectancy)**
- Reducing early exit pathways and linking retirement ages to two-thirds of projected life expectancy gains, combined with labor market reforms, lowers fiscal pressure in 2060 by approximately **4 percentage points of GDP** for the average country.
- This single reform lever offsets approximately two-thirds of the aging-driven fiscal deterioration.

**2. Labor market participation (women, older workers, migrants)**
- Simulations show that labor supply and productivity policies could boost global annual output growth by approximately **0.6 percentage points over the next 25 years** — offsetting nearly three-quarters of the estimated demographic growth drag.

**3. Structural reforms (overall package)**
- IMF estimates that structural reforms could offset as much as **60% of the demographically-driven slowdown in real GDP growth**.
- Reversal of past pension reforms (as occurred in several EU states post-2015) has the inverse effect: the IMF documented that reform reversals "generate substantial adverse macroeconomic costs and could pose challenges for fiscal sustainability."

### The cost of delay: 10 years

No single study provides a clean "reform now vs. reform in 10 years" comparison. However, the structural arithmetic is clear:

- **Locked-in cohorts**: The working-age population of 2035–2045 is already born. Fertility interventions today will not affect the labor supply until approximately 2042. Every year of delay in structural reform is a year of accumulating pension deficit and a year closer to the 2040–2055 peak stress period.
- **Debt compounding**: A pension deficit of 2.5% of GDP, unaddressed for 10 years, adds 25 percentage points to the public debt-to-GDP ratio (at zero interest — higher with real interest rates). Given the EU's average debt ratio already above 80% of GDP, this trajectory leads directly to debt sustainability crises.
- **Reform reversal penalty**: The IMF's framework for assessing pension reform reversal costs shows that undoing a decade of reform progress (as happened in France, Poland, and Spain between 2014 and 2022) costs approximately 1.5–2 times as much to restore as the original reform saved.
- **Japan benchmark**: Japan's fiscal space deterioration from the 1990s to today — from manageable debt to 245% of GDP — took roughly 30 years. Europe is entering that zone roughly 20–30 years behind Japan. The window for early-course correction remains open, but it is narrowing.

Source: [IMF — Pension Reforms in Europe: How Far Have We Come (2021)](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/DP/2021/English/PREHFWCGEA.ashx); [Bruegel — How demographic change will hit debt sustainability in EU countries](https://www.bruegel.org/policy-brief/how-demographic-change-will-hit-debt-sustainability-european-union-countries); [OECD — The fiscal impact of population ageing (2025)](https://oecdecoscope.blog/2025/11/07/the-fiscal-impact-of-population-ageing-how-can-we-afford-getting-older/)

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

This data provides the quantitative foundation for several arguments central to the zentala.eu manifesto:

**1. The urgency argument holds.** The OADR will pass 56% by 2050 regardless of what Europe does in the next decade on fertility. The working-age population decline is locked in. The debate is not about whether Europe will face a demographic stress — it is about whether Europe will be institutionally prepared to manage it.

**2. Eastern Europe faces disproportionate exposure.** Poland, Romania, and Bulgaria are being hit by a simultaneous triple shock: low fertility, net emigration, and fast-rising OADRs. Poland's projected OADR of 63% by 2100 (highest in EU) is a different order of problem than Germany's projected 54% or France's lower long-term trajectory. EU-level reform mechanisms that work for western demographics may be inadequate for eastern ones.

**3. The language integration and labor mobility argument is strengthened.** The eastern-to-western emigration drain is partly driven by wage and opportunity differentials. Policies that reduce those differentials — common labor markets, common social standards, mutual recognition of credentials — could help retain working-age populations in the countries that most need them. This is the demographic case for deeper integration.

**4. Digital government reduces the fixed overhead of serving an aging population.** Each 65+ person requires approximately 3–4 government service touchpoints per year (healthcare navigation, pension administration, social services). At 56% OADR, this is double the current administrative load. Digital services reduce per-capita service cost and are the only scalable response given fiscal constraints.

**5. Japan's precedent suggests reform momentum must precede peak stress.** Japan attempted pension reform in 2004 — at the point when the aging curve was already steep. Europe is earlier on that curve. The difference is meaningful: reforms enacted now (retirement age normalization, productivity investment, labor mobility) compound over 20–25 years before the 2045–2055 peak. Reforms enacted in 2040 do not.

**6. The reform ROI is strongly positive.** The IMF's 4 pp of GDP fiscal savings from linked-retirement-age reform is not a theoretical ceiling — it is a baseline. Combined with labor market activation (immigration integration, female participation, longer careers), the total potential offset is 60–75% of aging's GDP drag. The cost of these reforms is primarily political, not fiscal.
