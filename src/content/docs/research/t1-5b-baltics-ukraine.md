# Baltics & Ukraine as Security Allies: Tech Readiness, Threat Perception, and EU Integration

## Summary

Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and Ukraine occupy a unique position in the EU reform argument: they combine the highest security motivation of any European nations with real digital capacity to execute on that argument. All three Baltic states spend more on defence as a share of GDP than any NATO member except Poland — committing 5% of GDP from 2026 — precisely because they share a land or sea border with Russia. At the same time, Estonia is the EU's most advanced digital government, Lithuania has the fastest-growing tech ecosystem in Central and Eastern Europe, and Ukraine — under fire since 2022 — built one of the most digitally resilient governments in the world. Ukraine's tech sector, generating $6.2 billion in export revenue in 2024 despite the war, is already 4.4% of GDP and growing. These countries do not need to be convinced that the EU's digital future matters. Their security calculus makes them structural allies for any EU digitalization agenda: the deeper their integration into European digital infrastructure, the harder they become to isolate or coerce. The zentala.eu argument — that EU integration, digital sovereignty, and linguistic cohesion are security assets — lands here with zero persuasion overhead.

## Key Findings

- All three Baltic states registered over 70% of citizens viewing Russia as a direct security threat in 2024; Lithuanian surveys put defence as the number one political priority at 60% — the highest in the EU.
- Estonia ranked 2nd in the EU in 2022 DESI for digital public services; its ICT sector contributes 8% of GDP and it produces the most unicorns per capita in Europe (10 unicorns total).
- Lithuania's startup ecosystem grew 7x from 2019 to 2024, reaching €16 billion combined enterprise value; ICT contributes 5.1% of GDP with 60,300 employees.
- Latvia's ICT sector exceeds 6% of GDP; ICT exports reached ~€1.3 billion in 2023; over 7,000 ICT companies are active.
- All three Baltics disconnected from Russia's IPS/UPS electricity grid on 8 February 2025, completing their energy independence pivot started in 2022 when they became the first EU countries to ban all Russian gas imports.
- Ukraine's Diia platform has 21.8 million users (64% of citizens use government e-services), ranks 5th globally in the UN online service index, and was included in the EU eGovernment benchmark study starting 2024.
- Ukraine's IT sector employs 238,000–346,000 people in-country; IT services are now 43% of total Ukrainian exports as of 2025.
- Baltic states pledged 5% of GDP for defence starting 2026, with Estonia committing 5.4% for 2026–2029 — the highest defence/GDP ratio in the EU.
- 84% of Ukrainians supported EU membership in January 2024; Baltic EU membership support stands at 62–79% (Lithuania highest at 79%).

## Security Threat Perception

### Estonia

Estonia has the most explicit institutional threat assessment in Europe. Its Foreign Intelligence Service (VALISLUUREAMET) publishes an annual report naming Russia as the primary threat. The 2025 report, released in February 2025, maintained this assessment. In 2024, 95% of Estonian nationals opposed Russia's aggression against Ukraine; 61% of Estonians favoured providing military aid to Ukraine directly. Estonia's security reasoning is also structural: the 2022 Russia-linked sabotage of the Estlink-2 cable between Finland and Estonia demonstrated that hybrid attacks on civilian infrastructure are active, not theoretical.

Eurobarometer data from late 2023 showed 78% of EU citizens overall viewing Russia as a threat; among Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and Poland, these figures were consistently higher than the EU average.

### Latvia

Latvia's State Security Service (VDD) 2024 Annual Report identified Russia as the primary threat to national security. Latvians showed strong support for increasing defence budgets and for actively supporting Ukraine. Latvia has a significant Russian-speaking minority (~25% of population), which creates both a domestic security dimension and a hybrid-influence vector that Latvia's security services monitor continuously.

### Lithuania

Lithuania showed the strongest securitization of EU identity in Eurobarometer surveys: 60% of Lithuanians cited defence and security as the top priority ahead of the 2024 European Parliament elections — the highest of any EU member state. 88% of Lithuanians viewed Russia as a threat in Eurobarometer data. Lithuanians supported financing military equipment for Ukraine at 76% in favour — higher than the EU average. Lithuania's Ministry of National Defence found that the willingness to engage in armed resistance grew from 53% to higher levels post-2022.

### Ukraine

Ukraine's relationship with Russia as a security threat is existential, not merely perceptual. Since the 2014 annexation of Crimea and the 2022 full-scale invasion, no survey data is needed to establish threat perception: the country is under active attack. The war has paradoxically accelerated Ukraine's digital integration with the EU. Ukraine ranked 30th globally in the 2024 UN E-Government Development Index — up 16 positions since 2022 — partly because wartime conditions forced faster adoption of digital services. Approximately 7.5 million Ukrainians are displaced across Europe, creating a distributed diaspora with direct experience of EU digital infrastructure.

## Tech Sectors: Size and Talent

### Estonia

Estonia's ICT sector is the engine of its economy: approximately 8% of GDP, with the sector generating €1.69 billion in turnover in H1 2024 alone (11% year-on-year growth). Estonia has produced 10 unicorns — Skype (2003, built by Estonian engineers), Wise, Bolt (€1.4 billion turnover in 2024), Pipedrive (€282 million turnover in 2024), Veriff, Glia, and others. With 1.3 million people, Estonia has the highest number of unicorns per capita in Europe. ICT specialists are 6.7% of the workforce, versus an EU average of 4.8%. ICT graduates are 9.6% of all graduates — the highest in the EU. Estonia leads Europe in venture capital as a share of GDP (1.17% invested in startups over the past decade).

The e-Residency programme, launched in 2014, had attracted over 100,000 e-residents from 180+ countries by 2024, creating a globally distributed Estonian business community without requiring physical presence.

Sources: [Estonia leads Europe in unicorns](https://investinestonia.com/estonia-leads-europe-in-startups-unicorns-and-investments-per-capita/); [Startup Estonia 2024 report](https://startupestonia.ee/estonias-startup-sector-in-2024-growth-challenges-and-key-insights/)

### Latvia

Latvia's ICT sector exceeds 6% of GDP; over 7,000 ICT companies are active, concentrating in software, telecom, cloud services, AI, and cybersecurity. Riga is the regional hub. ICT exports reached approximately €1.3 billion in 2023, making Latvia the leading Baltic ICT exporter by absolute export volume. Notable companies include Printful (print-on-demand, global operations), Mintos (peer-to-peer lending), and Nordigen (open banking, acquired by GoCardless). Over 600 active startups operate in the country as of 2024. Latvia's per-capita startup value (€1,600) is lower than Lithuania's, but its absolute ICT export performance is strong.

Latvia experienced a 7% increase in startup funding in 2024, against a European average decline of 5%.

Sources: [Latvia ICT sector](https://investinlatvia.org/en/key-sectors/sectors/ICT); [Baltic Startup Funding H1 2024](https://www.changeventures.com/baltic-startup-funding-report)

### Lithuania

Lithuania's startup ecosystem is the growth story of the Baltic region. Between 2019 and 2024, the combined enterprise value of Lithuanian tech companies grew 7x, reaching €16 billion. ICT contributes 5.1% of GDP with 60,300 employees; ICT exports climbed from 1.89% to 3.27% of GDP between 2020 and 2024. Lithuania has produced three unicorns — Vinted (€5 billion valuation, leading European second-hand marketplace), Nord Security (€2.7 billion, VPN and cybersecurity), and Baltic Classifieds Group (€2.0 billion). Over 1,000 active tech companies employ 18,000+ specialists. Lithuanian startups raised €600 million in 2024, with Vinted alone raising €340 million. Lithuania's aspiration: ICT as the number one driver of economic growth, reaching 20% of GDP by 2030.

Sources: [Lithuania tech ecosystem](https://lithuania.lt/news/business-and-innovations-in-lithuania/lithuanias-economic-growth-driven-by-ict-and-startups/); [Lithuanian Startup Boom](https://unicorns.lt/en/news/lithuanian-startup-boom-ecosystem-now-valued-16-billion)

### Ukraine

Ukraine's tech sector is one of the most significant in Eastern Europe — and it has been growing under wartime conditions. In 2024, Ukrainian IT companies generated $6.2 billion in revenue, with IT services constituting 38% of total services exports and 43% of total exports as of 2025. The sector employs 238,000–346,000 people, with approximately 2,100–2,300 IT companies active. The sector contributes 4.4% to GDP. Ukraine's IT exports go primarily to the US ($2.4 billion), UK ($0.6 billion), and Malta/EU ($0.5 billion). The sector demonstrated resilience: despite the 2022 invasion, IT was the only major export sector that grew immediately post-invasion.

Ukraine has approximately 30% more tech talent than Poland by absolute numbers, much of it distributed across the EU diaspora since 2022.

Sources: [TechUkraine 2024 report](https://techukraine.org/2024/12/05/ukrainian-tech-industry-shows-resilience-in-the-face-of-war-it-research-ukraine-2024/); [Ukraine IT Exports](https://digitalstate.gov.ua/news/it-outsourcing/it-posluhy-staly-holovnym-drayverom-eksportu-ukrayiny-u-2025-rotsi)

## EU Digital Integration Status

### Baltic states (EU members since 2004)

All three Baltic states are full participants in eIDAS — the EU regulation establishing mutual recognition of digital identities and electronic signatures. Key facts:

- Estonia's qualified electronic signatures have been legally equivalent to handwritten signatures since 2000. The e-ID system (mandatory since 2002) is the template for EU-wide digital identity under eIDAS 2.0.
- Latvia's eParaksts Mobile and Lithuania's Mobile-ID and Smart-ID are all recognized as state-approved tools at the European level. All three countries appear on the EU Trusted List of Qualified Trust Service Providers.
- Under eIDAS, a document digitally signed in Estonia can be recognized and validated in Latvia, Lithuania, or any other EU member state without additional formality.
- Cross-border digital identity exchange between Nordic and Baltic countries is covered by a dedicated interoperability framework (Nordic-Baltic Baseline Study 2021, updated 2024).
- Estonia co-founded the Nordic Institute for Interoperability Solutions (NIIS) in 2017 with Finland, which now manages X-Road's global community. X-Road deployments: 25+ countries worldwide.

Ukraine is not yet an EU member, but:
- From 2024, Ukraine was formally included in the EU eGovernment Benchmark study — the first step toward DESI-comparable metrics.
- Ukraine's Diia was acknowledged as a trendsetter in open data maturity, exceeding EU27 average in the 2022 Open Data Maturity Assessment.
- Ukraine's digital infrastructure was migrated to European cloud providers in the first days of the 2022 invasion, making EU data centres the operational backbone of the Ukrainian state. This is already partial de facto EU digital integration.

Sources: [eIDAS cross-border Baltic guide](https://www.agrello.io/en/blog/cross-border-digital-identity-and-e-signing-in-the-baltic-states-a-practical-guide-for-smes/); [Ukraine eGovernment inclusion](https://eufordigital.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/EU4D_DESI-Accelerator_Report-on-DESI-recommendations.pdf)

## Russian Energy and Tech Dependency

### Energy: now largely eliminated

The Baltic states' exit from Russian energy dependency was the fastest and most complete of any EU member group:

- **Gas**: Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia were the first EU countries to ban all Russian gas imports — including LNG — in April 2022, within two months of the full-scale invasion. Lithuania's Klaipėda LNG terminal (operational since 2014) enabled this pivot.
- **Electricity**: The Baltics remained physically linked to Russia's IPS/UPS power grid until 8 February 2025, when Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania simultaneously disconnected and synchronized with the European synchronous area. This ended the last significant Russian infrastructure leverage over the three states.
- **New interconnections**: Estlink 1 and 2 (Estonia–Finland), NordBalt (Lithuania–Sweden), LitPol Link (Lithuania–Poland), and the Gas Interconnection Poland–Lithuania (GIPL, operational May 2022) now route all Baltic energy flows through European networks.

### Tech and software dependencies: reduced but not zero

For the Baltic states, Russian software dependencies were minimal before 2022 and have been systematically eliminated since. Kaspersky Lab (Russian antivirus) was specifically banned from government systems across all three countries.

For Ukraine, the dependency picture was more complex:
- Ukraine's tech sector was export-oriented toward Western markets (US, UK, EU), not Russia-oriented. Russian software was being phased out before 2022; the invasion accelerated this to near-zero.
- Russia's 2022 invasion severed virtually all commercial tech ties between Ukraine and Russia. Over 1,000 Western tech companies curtailed operations in Russia, leaving a vacuum filled by Chinese tech. Ukraine simultaneously deepened its integration with Western tech platforms.
- Russia itself faces an 80% Russian-software mandate by 2030 (Putin decree, May 2024) — a sign of how completely it has been cut off from Western technology.

Sources: [Baltic energy independence](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/new-atlanticist/baltic-states-unplug-from-russias-power-grid-but-moscow-still-looms-over-critical-infrastructure/); [Al Jazeera Baltic grid disconnect 2025](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/10/why-have-baltic-states-unplugged-from-russias-electricity-grid)

## Interest in EU Integration: Survey Data

### Baltic states

Eurobarometer Standard 102 (Autumn 2024) data shows:
- **Lithuania**: 79% view EU membership as "good" — the strongest in the Baltic group.
- **Estonia**: 67% view EU membership positively.
- **Latvia**: 62% view EU membership positively.

On EU common defence and security policy, Baltic support stands at 81–89% in favour — well above the EU average. This is the highest category of EU integration by public support in all three countries. The inference: Baltic citizens want EU integration most where it has security value.

Post-2022 Eurobarometer data shows more favourable Baltic attitudes toward EU enlargement, specifically for Ukraine's accession — consistent with the security logic: enlarging the EU eastward expands the security perimeter.

Defence and security top the priority list for Lithuanian citizens at 60% — the highest share of any EU member state ahead of the 2024 EP elections (Eurobarometer 2024).

### Ukraine

Survey data on EU membership support in Ukraine:
- **January 2024** (Razumkov Centre, n=2,000+): 84% in favour of EU membership.
- **April 2024** (IRI survey): 77% support EU membership.
- **July 2025** (IRI, n=2,000+): 75% support EU membership.
- **December 2025** (latest available): 59% support EU integration — lower than 2024, likely reflecting war fatigue and slower-than-expected accession progress.

Geographic split in Ukraine: western oblasts 79% in favour, central 62%, southern/eastern 38–44.5%. Support is highest where Russian influence was historically lowest.

In EU member states, Eurobarometer 2024 found 60%+ of EU citizens in favour of Ukraine's eventual EU membership — Ukraine was the most favoured candidate country in this survey.

Sources: [IRI Ukraine EU support](https://www.iri.org/news/iri-poll-shows-ukrainians-overwhelmingly-support-european-union-and-nato-membership/); [Eurobarometer Baltic EU support](https://www.euronews.com/2024/03/04/from-gains-to-grumbles-euroscepticism-in-the-baltic-states); [EU Neighbours East Ukraine survey 2024](https://euneighbourseast.eu/news/publications/annual-survey-2024-ukraine/)

## NATO Membership and Tech Sovereignty

### Baltic states: NATO since 2004

Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania joined NATO in 2004, the same year as EU accession. This dual alignment was a deliberate strategic choice: neither organization alone was considered sufficient.

NATO membership has directly shaped Baltic tech infrastructure decisions. Key examples:

- **NATO CCDCOE in Tallinn**: Established in 2008 directly in response to Russia's 2007 cyberattack on Estonia — the first state-level cyberattack on a NATO ally. Estonia hosts NATO's premier cyber defence centre, turning vulnerability into expertise.
- **Baltic Sentry (2025)**: NATO launched a Baltic Sea critical infrastructure protection initiative following the December 2024 sabotage of the Estlink-2 cable, attributed to Russia's shadow fleet. The initiative includes underwater surveillance, AI monitoring, and cyber-physical defences.
- **Tech sovereignty concern**: Baltic states have been the most vocal advocates within NATO and the EU for reducing dependency on non-Western technology in critical infrastructure. This includes banning Kaspersky, scrutinizing Huawei in 5G rollouts, and advocating for EU cloud sovereignty.

Defence spending commitments:
- **Estonia**: 3.43% of GDP in 2024; committing to 5.4% from 2026 to 2029.
- **Latvia**: 3.15% of GDP in 2024; target 4% in 2025, 5% in 2028.
- **Lithuania**: 2.85% of GDP in 2024; over 5% from 2026.
- **Poland** (adjacent ally): 4.48% of GDP in 2024 — highest in NATO after the US.

The top four per-GDP NATO defence spenders — Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia — are all countries directly bordering Russia or its closest ally Belarus. This is the geographic calculus making itself legible in budget numbers.

### Ukraine: NATO candidate + already integrated operationally

Ukraine is not a NATO member but operates alongside NATO in practice. Since 2022:
- Starlink (US) provided battlefield internet connectivity, demonstrating tech sovereignty's military dimension.
- Western AI and ISR analytics firms gave Ukraine real-time intelligence capabilities the state could not build alone.
- Ukrainian government data migrated to European cloud providers in February 2022, making EU cloud infrastructure operationally critical.

Ukraine's aspiration to NATO membership (supported by 77% in 2024 surveys) is partly a tech sovereignty argument: membership would accelerate interoperability, reduce Chinese tech dependency pressure, and give Ukraine access to NATO's cyber and signals infrastructure.

Sources: [NATO CCDCOE](https://ccdcoe.org); [Baltic Sentry NATO](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/cipr/items/888102/en); [Baltic 5% GDP pledge](https://www.kaitseministeerium.ee/en/news/baltic-defence-ministers-reiterated-their-commitment-increase-defence-spending-5-gdp-2026)

## Strategic Value of Digital/Linguistic EU Integration

### For Estonia

Estonia has already exported its model: X-Road runs in 25+ countries; the e-Governance Academy trains foreign governments; the e-Residency programme attracted 100,000+ digital residents from 180 countries. For Estonia, deeper EU digital integration means its model becomes the EU standard — which is commercially and strategically valuable. Estonia's interest is in EU-wide adoption of X-Road-style infrastructure, eIDAS 2.0 rollout, and EU Digital Wallet becoming as ubiquitous as Estonia's own ID card.

The language dimension matters more than it appears: Estonia's relatively high English proficiency (EF EPI: Estonia ranks ~30th globally) has enabled it to punch above its weight in international tech. An EU shift toward English as a common operational language would benefit Estonia disproportionately — both its tech sector and its government's ability to export the Estonian model.

### For Latvia

Latvia's interests in digital integration focus on infrastructure: completing VHCN coverage, bridging the digital skills gap, and accessing EU digital single market revenue. Latvia's ICT export strength (€1.3 billion) would grow with fewer cross-border barriers. Linguistic integration matters because Latvia has the largest Russian-speaking minority of the three Baltic states (~25%) — EU-wide English-as-lingua-franca reduces Russian language as a vector for influence.

### For Lithuania

Lithuania's tech sector is the fastest-growing in CEE; EU digital single market access is already core to that growth. Vinted's €5 billion valuation is essentially a bet that European e-commerce integration deepens. Nord Security's cybersecurity business depends on EU trust infrastructure. Deeper EU digital policy coordination on cybersecurity — where Lithuania has both expertise and exposure — is a direct national interest.

### For Ukraine

Ukraine's digital integration with the EU has already happened in practice during the war. The strategic question is formalizing it. For Ukraine, EU digital integration means:
1. Diia interoperable with EU digital identity (eIDAS) → Ukrainian diaspora in the EU can use their documents abroad.
2. Ukrainian IT companies gain EU data residency status → easier for EU clients to hire Ukrainian developers.
3. Ukraine's e-government model transfers to EU accession requirements → accelerates the political track.
4. EU cloud infrastructure as permanent backup → resilience against future Russian cyberattacks.

Ukraine's 21.8 million Diia users and wartime innovation (services launched in 3 weeks or less) make it a living laboratory for digital government under adversity. The EU's digital reform argument benefits from Ukraine's existence: the country demonstrates that digital government is not a peacetime luxury but a wartime resilience requirement.

## Security vs. Economic Opportunity: The Shared Interest Matrix

| Country | Security motivation | Economic motivation | Integration urgency |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| Estonia | High: 2007 cyberattack, 2024 Estlink sabotage; NATO CCDCOE host | High: tech exports, e-residency, X-Road licensing | Medium — already deeply integrated |
| Latvia | Very high: direct Russia border, Russian minority vulnerability | Medium: ICT exports growing, skills gap limiting | High — needs infrastructure investment |
| Lithuania | Highest: Suwalki Gap exposure, Kaliningrad adjacency; 60% prioritize security | High: fastest-growing CEE tech ecosystem, Vinted/Nord growth trajectory | High — security + growth both push integration |
| Ukraine | Existential: active war since 2022 | Very high: IT sector survival depends on EU market access; diaspora remittances | Very high — war makes EU integration urgent, not optional |

The matrix reveals a consistent pattern: the countries closest to Russia have the strongest simultaneous pull from both security and economic motives. They are not choosing between security and prosperity — they are discovering they are the same thing.

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

The zentala.eu manifest argues that Europe needs to "refactor" using three pillars: Language (English as lingua franca), Digitalization (Estonia-model e-government), and Robotization (care robots, logistics automation). The Baltic states and Ukraine validate each pillar from a security-first perspective:

**Language pillar**: Baltic states' relatively high English proficiency enables their tech export success. Ukraine's IT sector operates primarily in English, serving US/UK clients. An EU common operational language would deepen this integration, reduce Russian-language influence vectors in Latvia, and enable Ukrainian IT talent to integrate into EU projects without language renegotiation.

**Digitalization pillar**: Estonia is the proof-of-concept. Latvia and Lithuania demonstrate what the middle of the EU digital spectrum looks like — technically capable but with infrastructure gaps that EU investment could close. Ukraine demonstrates that digital government is a security asset: its Diia platform survived Russia's invasion, its government data survived because it was in EU cloud, its military uses digital tools because analogue was not fast enough.

**Security argument that the manifest adds**: The Baltic states and Ukraine answer the question "who benefits most from a digitally integrated EU?" not with abstract economic theory but with lived survival calculations. When Lithuania's defence minister says 5% of GDP for defence, the implicit complement is: EU digital infrastructure is the civilian layer of the same security architecture.

The "natural allies" framing in the manifest is correct and supported by data. These countries are not following the EU digital agenda because Brussels recommended it. They are pursuing digital integration because it makes them harder to isolate, harder to coerce, and more capable of responding to Russian hybrid warfare. That alignment of interest makes them the most credible voices for EU digital reform within the EU system itself.
