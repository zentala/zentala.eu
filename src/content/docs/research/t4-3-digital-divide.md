# Digital Divide and Rural Access: Ensuring No One Is Left Behind

## Summary

Universal digital government is the right direction — but it has a structural problem: it works for the majority while leaving out those who most need government services. In 2024, 40% of EU citizens still lack basic digital skills. Rural households with internet access lag 4+ percentage points behind urban ones. In eight EU member states, rural broadband coverage falls below 30% of households. The elderly, low-income, and non-EU language speakers face compound exclusion — not just connectivity gaps but skills gaps, device gaps, and service design that was never built for them. The EU's Digital Decade targets require €200 billion to close the connectivity gap by 2030, and current trajectories put the gigabit-everywhere goal at risk. Meanwhile, governments offering only digital channels are explicitly leaving out people aged 65+, who use online government services at half the rate of the general population. The conclusion for EU reform is uncomfortable but necessary: digital-first policy is not the same as digital-only policy. Hybrid delivery — digital plus phone, kiosk, and paper fallback — is not a concession to failure. It is the architecture of inclusion. The cost of building it is far lower than the political and social cost of abandoning 100 million Europeans.

---

## Key Findings

- **Rural broadband gap is structural:** EU27 NGA (Next Generation Access, ≥30 Mbps) coverage nationally reaches 92.9%, but rural coverage lags significantly — 10 member states have fewer than 90% of rural households online, with Portugal (79.9%) and Greece (78.5%) at the bottom (Eurostat, 2024).
- **€200 billion investment gap:** Achieving the Digital Decade's gigabit-everywhere goal requires at least €174–200 billion in additional investment. Private capital will not reach rural areas without public subsidy. The EU's CEF Digital and ERDF funds are significant but not sufficient at current scale.
- **40% lack basic digital skills:** As of 2026 Eurostat release, 40% of EU citizens cannot perform basic digital activities. Only 31.8% of Romanians have basic skills; the Netherlands leads at 84%. The EU target is 80% by 2030 — implying 180+ million people need skills upgrades in five years.
- **Elderly are the largest excluded group:** Internet use among 65+ reaches 76% in the EU, but only 49.9% use online government services (vs. 70% of the general population). Non-use of e-government among the elderly doubles the burden on in-person channels.
- **65% of government websites fail accessibility:** In the 2024 eGovernment Benchmark, 65% of EU public sector websites failed to meet all tested WCAG criteria. The European Accessibility Act mandated compliance deadline was June 2025.
- **Digital channels are 20–50x cheaper than alternatives:** UK Government Digital Service data shows online transactions cost on average 20x less than phone, 30x less than postal, and 50x less than face-to-face. But cheap for government is not accessible for everyone.
- **Hybrid delivery works:** France's 2,750 Maison France Services branches, staffed by 7,100 advisors, provide in-person digital mediation across all regions. Denmark exempts digitally excluded citizens by law from mandatory digital self-service since May 2023.
- **2030 targets are at risk:** EU gigabit coverage stands at 82.5%, 5G rural coverage at 51%. With 55% of rural households still unserved by advanced networks and 9% without any fixed network, hitting 100% gigabit by 2030 requires an investment pace that does not match current deployment rates.

## 1. Broadband Coverage in EU Rural Areas

### The State of Rural Connectivity (2024)

The European Commission's Broadband Coverage in Europe 2024 report, covering 31 countries (EU27 plus Norway, Iceland, Switzerland, UK) with data as of June 2024, provides the most recent picture of rural connectivity.

**National vs. rural NGA coverage (≥30 Mbps fixed access):**
- EU27 total fixed NGA coverage: **92.9%** (up from 91.5% in 2023)
- EU27 rural NGA coverage lags significantly — the report documents persistent urban-rural gaps across all technology types

**Household internet access by area type (Eurostat, 2023):**
- Cities: **94.9%** of households have internet access
- Towns and suburbs: **92.7%**
- Rural areas: **90.5%**

Ten EU member states have fewer than 90% of rural households online. The worst performers:
- Greece: **78.5%** rural household internet access
- Portugal: **79.9%** rural household internet access
- Luxembourg and the Netherlands lead at **98.3%** rural access

**Gigabit (VHCN) coverage in rural areas:**
- EU national average VHCN: **78.8%** of households
- Greece minimum: **38.4%** VHCN coverage nationally
- Malta maximum: **100%** VHCN coverage nationally
- Rural FTTP/B (full fibre): approximately **48%** of rural inhabitants reached, versus 64% nationally — a 16-percentage-point gap

**Notable outlier:** Romania has the highest rural FTTH/B coverage at **92%**, a consequence of aggressive network deployment in the 2010s driven partly by EU ERDF funding. Denmark reaches 90% rural FTTH. The Netherlands and Latvia reach 83%. By contrast, western European countries with older DSL infrastructure (Germany, Austria) show worse rural fibre penetration than eastern EU states with greenfield deployments.

**5G rural coverage:**
- EU national 5G population coverage: **89.3%**
- Rural 5G: **51%** — a 38-point gap that represents the most acute connectivity divide currently active

### What 30 Mbps Actually Means in 2025

The 30 Mbps NGA threshold was set by the EU's Digital Agenda for Europe in 2010 as a 2020 target. It is now a floor, not an aspiration. Video conferencing, e-health applications, and government identity verification services routinely require more. The Digital Decade's 2030 target has moved to gigabit everywhere — meaning even "connected" rural households on 30-100 Mbps DSL are below the functional threshold for next-decade digital services.

## 2. The Investment Gap: What It Costs to Close the Digital Divide

### The €200 Billion Deficit

The European Commission's study on investment and funding needs for the Digital Decade connectivity targets is explicit: **at least €174–200 billion in additional investment is needed** to achieve the 2030 goals.

The breakdown:
- Fixed gigabit (FTTP) to 100% of households: **~€114 billion**
- Full 5G service across all populated areas: **~€33.5 billion**
- Base investment total: **€148 billion**
- Additional costs (transport infrastructure, upgrade paths): **€26–79 billion**
- Total range: **€174–200+ billion**

This is not the total cost of deployment — it is the gap above what the private market will deliver. Commercial operators concentrate investment in dense, profitable areas. Rural zones, by definition, have lower population density and lower expected revenue per connection. Without public subsidy, the market will not close the gap.

### EU Funding Mechanisms (Current State)

**Connecting Europe Facility (CEF) Digital:**
CEF Digital supports very high-capacity network deployment, cross-border backbone infrastructure, and digital connectivity for key socioeconomic drivers (schools, hospitals, local administrations). The 2021–2027 CEF Digital budget is approximately **€2.07 billion** — significant but a fraction of the €200 billion gap.

**European Regional Development Fund (ERDF):**
ERDF is the primary vehicle for rural broadband investment in lower-income regions. A documented example: Grand Est region in France received **€33 million ERDF** through regional operational programmes for fibre deployment across a territory where 89% of communes have fewer than 3,500 inhabitants.

Across the 2021–2027 programming period, ERDF and Cohesion Fund allocations for digital infrastructure total several tens of billions across all member states — but the distribution is uneven, and administrative capacity to absorb funds varies dramatically.

**Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF):**
Post-COVID recovery plans include connectivity investment across member states. A European Fund for rural connectivity reached over **one million premises passed with fibre in rural and semi-rural Europe** by 2023, demonstrating that concentrated investment produces measurable results.

**The funding reality:** EU public funds cover a meaningful but insufficient share of the investment gap. The math requires private capital participation at scale, which requires regulatory certainty, return on investment timelines extending beyond typical infrastructure cycles, and in some cases direct state aid — all of which create their own friction.

## 3. Device Access and Digital Exclusion by Group

### Eurostat Data: Who Is Offline and Why

**Overall internet access (2025, Eurostat):**
- 94% of EU households have internet access
- 94% of individuals report using the internet in the past three months
- Approximately 4% have not used the internet in the past year

These aggregate numbers obscure severe within-group disparities.

**By age (2024 Eurostat ICT household survey):**
- Ages 16–24: **98.6%** internet use
- Ages 65+: **76.3%** internet use — a 22-point gap

For government digital services specifically:
- General population using online government services: **70.0%**
- Population aged 65+: **49.9%** — a 20-point gap that doubles in-person demand

For online educational services:
- General population: **33.4%**
- Ages 65+: **12.2%** — meaning elderly use this service at one-third the general rate

**By income (2022 Eurostat data):**
- 2.4% of EU inhabitants (≈10.8 million people) cannot afford internet access
- Among those at risk of poverty: **7.6%** cannot afford internet access — three times the overall rate

**By region (Eurostat, 2023):**
- Urban internet access: 82–99% (range across EU regions)
- Rural internet access: 62–99% — a much wider range indicating uneven development
- Highest regional share of people who never used internet (aged 16–74): **17.3% in Kentriki Elláda (Greece)**
- EU27 average for never-internet-users: **6.0%** of population aged 16–74

**By non-EU citizen status:**
- Non-EU citizens at risk of poverty or social exclusion: **43.8%** (vs. 18.5% for nationals in their own country)
- Non-EU citizens aged 65+: **53.8%** — the highest risk group among any demographic combination measured

**Device access:**
Mobile is the dominant access method — 9 in 10 internet users go online via smartphone. This matters for service design: government websites optimized for desktop often fail on mobile. In lower-income households, a smartphone is frequently the only connected device — and smartphone-only users encounter more barriers with form-heavy government portals designed for keyboard and mouse.

**Digital skills deficit:**
As of the April 2026 Eurostat release, **40% of EU citizens lack basic digital skills**. The Digital Decade target is 80% of adults with basic skills by 2030. Current top performers: Netherlands (84%), Ireland (83%), Denmark and Finland (81%). Worst performers: Romania (31.8%), Bulgaria (approximately 40%). This skills gap is structurally linked to: age (correlation with 65+ non-use), income (low-income populations have less exposure), and education level.

## 4. Accessibility Standards for Digital Government

### The Legal Framework

**EU Web Accessibility Directive (2016/2102):** Requires public sector websites and mobile applications to comply with **WCAG 2.1 Level AA**. Covers government websites, healthcare portals, educational platforms, and other public services. Member states must submit monitoring reports every three years (first due 2021, second due December 2024).

**European Accessibility Act (EAA):** Extends requirements beyond public sector to private sector digital services. Implementation deadline: **June 28, 2025**. Covers e-commerce, banking, transport, and communication services.

### Compliance Reality (2024)

The 2024 eGovernment Benchmark (covering EU27 member states) used automated accessibility testing (Deque's axe extension) against selected WCAG criteria:

- **65% of EU government websites fail** to meet all eight tested WCAG criteria
- EU27 overall eGovernment performance: **76 points out of 100** — a five-point improvement over two years
- Top performers: Malta (97/100), Estonia (92/100), Luxembourg and Iceland (90/100)
- 86% of government services are now available online — but "available" does not mean "accessible"

An independent comparison of e-government website accessibility across Europe (2024) found an EU average WCAG compliance metric of approximately **77%** — meaning roughly one in four public-sector websites has accessibility barriers for people with visual, motor, or cognitive impairments.

**Elderly usability gap:** The WCAG compliance rate measures technical accessibility (screen reader compatibility, color contrast, form labeling). It does not measure cognitive accessibility — the ease with which people with limited digital literacy can understand and use a service. Seniors with basic internet skills routinely encounter government websites that are technically compliant but functionally unusable: complex navigation, jargon-heavy language, multi-step processes with no clear progress indicators, and session timeouts that punish slower users.

The EU Fundamental Rights Agency (FRA) published a 2023 report on older people's digital rights specifically documenting that accessibility barriers in public digital services are not primarily technical — they are design and language barriers that automatic WCAG testing does not capture.

## 5. Cost of Hybrid Service Delivery

### Channel Cost Benchmarks

The most comprehensive public dataset on government transaction costs by channel comes from the UK Government Digital Service (GDS), which measured costs across thousands of government transactions:

| Channel | Average cost per transaction |
|---------|------------------------------|
| Online (digital) | **~£0.15–£0.20** |
| Telephone | **~£3–8** (varies significantly by service type) |
| Postal | **£6–9** |
| Face-to-face / in-person | **£8–30** |

A specific documented example: booking a driving test costs £0.22 online, £4.11 by telephone, £6.62 by post.

The UK analysis concluded that online transactions cost approximately **20x less than telephone, 30x less than postal, and 50x less than face-to-face** on average. Moving all offline transactions (250+ million annually) online was projected to save **£1.7–1.8 billion per year**.

### Why Hybrid Is Still Necessary

These cost differentials create a strong institutional incentive to go digital-only. But the numbers contain a systematic error: they measure cost per completed transaction, not cost per person successfully served. If a digital-only service excludes 20% of potential users who then receive no service or receive it through a crisis-intervention pathway (emergency services, hospital, social worker), the "savings" are illusory — costs are transferred, not eliminated.

**Hybrid delivery staffing models:**

France's Maison France Services network provides a documented example of structured hybrid delivery:
- **2,750 branches** distributed across all French regions
- **7,100 advisors** providing in-person digital assistance
- Services covered include tax, benefits, housing, health, and identity documentation
- Target population includes elderly, low-digital-literacy, and rural citizens
- Partially funded through France's **€1.8 billion National Digital Inclusion Plan**

An independent estimate for France: digital illiteracy (illectronisme) affects **15–20% of the French population**, with one in two seniors lacking full digital access, and 40% of French people having difficulty mobilizing digital skills for internet use. The Maison France Services network is a direct response to this structural exclusion.

**Phone support model:** Denmark's Agency for Digital Government operates a structured phone support model for citizens who struggle with digital self-service. A May 2023 law created a **legal right to be exempt from mandatory digital self-service** for citizens who cannot manage it — the first EU member state to formalize this exemption in law. Approximately **23% of Danes aged 15–89** (1.14 million people) self-report as "digitally limited."

**Local Government ROI (UK):** A Local Government Association funded program of 21 digital inclusion projects across 23 councils found a **5:1 return on investment** — £300,000 investment generated £1.5 million in estimated benefits. Scaled to all UK local councils: potential £25 million in benefits. The mechanism: helping users self-serve rather than use costly in-person channels, but doing so through supported digital access rather than pure digital mandate.

## 6. Successful Models for Inclusive Digital Services

### France: Maison France Services

**Model:** A national network of proximity service branches staffed by trained advisors who help citizens access digital public services in person. Not a replacement for digital — a guided pathway to digital.

**Scale:** 2,750 branches, 7,100 advisors (2024). Present in all regions, with deliberate deployment bias toward rural and small-town locations.

**Services covered:** Administrative services from 11 major public institutions including tax authority, health insurance (CPAM), pension funds, postal services, national employment agency.

**Results:** The 2024 digital inclusion observatory for France (Labo Société Numérique) documented: 66% of structures now operating in rural territories (up from 61% in 2022), 60% operating through "permanences" in non-central locations (up from 47% in 2022). Mobile accompaniment (visiting citizens at home or in mobile vans) serves an additional 36% of locations.

### Denmark: Legal Exemption and Agency for Digital Government

**Model:** Denmark has among the highest digital public service usage rates in the EU (consistent DESI top-3 ranking). But it also acknowledged that the digitally limited cannot be forced to comply with digital mandates.

**Key policy:** May 2023 law allowing legal exemption from mandatory digital self-service for those unable to comply. This formally creates a two-track system: digital-first for the majority, assisted or paper-based for those who need it.

**Support infrastructure:** Municipalities provide help with MitID (national digital identity) and digital government access. A 2023 Government Network for Digital Inclusion was established with 70 public authorities sharing knowledge on inclusion.

### Romania: Rural FTTH as Infrastructure Inclusion

**Counterintuitive model:** Romania, one of the EU's least digitally skilled countries, has one of its highest rural fibre coverage rates (92% rural FTTH). The infrastructure investment preceded the skills gap closing — a deliberate bet that connectivity is a prerequisite, not a reward.

**Lesson:** Connectivity and skills are separate problems requiring separate interventions. High-speed infrastructure in rural Romania did not automatically produce high digital skills, but it created the precondition for skills development programs to work.

### Belgium and Portugal: Digital Mediator Networks

Belgium (Wallonia and Brussels) has invested in "digital mediators" — trained individuals embedded in social services, libraries, and community centers who provide one-to-one digital support. This model targets the intersection of digital exclusion and social exclusion, reaching people who would not self-identify as needing digital help but who present at social services for benefits, housing, or health support.

Portugal, despite its low rural internet access (79.9%), has run digital inclusion programs through its network of "Espaços Internet" (Internet Spaces) — public access points with free broadband and support staff — since the early 2000s. The model predates EU Digital Decade policy by two decades, demonstrating that public access infrastructure works when it is combined with human support.

## 7. Who Is Most at Risk of Digital Exclusion

### Compounding Exclusion Factors

Digital exclusion is rarely caused by a single barrier. The most excluded groups face compound barriers: no device, no connectivity, no skills, services designed for other users, and no alternative pathway when digital fails.

**Elderly (65+):**
- Internet use: 76% (vs. 98% for 16–24 age group)
- Government service online use: 49.9% (vs. 70% general population)
- E-government non-use is not primarily a connectivity problem for this group — most elderly have broadband. It is a skills and design problem.
- In absolute numbers: the EU has approximately 100 million people aged 65+. Even at 76% internet use, that represents roughly 24 million elderly people offline.

**Low-income households:**
- 2.4% of EU inhabitants cannot afford internet — approximately 10.8 million people
- Among those at risk of poverty: 7.6% cannot afford access (≈16 million in the EU's at-risk-of-poverty population)
- Low-income users more likely to be smartphone-only, limiting access to form-heavy government portals

**Rural populations:**
- 10 EU member states have below 90% rural household internet access
- Rural users face both connectivity gaps and lower density of in-person support infrastructure
- Rural areas have fewer libraries, social service offices, and digital mediators per capita

**Non-EU language speakers and migrants:**
- 43.8% of non-EU citizens in the EU face risk of poverty or social exclusion
- Language barriers compound digital barriers: government websites are often available only in national language(s), and machine translation of legal forms introduces errors
- Non-EU citizens aged 65+: 53.8% face poverty/social exclusion risk — the most severe risk profile of any demographic combination

**People with disabilities:**
- 65% of EU government websites fail to meet all tested WCAG criteria
- Visual, motor, and cognitive impairments create distinct accessibility barriers that overlap poorly with generic "elderly" or "low-income" categories
- The European Accessibility Act (June 2025 deadline) is the primary legal mechanism — but compliance enforcement varies

**Combined profile of maximum exclusion:**
A 70-year-old non-EU citizen, living in rural Greece or Portugal, with a primary school education and a low income, faces all barriers simultaneously: poor rural broadband, no device, no digital skills, inaccessible websites (even if connected), language barriers, and likely no physical alternative service point within reasonable distance. This is not a theoretical profile — it describes a measurable portion of the EU's southern and eastern rural populations.

## 8. Timeline to Universal Access: Digital Decade 2030 Targets

### The Official Targets

The EU's Digital Decade Policy Programme 2030 sets connectivity targets:

1. **All European households** covered by a gigabit network (1 Gbps) by 2030
2. **All populated areas** covered by 5G by 2030
3. **80% of adults** with basic digital skills by 2030
4. **80% of public services** available online by 2030

### Current Progress (as of 2024–2025 data)

| Target | 2030 Goal | Current Status | Gap |
|--------|-----------|----------------|-----|
| Gigabit household coverage | 100% | 82.5% | 17.5 pp |
| 5G national coverage | 100% | 89.3% | 10.7 pp |
| 5G rural coverage | 100% | 51% | 49 pp |
| Basic digital skills (adults) | 80% | ~60% | 20 pp |
| Online public services | 80% | 86% | **Achieved** |

### Realistic Assessment: Which Targets Are Achievable

**Online public services (86%):** Already exceeded. But this measures availability, not accessibility or usability. 65% of those services fail WCAG testing. Achievement on paper, not in practice for excluded populations.

**5G national coverage (89.3%):** On track for near-100% with continued private investment in urban areas.

**5G rural coverage (51%):** Critically behind. Rural 5G deployment requires either public subsidy or satellite alternatives (Starlink-type services, EU-backed IRIS² system). A 49-percentage-point gap with 5 years remaining is not closeable through market forces alone.

**Gigabit household coverage (82.5%):** 55% of rural households are still not served by any advanced network, and 9% have no fixed network at all. Achieving 100% gigabit by 2030 requires both unprecedented investment pace and resolution of the "final mile" problem in the most dispersed rural communities. The EC's own investment gap analysis puts this at risk without >€114 billion in additional fibre deployment spending.

**Basic digital skills (60% → 80%):** Requires 180+ million Europeans to develop new skills in five years. Historical trajectory suggests this is achievable only with major national adult education programs, employer training mandates, and sustained funding — none of which are currently in place at the required scale. Romania (31.8%), Bulgaria, and several other member states need to roughly double their skilled-population share in five years.

**The honest projection:** Not all targets will be met by 2030. The most at-risk targets are rural 5G coverage and basic digital skills in lower-performing member states. The most likely outcome is a two-speed EU where digitally advanced member states (Estonia, Denmark, Netherlands, Finland) achieve near-full digital inclusion while less developed regions (parts of Greece, Romania, Bulgaria, Portugal) remain 10–20 percentage points behind on multiple indicators simultaneously.

## Implications for EU Reform Argument

The zentala.eu manifest argues for universal digital government — a single European digital identity, cross-border services, government that works for Europeans as Europeans. This research does not invalidate that argument. It forces it to be more specific.

**The access problem is real but solvable.** Broadband infrastructure gaps in 2030 will be smaller than in 2024. The €200 billion investment gap is large, but it is bounded and addressable through a combination of public funding, state aid for rural networks, and satellite connectivity as a last-mile fallback. The question is political will and funding sequencing, not technical feasibility.

**The skills problem is the harder one.** Digital infrastructure arrives faster than digital literacy. The EU can wire every village in Romania by 2030 — but if 68% of Romanian adults still lack basic digital skills, the wire achieves little. Skills development requires sustained, locally adapted, human-intensive programs. EU funding can support this, but it cannot substitute for national education systems that have failed to prioritize digital literacy for decades.

**Digital-only is a political non-starter.** Any EU-wide digital government initiative that treats in-person, phone, and paper channels as failures or inefficiencies will face legitimate resistance from member states with large elderly populations, language minority groups, and rural constituencies. The French and Danish models show the path: digital-first as the default, with structured and legally protected alternatives for those who need them. This is not a retreat from the digital agenda — it is the political engineering that makes the digital agenda durable.

**The cost argument runs both ways.** Online transactions cost 20–50x less than alternatives. But excluding 100 million Europeans from digital services does not eliminate their service needs — it transfers costs to crisis and emergency systems, which are far more expensive. The fiscal argument for digital inclusion is not charity; it is efficiency.

**Accessibility is the invisible blocker.** 65% of government websites failing WCAG criteria is not a minor compliance issue. It means the entire population of visually impaired, cognitively impaired, and lower-literacy Europeans is systematically excluded from the "digital government" that the EU claims to have built. The European Accessibility Act (June 2025 deadline) is the right tool, but enforcement is member-state level and compliance is inconsistent. A universal digital EU government requires a universal accessibility standard with real enforcement — not periodic monitoring reports that no one reads.

**The 2030 gap will become visible.** When Digital Decade targets are assessed in 2030, the divergence between digitally advanced and digitally lagging member states will be stark and politically exploitable. The narrative of "EU promises that only work for some Europeans" is already forming. Getting ahead of it requires honest acknowledgment that inclusion costs money, time, and human effort — and that those investments are budgeted and funded, not aspirational.
