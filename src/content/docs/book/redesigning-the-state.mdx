I had a dream once. We were flying through space on a ship, and we were the
fourth generation aboard. Our parents were around forty-two; we were
twenty-three, twenty-four. We were patching the ship. Pipes, mostly, with
two-part universal glue, the kind that is meant for everything, and grey
insulating tape. They leaked. The ship was breaking down around us, coming apart
while we watched. Parts rusting. A handrail came away and somebody fell.
Pressure escaping somewhere, constantly. It was hot, and we could not regulate
the temperature.

Here is the part of the dream that stayed with me. The people who designed that
ship designed it well. So well that the next two generations never had to
service it. One generation did not have to think about it at all, and only the
one after that started having any problems. But because it had been so good for
so long, the knowledge of how to tune the thing so it did not produce those
problems in the first place, how to maintain it, how to rebuild parts of it,
how to repair, did not survive. So the fourth generation could not cope. Glue
and tape were all they had.

It is a little like that with the European Union, and with every other
political system. A system goes out of date, because the times and the
circumstances it was built for have changed. But something else happens too:
the incentive systems get used up, or they get hacked. Everybody reaches the
finish line, and then something unsettling starts: corruption, elbowing in at
the trough. At some point competition disappears and what counts is the access
you inherited, handed down from one generation to the next.

That is when you have to restart the game. We are living in those times. We
will be restarting this game, and it is worth thinking hard about how to set it
up properly, so that the incentives are right for the generations after us, and
maybe even for us, if we happen to live a long time.

## The redesign is not a takeover

We need to redesign democracy so that it serves our interests: not so that
anyone dominates it in some bad way, but because there has to be a balance
between public servants and society, and the balance has to pay off. Good for
the people who want to work and get somewhere. Good for the people who want to
serve the state well; they should get something out of doing it well. A
motivating package, with benefits for citizens and for officials alike. The
state has to be thought through again, from the beginning.

This is not a generous afterthought. It is the whole mechanism. A system where
serving the public honestly is a worse deal than the alternatives will select
for people who take the alternatives, and no amount of complaining about their
character changes the arithmetic. Anything that only works for one of the two
sides is not a design; it is a fight, and fights get won back.

## Popularity contests, not contests of solutions

What counts in politics today is popularity. Popular views, you have to match
them, and that is how groups form: around popular opinions, or popular ways of
feeling. These are popularity contests. *I feel this way, you feel that way.*
There is no contest of solutions.

I am a positivist, so my question is a different one: how do you see this being
solved, so that both you and I end up satisfied? Where is the line, where is the
mechanism? How do we find the core, and build from the beginning, from values,
from what actually matters to us?

Those values are under threat now, perhaps even the European ones, from the
institutions that were supposed to guard them and from the people who work
inside them, who do not respect us and who trampled the fact that we are the
sovereign. So we start from values again. Democracy, for instance: where is it,
how do you build it. Not complaining, not shouting; doing the groundwork, and
the groundwork is designing a state. I think we can design one. I do not think
it is that hard, with artificial intelligence to help. The problem is simply
that almost nobody is working on it.

## Who designs the digital state

This is the thesis I hold most strongly in this chapter, and I would rather it
were argued against than ignored.

A **digital state** is the same system [The Total State](/book/the-total-state#why-a-digital-state-is-a-total-state)
describes from the other side: total in reach, and therefore potentially
totalitarian. Which makes it dangerous for officials to be the ones deciding
what it looks like and what its architecture is. They will build it so that
steering us is convenient for them, so that they can do it easily, along the
path of least resistance. That is not malice. It is normal. Everyone optimises
the energy they spend.

<PullQuote>
  The mistake is not theirs; the mistake is handing them the pen.
</PullQuote>

The nation's interest points the other way, and it is worth writing out as a
design brief rather than as a complaint.

<Callout kind="claim" label="The design brief">
  The political and digital system should extract the most value out of the
  state machine, not be the easiest thing to steer. It should force competition
  and advancement among those who govern, reward competence and good ideas,
  support European diversity while integrating, and leave room to experiment.
</Callout>

Every item on that list makes the system harder to operate from a console and
better at producing outcomes. That tension is the point. If a proposed
architecture is easy to run and produces little, somebody optimised for the
operator. Ask who wrote the brief.

## A shared core, your own deployment

There is one thing worth taking from China: their regions have a lot of
autonomy. They do not have a common digital state. I would build one.

Every country and region gets its own deployment, and decides how to customise
it. But the core, where we have an enormous amount in common, can simply be
shared. That way the whole European digital state works together, because we
are all running the same infrastructure. And European digital startups get
built on top of it, on European digital infrastructure. What that system would
look like is set out as a proposal in
[European eGovernment](/book/european-egovernment).

Then the part I find more interesting than the country layer. Ideally, digital
systems would be distributed where they are needed, rather than along
administrative borders.

| Kind of place | System |
|---|---|
| An old Italian hill village where you cannot drive a car; a Portuguese one; a town in Albania today | The same one, and possibly the best one for all three |
| A Scandinavian port city | A different one |
| Mountain tourist towns | Another |
| Industrial towns | Another again |

Different systems, deployed where they fit, built and shared in common. The same
goes for legal and administrative arrangements: they can differ between regions
according to the function the region performs. It does not always have to be
"this is one country, so here we do it this way". It can be more functional than
that.

## An offer to Germany

Now a proposal, and I want to label it clearly: this is my idea, not an agreed
plan, and nobody has been asked.

What the Germans built, that whole complex German administration, could be
carried into the digital world with its complexity intact, and I suspect it
would then be comparatively light. I think the Germans could take this on. At
least, it is what I would expect of them, and I think it is worth us expecting
it of them. Germany is a bit lost right now, and we should have expectations of
them. Offer a hand and say: listen, this is the role we would like you to play.
Europe is short of that kind of leadership. As it stands, it may be the United
States that says who leads, what Germany's role is, what we allow them. Germany
does not entirely know what to do with itself either. So give them an idea, one
they gain from and one we all gain from. Win–win.

## Longevity changes the equation

One more thing, and I flag it as speculation: I am guessing at a future, not
reporting a fact.

<EvidenceBox variant="against" title="The strongest case against this site's own premise, from its author">

It is possible we will significantly extend life, and rejuvenate. If that
happens, there probably is no labour-force problem at all in the shape we
currently argue about it: not if we can biologically rejuvenate and
bioengineering goes through a boom. The
[frame](/book/designing-our-retirement) this site stands on assumes no such
boom arrives in time; if one does, the argument about who will do the work
changes shape, and the argument about who holds the state does not.

</EvidenceBox>

Whether *we* get access to those benefits, to bioengineering and to a long life
of decent quality, depends on whether we organise ourselves, whether we arrange
Europe so that it happens here. We are the sovereign; we have to organise it.
Waiting for one party or another to do something does not work, and not because
parties are bad: because the system is set up this way. We have to change the
settings. We have to work out which system creates the right incentives for
those in power.
