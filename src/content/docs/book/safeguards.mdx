I have argued that we should copy the Swiss model of direct democracy and not
get clever about it. The objection people put first is what stops a crowd from
voting something terrible into the constitution on a bad Tuesday. The answer is
that Switzerland has thought about this for well over a century, and the model
comes with its safety mechanisms attached. **We are not buying only the
referendum. We are buying the safeguards too.**

One thing has to be said before the list. The author did not know the Swiss
safeguards and asked for them to be mapped — *"I do not know them, but you can
propose how to map them and set out what it would look like mapped"*. This
chapter is that mapping. The Swiss column is fact, sourced to the Federal
Constitution and the Federal Chancellery. The European and Polish columns are a
proposal made at his request and not yet adopted. His own choices — the votes
bind, two per cent and one per cent, paper as the permanent fallback,
specialists prepare and citizens approve — are marked as his where they appear.

## What we are copying

The two instruments — the citizens' initiative and the optional referendum — and
the ask of 2% and 1% are defined once, in [Direct
Democracy](/book/direct-democracy#the-words-in-one-place). This chapter assumes
them and adds the third Swiss instrument only where it matters: the mandatory
referendum, a vote that happens automatically because the subject requires it —
every change to the federal constitution, and accession to a supranational
community or a collective-security organisation (Art. 140).

The Swiss electorate is called [about four times a
year](https://www.aboutswitzerland.eda.admin.ch/en/direct-democracy) to vote on
an average of fifteen questions, and turnout in recent decades has been a
little over 40% on average. The instrument is used constantly and passes
rarely; both halves of that sentence matter.

### 1. The double majority, worked

A proposal put to the People *and the Cantons* passes only if a majority of
those voting approve it **and** a majority of the cantons approve it (Art. 142
§2). Each canton's vote is decided by the result inside that canton (§3), and
six cantons — Obwalden, Nidwalden, Basel-Stadt, Basel-Landschaft, Appenzell
Ausserrhoden and Appenzell Innerrhoden — carry half a cantonal vote each (§4).
With 20 full and 6 half votes that is 23 cantonal votes, so the threshold is
12. It protects the periphery from being outvoted by a dense core on the rules
themselves — the failure mode that ends federations.

Note the asymmetry, because it is the cleverest part of the design. The double
majority applies to constitutional change. The optional referendum — the
citizen veto on an ordinary law — needs only a majority of those who vote (Art.
142 §1). Vetoing is easy; amending the constitution is hard.

**The proposed Union equivalent** makes the member states the second chamber: a
citizens' vote on anything of treaty or constitutional rank passes only with a
majority of votes cast across the Union and a majority of member states, 14 of
27; a citizen veto of an ordinary legislative act needs only the popular
majority. The half-vote question is left open — the six Swiss half-cantons are
a historical artefact of cantons that split, not a formula anyone derived.
**The proposed Polish equivalent** is the weakest mapping in the set, and it
should be said rather than dressed up: Poland is not a federation, the nearest
unit is the voivodeship, and a constitutional-rank vote would need a national
majority plus a majority in 9 of 16 of them. The objection is real —
voivodeships are administrative, not constituent, and giving them a veto invents
a federalism Poland does not have.

### 2 and 4. Validity, and the counter-proposal

An initiative that bundles unrelated matters, or mixes a general proposal with
a specific draft, is declared invalid by the Federal Assembly, in whole or in
part (Art. 139 §3). This kills the rider — the single most common way a
referendum is used to launder a decision the public did not actually approve.
The proposal is to copy it unchanged and say who rules: plausibly the Court of
Justice on referral in the Union, the Constitutional Tribunal in Poland. What
happens when the body ruling on validity is itself contested has no answer here
that survives a bad-faith government.

The Federal Assembly also recommends acceptance or rejection and may put a
**counter-proposal** beside the initiative; voters may say yes to both and
answer a third question on which they prefer (Art. 139 §5). That is the answer
to "referendums force a complicated question into yes or no": the expert body
puts a better-drafted version on the same ballot paper. It is also the shape I
actually want from specialists. **Specialists prepare the work; citizens can
intervene at any stage, and on the important questions they approve or reject
it.** Not a menu of priced options handed to the public like a restaurant card —
that is not how a state is run, and I will not load the promise that heavily.
Copy the counter-proposal unchanged at both levels. It is the cheapest
safeguard on the list and the one most likely to be dropped for looking
complicated.

### 3. The rights floor

An initiative that infringes **mandatory provisions of international law** is
invalid (Art. 139 §3) — the bans on torture, slavery and genocide, the
non-derogable core usually called *jus cogens*. This is the safeguard aimed at
the scenario the objectors raise: a majority voting to do to a minority what no
majority may do.

The Swiss filter is narrow on purpose, and the Swiss electorate has passed
initiatives that ran into Convention obligations and argued about the conflict
afterwards. A European version has to decide in advance whether the whole
European Convention on Human Rights binds an initiative or only its
non-derogable core. The two are materially different systems. This draft takes
the narrow option as its working default — the non-derogable core, less rather
than more — because it is the one the Swiss model actually runs, and it leaves
the choice marked.

### 5. No turnout quorum, and the Polish clause

Switzerland has no turnout quorum: a proposal passes if a majority of those who
vote approve it (Art. 142 §1). That looks like a missing safeguard and is a
deliberate one. With a turnout quorum, the way to defeat a proposal is to tell
your side to stay home; abstention gets counted as a no, and a boycott beats an
argument. The Venice Commission's [Code of Good Practice on
Referendums](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD%282022%29015-e)
says exactly this: it advises against both a turnout quorum and an approval
quorum, and accepts an approval quorum or a special majority only for questions
of fundamental constitutional significance (III.7).

A Polish national referendum is binding only if **more than half of those
entitled to vote take part** ([Constitution, Art.
125](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Poland_2009)). That single
clause is why Polish referendums keep producing nothing, and it is what lets a
parliament hold one and ignore it. The proposal: drop the turnout quorum, make
the result binding on the votes cast, and keep an approval quorum only for
constitutional-rank questions, on the Venice Commission's line. It is the most
consequential change on this page and the one that will be fought hardest.

### 6. Binding force, and where Poland's instrument stops

In Switzerland the result changes the law: an adopted initiative amends the
constitution; a successful referendum kills the act. Poland already has a
citizens' motion for a referendum — 500,000 citizens may petition for one
([Act on the national referendum, Art.
63](https://isap.sejm.gov.pl/isap.nsf/download.xsp/WDU20030570507/T/D20030507L.pdf)) —
but the Sejm is not obliged to call it, and no deadline compels it to decide.
The right is to ask. That is the whole difference between the two systems, and
it is the thing itself, not a detail of implementation.

My position is the binding one: **the referendum binds**, both instruments. The
proposal that follows from it is an automatic trigger: signatures verified,
question valid, vote scheduled, with no discretionary step between the citizens
and the ballot box.

### 7 and 8. The layers below, and fixed dates

Federal votes are the visible part of the Swiss system. Cantons and communes
run their own initiatives and referendums with lower thresholds, and a citizen
votes far more often on local matters than national ones: the system is trained
where the stakes are small and the feedback is fast. Ours is not, and that is
the level to start at — school councils, municipalities, regions, then the
state, then the Union, the order he gave. Start where a mistake is cheap. It is
also where **paper voting in schools and town halls** is obviously affordable:
if a school hall can hold a national election, it can hold a local vote, and
the number of people turning out for a municipal question is not large. If
digital voting is too expensive, the answer is to work on the cost of it, not
to drop the votes.

Four fixed voting dates a year, questions bundled onto them, is how the Swiss
keep the cost down and the temperature low: a question that loses can come
back, so no ballot is the last word forever. I asked for decisions every three
months; the Swiss calendar is the same rhythm with a name. Fixed dates, at every
level.

## The arithmetic at our scale

The thresholds are my figure: **2% of the electorate to launch an initiative, 1%
to force a referendum.** The Swiss numbers, as a share of their electorate, are
the comparison, and that share is the only way to compare countries of
different sizes. Switzerland had 5,611,289 registered voters at the [September
2024 federal vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Swiss_referendums); so
100,000 signatures is 1.78% of the electorate and 50,000 is 0.89%. The 2:1
ratio between proposing and vetoing is Swiss; the level is his. The reasons
offered for rounding up rather than copying the share — a round figure people
can repeat, and an instrument this powerful being slightly harder to reach than
the one it is copied from — are the editor's, not a position he has taken.

Three remarks on that table. **The Polish initiative number lands close to the
threshold Poland already uses:** about 580,000 against the existing 500,000 for
a referendum motion. So the problem with the Polish instrument is not the
number; it is that the result does not bind and the Sejm does not have to act.
**The 100-day veto window does not survive translation to Union scale.** A
hundred days to collect three and a half million signatures across 27 states
and 24 languages is a different task from fifty thousand in a country the size
of Switzerland; six months is proposed here, with the cost stated — every month
added is a month of legal uncertainty for the act being challenged. **The
European Citizens' Initiative is not this and is not close.** One million
signatures from seven member states, and the Commission is not obliged to
propose anything; the Union built the collection machinery and attached it to
nothing. [The first move](/book/first-move#how-it-actually-works) is about
using it anyway.

## What the Swiss model costs

The model has costs, and a chapter that copies it owes the list.

**Turnout is low** — [a little over 40%](https://www.aboutswitzerland.eda.admin.ch/en/direct-democracy)
on average in recent decades. Read that as fatigue, or as citizens sensibly
skipping the questions they do not care about; both readings are honest. It
does mean a determined, organised minority of the whole electorate can carry a
federal vote. **Money works.** Campaigns can be funded, and frequent voting
multiplies the surface. This is the strongest practical criticism of the model,
and this chapter has no answer to it beyond disclosure rules stricter than the
Swiss ones. **It is slow.** A law can be stopped after parliament has passed
it; a constitutional change takes years. In a crisis that is a liability, which
is why the emergency-act provisions exist — and those are themselves subject to
a referendum if they run past a year.

<EvidenceBox variant="against" title="The case against me">
  Swiss women got the federal vote in [February
  1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_Switzerland). An
  all-male electorate had voted the question down in 1959, two to one, and took
  twelve more years to reverse itself; the first federal election women voted in
  was 31 October 1971, and the last canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, gave women the
  cantonal vote only after a Federal Supreme Court judgment of 27 November 1990.
  If you want a case where a majority withheld a right from a minority through
  the direct instruments, it is right here, in the model I am proposing to copy.
  It argues for the rights floor in safeguard 3 being stronger than the Swiss
  one — not for abandoning the instrument that eventually, slowly, corrected it.
</EvidenceBox>

## Where I stand, and the argument against me

My position is this: **history's record of mass harm is overwhelmingly a record
of what governments did, not what electorates voted for.** The catastrophes of
the last century were organised by states, by administrations, by small numbers
of people holding a monopoly of decision. I am much less afraid of a crowd
making a bad call on a Tuesday than of a cabinet making one for thirty years
with no mechanism to stop it. That is why I want the safeguards attached to the
instrument rather than used as an excuse to refuse it — and it is also why the
instrument matters for the rest of this site: direct democracy is what keeps
[the gains from the machines](/book/who-it-earns-for) with society rather than
with whoever owns them.

The standard counter-argument is serious and belongs here at full strength.
**Majorities have voted to restrict minorities.** Referendums on minority
rights have a bad record in several countries, including Switzerland; a right
that requires fifty per cent plus one is not a right but a permission; and the Swiss
women's vote above is this exact objection, evidenced, inside the model I want.
That argument is not settled here in his favour. What can be claimed is
narrower: the objection is an argument about *which safeguards* and *how strong
a rights floor*, not an argument for leaving decisions where they are now. Both
sides want a limit on what a majority may do. The disagreement is over who is
more dangerous without one.

## Left open in this draft

- **The rights floor** — the whole Convention or only its non-derogable core
  (safeguard 3; ASK-2).
- **Who rules on validity**, and what happens when that body is itself captured
  (safeguard 2).
- **The second chamber for Poland** — voivodeships are the least bad unit named
  here and they are not good (ASK-1).
- **The veto window at Union scale** — six months is a guess, not a finding
  (ASK-3).
- **Campaign-finance rules**, which have to be stricter than the Swiss ones and
  are not drafted here.
- **Digital voting** — paper first, digital built in parallel as something we
  learn on, paper kept permanently as the fallback for the basic functions of
  the state; what has to be true of identity, verification and audit before a
  digital referendum binds is unanswered.
- **Whether a Union instrument needs treaty change**, or whether an automatic
  referral obligation could be attached to the existing Citizens' Initiative
  first — the lawyer's question that decides how soon any of this can start.
