In the manifesto I wrote that the work is going away and that this is the time
we need. This chapter is the practical form of that sentence. If the time is
real, somebody has to say where it comes from, who pays for it and what it is
for. Otherwise it arrives as unemployment, which is the same hours with none of
the money and none of the dignity.

There are two ways a society can receive a jump in productivity. It can take it
as fewer people working, with the rest thrown out and told they failed. Or it
can take it as everyone working less. The second is a choice, not a mechanism.
Nothing about a machine decides which one you get.

## Lifelong learning is a slogan until you free the days

Every serious document about the next twenty years says the same thing: people
will have to keep learning. Skills will go stale faster than careers. We are
genuinely asking whether we can keep up with AI at all.

Then the same document leaves the working week at five days and says nothing
more.

You cannot learn a new trade in the evening after a full week, with children in
the house. Almost nobody does. Lifelong learning is not a question of will or of
platforms or of another funded programme of certificates. It is a question of
days. If we mean it, we have to take the days out of the week and give them to
people.

So: **two days a week for something else.** Study, if you want to study. Your
own projects, if you are building something. Your children. Rest. It is not the
state's business which. The state's business is that the days exist.

## Where to cut, and why there

Not everywhere at once, and not by decree in the abstract. Cut the week where
the machines demonstrably took the work.

Start with services, where AI already took a great deal of it — support, back
office, translation, routine analysis, and a large part of what I used to be
paid to do. The output is being produced with fewer hours. Shorten the week
there by a day and the same work still gets done, because it is already getting
done that way.

Then the factories, as the robots arrive. Chapter
[Robotic Reindustrialisation](/book/robotic-reindustrialisation) argues that
Europe has to build those lines anyway, for reasons that have nothing to do with
working time — we will not have the workers. When a line is automated, the hours
it used to need do not disappear from the economy; they disappear from somebody's
life. Shorten the week on that line.

The end state I have in mind is four days for most people and three for some.
Not one uniform week. A range, with four as the default.

## Pay: make four days the normal week in law

The objection is immediate and fair: a four-day week where the fifth day is
simply unpaid is a 20% pay cut with a friendly name. That is a real risk and it
is how this reform fails.

The answer is to move the legal anchor. **Redefine the minimum wage as pay for
four days, not five.** The fifth day is paid on top of it. A person working five
days earns more than a person working four, but the floor — the number the law
protects, the number a full-time job is measured against — covers four.

Then price the edges:

- Weekend work pays more. Sunday pays more still.
- Overtime past the four days is priced so that a fifth day is a real choice an
  employer makes, not the silent default.

That is the whole mechanism: a set of incentives arranged so that the cheapest,
most ordinary thing an employer can do is offer four days. Today the incentives
point the other way, and that is the only reason five days feels natural.

## Flexibility that runs both ways

A shorter week must not become a new uniform. Some people want more hours, not
fewer.

Take my own case. I have not worked for seven months. I spend my days building
things with AI — including this site. In the system I am describing I could go
and work Friday, Saturday and Sunday at weekend rates, three long days, and keep
four days for what I am actually doing. I would earn a living and keep the
project. Right now the labour market offers me either a full week or nothing,
so I take nothing and live off savings. That is not flexibility; that is a
market with one setting.

And there are people on the other side of it — building a business, saving for
something, simply wanting the money — who should be able to take five days or
six and be paid properly for them. The point is not to force anyone into
leisure. The point is that the default should be four, and that the deviations
in both directions should be priced honestly.

## Why this is not about leisure

Here is the part that matters most to me, and it is not about rest.

This site argues for [direct democracy](/book/direct-democracy) — that citizens
should hold an initiative and a veto over the laws that govern them, and should
decide the direction of the Union rather than ratify it every four years. The
strongest objection to that argument is that people do not have time to think.
They are tired, they are working, they will vote on a headline.

That objection is correct under a five-day week. It is a statement about
schedules, not about people's intelligence.

A citizenry that decides things needs time to read, argue and change its mind.
Nobody is going to hand that time over out of civic good will. It has to come
from somewhere, and the only place it can come from is the working week — which
is precisely where automation is about to create a surplus. The freed hours and
the democratic deficit are the same problem seen from two sides.

The same goes for the rest of what this book asks for. Rebuilding the state,
[paying for our own retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement), learning fast
enough to stay useful next to the machines — all of it needs a population with
hours to spare and something worth doing with them. We are about to have the
hours. The question is whether anyone arranges them into anything.

## What I do not know

I would rather name the hard parts than pretend they are solved.

**Who pays for the fifth day.** If the productivity gain is real, the gain pays
for it — that is the claim, and in an automated service company it is plausible.
In a firm where the machines have not arrived yet, it is not. Cutting the week
there means either raising prices or cutting profit, and somebody has to say
which. My instinct is that the cut should follow the automation rather than
lead it, sector by sector, which is why I wrote "where the machines
demonstrably took the work" rather than "everywhere by 2030". But what counts
as demonstrably, and who measures it, I do not know.

**What stops it becoming a pay cut.** The minimum-wage redefinition is my answer
and I think it is the right shape, because it moves the floor rather than
relying on good employers. But a floor can be eroded by hiring people as
contractors, by splitting jobs, by pushing work off the books. Anyone who has
watched labour law knows those moves. They would need closing, and I do not know
the law well enough to say how.

**Sectors where output is hours.** A nurse, a bus driver, a carer, a teacher in
a classroom. Their output is not going to be automated in the next decade, and
a four-day week there means hiring more of them, in professions that are already
short-staffed. This is the genuine hole in the argument. I can see two honest
answers — pay much more and treat the extra staff as a public investment, or
accept that the shorter week arrives later in those sectors than in the
automated ones — and I do not know which is right. Anyone saying it is costless
there is not being straight with you.

**What would settle it.** Not an essay. Run it: pick a sector where AI has
measurably taken hours, cut the week there with the four-day minimum wage
attached, publish output, wages, staffing and sick leave before and after, and
let the numbers argue. Then do the same in a hospital and publish the bill.

## What has actually been tried

I promised not to invent evidence, so here is what exists and what it does not
show.

Iceland ran two large public-sector trials between 2015 and 2019, covering about
2,500 workers — roughly 1% of the country's working population — with no cut in
pay. The
[report by Autonomy and Alda](https://autonomy.work/portfolio/icelandsww/) found
that productivity and services stayed the same or improved in most workplaces,
and that wellbeing rose sharply. Union agreements that followed mean a large
share of Icelandic workers now have shorter hours or the right to negotiate
them.

The honest caveat, which the headlines dropped: those trials cut the week to
roughly 35–36 hours, not to four days.
[Researchers writing in The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/the-success-of-icelands-four-day-week-trial-has-been-greatly-overstated-164083)
argued the result was widely overstated for exactly that reason. It is evidence
that a shorter week can be done without losing output. It is not evidence for
four days.

The UK pilot is closer. 61 organisations and around 2,900 workers ran a
four-day week from June to December 2022, studied by Boston College, Cambridge
and Autonomy. [The results](https://autonomy.work/portfolio/uk4dwpilotresults/)
report revenue broadly steady over the trial, sharp falls in resignations and
absence, and large drops in reported burnout. A
[follow-up a year later](https://autonomy.work/portfolio/making-it-stick/) found
most participating organisations had kept the policy and about half had made it
permanent.

What that is: strong evidence that firms which volunteered for a four-day week
mostly kept it and did not fall apart. What it is not: evidence about firms that
did not volunteer, about hospitals and buses, or about what happens when the
week is shortened by law rather than by choice. I am arguing for something
larger than has been tested, and I would rather say so.

---

I am not a politician and I do not have the legislative text. I am someone who
thinks the freed time is the largest thing about to happen to Europe, and that
we are on course to waste it as unemployment, resentment and eventually war —
which is what the [manifesto](/manifesto) says, and which is the reason I am
writing any of this down.

Four days, three for some, the minimum wage measured over four, the weekend
priced properly. That is the proposal. If you know how to close the holes I
named, I would rather hear it than be right.
