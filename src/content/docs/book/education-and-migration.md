# Education Instead of Import

I have written elsewhere that importing labour will not carry an aging
continent, and that machines will have to do most of the work instead. That
argument is in [Designing Our Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement), and I
stand by it. But it leaves a hole, and people are right to point at it.

Saying "not this" is not a position. Europe will still need people. Some of them
will come from outside Europe, and they should. The question is how we choose
them, what we offer them, and what we owe the places they leave.

This chapter is the other half. It is not about closing a door. It is about
deciding, in the open, who walks through it — and about doing something for the
people who never will.

## What our governments actually did

They took the lazy route, and then acted surprised.

Instead of teaching people where they already live, we moved them. We set up
incentives that selected for who could reach a border and endure the crossing,
rather than for who had prepared. We brought people into countries whose
language they did not speak, into labour markets that had no use for their
actual skills, with no published standard anyone could have worked toward. Then
we looked at the result — expensive, resented, and bad for almost everyone
involved — and called it an unavoidable consequence of a complicated world.

It was not complicated. It was stupid. And I want to be precise about who I am
calling stupid, because this argument gets dirty fast: I mean the governments
that designed this, not the people who walked into it. Somebody who crosses a
desert for a chance at a wage is behaving rationally. The policy that made that
the entry exam is the thing that failed.

The failure is a design failure. If you never state your criteria, you still
have criteria — you just let geography, smugglers and luck pick for you.

## The alternative: teach first, then invite

Here is what I think we should do instead, and almost all of it is available
today over the internet.

Europe builds free online education for anyone in the world who wants it. Real
courses, in European working languages — English first, and German, French,
Polish and the rest where there is demand. Language teaching, vocational
training, and full remote degrees from European universities. Free at the point
of use, open to anybody with a connection.

And alongside it, we publish the criteria. Plainly, on a page anyone can read:
this is the language level, this is the qualification, this is the demonstrated
skill. Meet them and you may come and work. Do not meet them and you may not,
and you will have known that from the beginning rather than after selling
everything you own.

That turns the whole thing around. The selection stops being *who got here* and
becomes *who put in the work*. You show that you learned the language. You show
you finished the school. You show you can do the job. Then we bring you over —
deliberately, knowing what we are getting, and so do you.

We will need workers. That is the premise of this entire site. I would rather we
chose the capable and the determined, and chose them openly by a standard they
could see, than take whoever arrives and pretend it was a plan.

## The certificate is not a problem

A lot of people in the global south will enrol in a remote European university
mainly for the European certificate. I have heard that said as an objection. I
do not think it is one.

The education is real either way. A degree taken seriously enough to finish
leaves somebody genuinely more capable, whatever their first motive was. It
lifts people out of poverty — with us, or at home. And in many countries the
remote European course will simply be better than what is locally on offer,
which means our self-interested recruitment channel is also, without any extra
effort, the best schooling available to a lot of people who will never leave.

I am content with a system where the incentive that gets someone in the door is
a piece of paper, and the thing they walk out with is a skill.

## Taking responsibility instead of importing people

Now the larger part, and the one I care about most.

If we only build a pipeline for the ablest, we have creamed off exactly the
people a poor country needs and left everyone else where they were. That is not
responsibility, it is a better-organised version of the same extraction.

So the education programme has to be much bigger than recruitment, and it has to
be aimed at people staying put. Practical knowledge, free, translated by machine
into hundreds of languages — and I mean hundreds; this is the thing that changed
in the last few years. Translating a course into three hundred African languages
used to be an impossible budget. Now it is a technical task.

What would be in it:

- **Growing things.** Gardening, orchards, soil, what to plant and when.
- **Farming against desert.** How to hold ground that is drying out, and how to
  get water to it.
- **Wounds and basic medicine.** What to do before a doctor, when there is often
  no doctor.
- **Basic construction.** Shelter, sanitation, water, repair.
- **How to organise.** Running a cooperative, keeping accounts, settling
  disputes, making a decision a village will accept.

And possibly the tools for that last one: software for self-organisation and for
[direct democracy](/book/direct-democracy), the same instruments I want us to
use here, offered to people who currently have no mechanism for a collective
decision at all.

The blunt version of the argument is this. **To stop global migration we have to
start helping people where they are.** Europe has Africa as a neighbour. Not as
a moral abstraction — as a continent next door with a young and growing
population and, in many places, nothing worth staying for. Either we take some
responsibility for that, or we spend the next fifty years arguing about boats.

I think collecting what humanity knows about the practical business of living,
translating it into every language it can reach, and giving it away is the
cheapest serious thing Europe could do for the world. And it happens to serve us
too, which is why I think it might actually get done.

## Bringing people here, and sending them back

The last piece is one people find uncomfortable, and it is the piece I am
surest about.

China sent its people to the United States to study and work, and then brought
them home. Whatever else you think of China, that policy worked: it ran a
generation through the best institutions elsewhere and got the knowledge back.

We should do the same thing, pointed the other way — this time we do it *for
them*. Educate people where they are. Bring them to Europe for a few years to
work and learn properly. And prepare them, while they are here, to go home
carrying something: capital, a qualification, contacts, the concrete experience
of how a functioning system runs.

That means the return is designed, not enforced as an afterthought. A person who
spent four years here and goes back with a business plan, a network, and savings
is worth more to their country than the remittances they would have sent over a
lifetime. It also means Europe is not quietly permanent-stripping the people a
poor country cannot spare.

Some will want to stay. Fine — we needed workers, that was the point. But the
default relationship should be an exchange, not a drain.

## An open door for Japan and Korea

Here is a specific case where Europe has the opposite problem. Not "how do we
choose among people who want to come", but "why are we making it hard for people
we should be actively inviting".

I think it is a scandal that there is no easy route into Europe for Japanese and
Korean people who want to work and settle here. The Union could make this simple
and has not. Let me be clear that what follows is my opinion and not a finding:
the Japanese and Koreans I have met are creative and hard-working, many of them
are looking for a way out of a work culture at home that grinds people down, and
I think they would bring a great deal to any country that took them in. I would
like us to be that country.

What I want is not complicated. Work visas that are easy to get. A settlement
path that a person can actually plan a life around. And on top of that, the
thing Canada already does: a working-holiday route where you simply come, look
around for a year, take casual work or a permanent job, and are told plainly
that you are welcome.

**We already have a thin version of this, which is the good news and also the
point.** Canada's [International Experience
Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/work-canada/iec/eligibility.html)
scheme admits Japanese and South Korean citizens under Working Holiday, with two
further categories — Young Professionals and International Co-op — open to
Koreans. Poland, for its part, signed a working-holiday agreement with
[Japan](https://www.gov.pl/web/family/working-holiday-scheme-poland-japan) in
2015 and one with South Korea in 2018; they sit alongside six others on the
ministry's [list](https://www.gov.pl/web/family/working-holiday-agreements).
Other EU states have their own — Germany, France, Italy, Ireland, Austria,
Denmark and more are among the [thirty-odd
partners](https://japan-visa.com/visas/working-holiday) Japan deals with.

So the argument is not "we have nothing". It is: what we have is tiny and
deliberately kept that way. These schemes are bilateral, capped in the hundreds
— Poland's Japanese scheme runs to 500 places a year on the Polish side — open
only to people aged 18 to 30, good for a single year, and written so that the
work is incidental to a holiday rather than the point of the trip. They are a
cultural-exchange gesture. They are not a door.

Widen it. Raise or drop the caps. Lift the age ceiling. Let the work be the
reason you came rather than a footnote to it. Make the year convertible into a
residence permit for anyone who found something worth staying for. None of this
requires a new theory of migration; it requires taking an instrument that
already exists and stopping being timid with it.

And Poland could do most of it alone. Admission rules are largely national, and
a bilateral scheme is a bilateral scheme — we signed the last two without asking
anyone's permission.

**Which brings me to the part I will not skip.** I would put this to a citizens'
vote, and I would abide by the answer. This is a national decision, and each
country should be free to make it separately. I do not accept having decisions
of this kind imposed on me from above, and I am not going to argue for imposing
them on anybody else — not on other Poles, and not on other member states. If I
want the mechanism in [Direct Democracy](/book/direct-democracy) to be real, it
has to be real on the questions I care about most, including this one. I will
make the case and try to win the vote. That is the whole of my claim.

It is also the same principle as the rest of this chapter. Decide openly who you
are inviting, say so out loud, and build the route. The alternative is not
neutrality — it is having no policy and then describing whatever happens as
though somebody chose it.

## What I do not know

I am not going to invent numbers for a website, so here is the honest list of
what would have to be established before any of this is a programme rather than
an argument.

- **Whether remote qualification predicts working out here.** The whole scheme
  rests on the claim that somebody who learned the language and finished a
  remote degree integrates and performs better than somebody selected the
  current way. That is testable. Run one cohort, follow it for five years, and
  publish what happened.
- **What the criteria should actually be.** Which language level, which
  qualifications, for which jobs. That is work for people who know the labour
  market, not for me.
- **Whether the legal route exists.** Admission rules are mostly national, and
  a common European standard for education-based entry may need instruments we
  do not have. I do not know which. Same admission as in [The Political
  Path](/book/political-path): somebody with the legal training should say.
- **Whether machine translation is good enough for teaching.** It is plainly
  good enough for a news article. Whether it is good enough to teach wound care
  in a language with little written material is an empirical question, and one
  wrong instruction in medicine is worse than no instruction. Test it before
  shipping it.
- **Whether return actually happens.** Every scheme of this kind has been
  promised before. What makes people go home, and what makes a returnee's
  knowledge stick once they are back, is the part with the worst track record.

None of these is a reason not to start. They are the reasons to start small and
publish the results.

## What this means for you

Almost every piece of this is buildable by people who do not need permission.

A course is a course. If you can teach something practical — planting,
plumbing, wiring, first aid, bookkeeping, a trade you know — it can be recorded,
translated and given away this year, by you, without waiting for a European
programme to exist. If you work at a university, a remote track open to students
outside Europe is an administrative decision, not a treaty. If you write
software, the translation pipeline and the tools for self-organisation are
ordinary engineering.

And if you argue about migration in public, you can carry the constructive half
of it instead of only the refusal. That is most of what I am asking. Europe has
spent twenty years with one side shouting *let them in* and the other shouting
*keep them out*, and hardly anyone saying **teach them, publish the standard,
choose openly, and do something about the reason they are leaving.**
