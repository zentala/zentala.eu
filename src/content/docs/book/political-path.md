# The Political Path

Every chapter here ends the same way, and I have been getting away with it. A
phased approach. Pilot countries first. Policy catches up to what is already
running. All of it true, and all of it silent on the question anybody serious
asks first: **who decides?**

This chapter is my answer. It is the least finished part of the vision, and I
would rather publish it half-built than keep pretending the plan has no hole in
it.

## Start from the assumption that they say no

I do not expect the current European administration to want this.

Not because the people in it are bad. Because everything here shortens the
distance between a citizen and a decision, and an institution does not usually
volunteer to become less necessary. Direct democracy takes votes away from
parliaments. A shared working language reduces the value of twenty-four-language
machinery. A digital state built once removes twenty-seven procurement budgets.
Read the proposals from inside Brussels and they look less like reform and more
like a demotion.

So the route does not start in Brussels. If you plan a reform that requires the
enthusiasm of the people it demotes, you have not made a plan, you have made a
wish.

It starts with citizens. Not as a slogan — as the actual order of operations.
The idea gets promoted until enough people agree with it, and then it becomes
something politicians campaign on rather than something they concede.

## The discontent is already here. It has nowhere to go.

Europeans are angry at their government, and they are right to be. That anger is
the largest political resource on the continent and it is being spent badly.

It is spent on individuals. This commissioner, that prime minister, this
coalition. You throw them out, the replacement behaves the same way within a
year, and the lesson people draw is that nothing can be changed by anyone. That
conclusion is corrosive, and it is not even correct. The politicians are mostly
behaving rationally inside the machine they were elected into.

**So my move is to point the discontent at the machine instead: at
representative democracy itself.** Not at a party. At the arrangement where you
pick a person once every four or five years, hand them everything, and have no
instrument between elections but complaint. A system where a lobbyist gets a
meeting and you get a mailing list.

That redirection is dangerous on its own, and I want to be honest about why.
Anger at the system with nothing offered in its place has a well-known
destination in Europe: it goes national, it goes backward, and it ends up
looking for someone to blame rather than something to build. We have seen this
film. Some of us are living in the sequel.

**Discontent without an offer turns into nationalism. Discontent plus a
mechanism turns into reform.** The whole weight of this chapter rests on getting
the second one out in front.

## The offer is a mechanism, not a mood

The offer is direct democracy — the ability to decide specific questions
yourself, often, at low cost, with a result that binds.

This is the part where the rest of the site stops being a wish list. A digital
state with real identity, verifiable records and receipts is not a convenience
project; it is the infrastructure that makes frequent, trustworthy voting
possible at all. Direct democracy was impractical for two centuries because
asking everybody anything was slow and expensive. That constraint is gone, and
almost nothing in our institutions has been updated to notice.

I am not proposing that every question goes to a vote. Budgets, treaties and
long-horizon strategy need people who work on them full-time, and I have no
interest in governing by weekly referendum on subjects nobody has read about.
What I want is a system where citizens hold decisions that are close to them,
where a mandate can be corrected between elections, and where the default answer
to "you had your chance in 2027" is no longer accepted.

That is an offer. It is concrete, it is buildable, and it answers the anger with
something other than a new face.

## Unanimity is not needed for everything

Here is the practical unlock, and it is the most useful thing in this chapter.

The reflex when talking about European reform is to imagine twenty-seven
governments agreeing at once, which is why the conversation usually stops within
a minute. But a large part of this does not need twenty-seven signatures. It
needs one government willing to go first, and then a few more copying what
visibly worked.

**English as an official working language can be adopted by a single state.**
Poland could do it. A group could do it together — Central Europe, the states
that live next to Russian aggression and already know they are one bloc whether
or not anyone drew the line. This is an organisational decision, taken from
below, about what language your administration and your business actually work
in. It does not require anybody in Brussels to approve the idea of Europeans
understanding each other.

**The military argument makes this immediate rather than visionary.** Armies in
this region already have to operate with the Anglosphere — the planning, the
doctrine, the equipment manuals, the exercises, the alliance we would call if
the shooting started. That happens in English today, imperfectly, by
improvisation. Making English official in the military and in business does two
things at once: it ties Central Europe tighter to the English-speaking world,
which is what keeps deterrence credible, and it removes friction from the
economy while it is at it. Security and growth pointing the same direction is
rare. When that happens you should take the offer.

**A digital state can be built by one country and copied.** One state builds
identity, records and services that work; its citizens stop standing in queues;
the neighbours' citizens notice. Copying a running system is a far easier
political act than legislating one into existence for twenty-seven countries
that have not seen it yet. Build the demonstration. The demonstration does the
arguing.

None of these three needs a treaty. They need a government that wants to be
first.

## The treaty question I cannot answer

I do not know whether the full version of this requires changing the European
treaties.

It may be that new treaties can simply be written, the way the Union has always
added instruments alongside the ones it had. It may be that parts of this — the
language regime, the legal force of a binding citizen vote, a common digital
identity, a joint industrial programme — collide with commitments already made.
I am not a lawyer, and I am not going to invent a reassuring legal conclusion
for a website.

What I can do is name what would have to be checked, because that is a real
piece of work somebody can pick up:

- **The language question.** What a member state may declare as its own official
  working language, and where that runs into the Union's rules on languages and
  on equal treatment.
- **Binding citizen votes.** Whether a national referendum result can bind a
  government's conduct at European level, and what the existing citizens'
  initiative instrument can and cannot do today.
- **Coalitions of the willing.** Which of the existing routes for a subset of
  states to act together apply here, what they permit, and what they forbid.
- **Shared digital infrastructure.** Whether one state's identity and records
  system can be adopted by another without a new legal instrument, and who is
  liable when it is.
- **Pension money as investment.** What the rules allow when contributions are
  put into building public infrastructure rather than into financial assets.

If you are a European lawyer and any of that is answerable, that answer is worth
more to this project than another chapter of mine. I would like to be corrected
in public.

## Critical mass is the mechanism

I want to say plainly what I think the causal chain is, so it can be attacked.

This becomes politically possible when enough people obviously want it. Not a
majority in one poll — obvious, visible, sustained want. At that point
politicians do not need convincing, because supporting it stops being brave and
starts being the safe option. That is how every large change I can think of
happened: the position became popular first and respectable second.

Which means the bottleneck is not Brussels, and it is not the treaties. It is
the number of people who have heard the argument and found it sensible. That is
a number you and I can move this year.

## What this means for you

You are not waiting for permission, and there is nobody to ask.

Two things are worth your time. **Carry the argument** — in your company, your
town, your union, your party, whichever one it is, because nothing here belongs
to a single political side and I have deliberately kept it that way. Tell people
the version you believe, including the parts where I am guessing.

And **build a piece.** A demonstration is worth more than agreement: working
software, a municipality that automated something visible, a firm that switched
to English and got faster, a legal memo that closes one of the open questions
above. Pieces that run are the only argument politics reliably respects.

I do not expect the institutions to start this. I expect them to join once it is
already happening and claim they were in favour all along. That is fine. That is
the deal, and it works.
