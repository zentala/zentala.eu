{/* SPINE: English is the bottleneck of Europe's information economy; making it the working language of business and administration is a formality we already live by, and the fact that nobody has done it is the question. */}

So much is said about European integration, and nobody has introduced the language. Why not? It is only a formality. We already speak it: we do business with each other in English, we read each other in English, and making that official is not hard. Maybe somebody does not want integration to happen this way. Then it should be us, as citizens, who say that we do.

**English is the bottleneck of Europe's information economy.** A plausible reason no European startups and no European-scale corporations grew here is that we never had one working language. Europe's real failure is the absence of companies whose capital cannot be called French, German or Dutch, just European. Creating them is the point of language integration, not a side effect of it.

<Callout kind="demand" label="The ask">
  English as an official working language of European business and administration, in the interest of Europeans: not of capital, and not of interest groups tied to this or that nation.
</Callout>

This is the floor of <TermRef slug="the-three-layers">the three layers</TermRef>. The digital state is built on the language, and the robots are built on the digital state; the order is not decoration, each level is what makes the next one possible.

## The scarce resource is the attention of the most capable people

A shrinking workforce has to be organised well, because there is no slack left. The scarce resource becomes the attention of the most capable people, and we already lose them to elsewhere. For them to work with each other inside Europe, they need a shared working language.

The same shortage explains why there is no European AI: no European science working shoulder to shoulder, and no shared language for it to work in. That argument is made in full in [European science](/book/european-science); here it is one consequence of the same missing floor.

What the reform looks like from where you stand, in the version first written down in 2024:

- wherever you are in the Union, you can speak to the local administration and complete its forms in English;
- contracts and court proceedings can be conducted in English anywhere in Europe;
- the Union's institutions produce documents in English that are legally recognised across member states, and the era of translating everything ends.

Language integration brings economies of scale on a scale few reforms can reach, and it is possibly the cheapest reform we could introduce. It is cheap because the expensive part, teaching a continent a second language, is already happening in every school; the reform is the right to use, in an office and in a court, what school already taught.

## There is no European public without a shared language

There is a second reason, and it is political rather than economic. **European political consciousness cannot exist without a shared language.** The awareness people build working together in business becomes, over time, the awareness they bring to European politics. Without it there is no European public, only national publics talking past each other.

That is why the ask includes media, not only forms: European media in English, because we already talk to each other in English anyway. A Union-wide argument cannot be had in twenty-four languages at once, and a Union that wants its citizens to decide things together, as [direct democracy](/book/direct-democracy) asks, needs a place where they can hear each other first.

## A shared interest is what keeps us from turning on each other

And there is a blunt third reason, older than the other two. A shared interest is what keeps us from turning on each other. The Union was built so there would be no more war; a common venture that stays worth more than any quarrel is how that works in this century.

Maybe we should have introduced English as the Union's official language after the crisis of 2008. Maybe we would be somewhere else today. Maybe we should have been building fast rail across Europe then too, while the workforce was still young, before we got old. We did not, and [the window](/book/designing-our-retirement) is now roughly thirty years.

## Where it starts: administration, including the military, and Central Europe first

The state that goes first builds its digital administration, its health service and its military on the language. That is the order: language first, then the whole digital state on top of it, then robotics, military and civilian. English official in administration means official in the ministries that matter most, and the army is one of them.

<SideNote label="Argued in full">
  Why one state, or a bloc, can adopt this without twenty-seven signatures: [Political path](/book/political-path#unanimity-is-not-needed-for-everything).
</SideNote>

Central Europe can go first. Deciding what language your own administration and your own business work in is an organisational decision, taken from below; it does not need anybody in Brussels to approve the idea of Europeans understanding each other. A group of states that already knows it is one bloc can do it together.

The research behind this chapter adds an argument the author did not make, and it is offered, not his. The armies of the region already plan, train and read their manuals with an alliance whose two official languages are English and French ([NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/work-with-us/careers/frequently-asked-questions)); an official English in the military would formalise what the alliance already runs on, and tie the region's deterrence to the language it is already conducted in.

<EvidenceBox variant="against" title="The strongest fact against this chapter">
  Almost half of Europeans speak English as a foreign language (47%, Special Eurobarometer, Europeans and their languages, fieldwork September–October 2023). The other half do not. An official working language that half the Union cannot use is, on the day it is adopted, the language of the mobile, the young and the educated; it becomes everyone's only as fast as the schools deliver it. The schools are delivering: [97.6% of lower-secondary pupils in the EU were learning English in 2024](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Foreign_language_learning_statistics) (Eurostat). Until that cohort is the workforce, the reform has a distributional edge, and a chapter that wants it has to say so.
</EvidenceBox>

<ObjectionList title="The objections, answered in five sentences each">
  <Objection claim="English is the language of a country that left. Ireland and Malta are too small to carry it." verdict="The precedent is on this side, not the other.">
    English stays an official and working language of the Union as long as Regulation No 1 lists it, and changing that list takes a unanimous Council; the Commission [said so after Brexit](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/languages_en). Ireland's constitution makes Irish the first official language and English the second ([Article 8](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/cons/en/html)); Malta's makes Maltese the national language, both languages official, and lets the administration use either ([Article 5](https://legislation.mt/eli/const/eng)). Two member states already run the arrangement this chapter asks for: a national language, and English beside it in the office. That the language is now no large member's own is not a weakness; it is what makes it neutral in a way French or German can never be.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="France will never accept it." verdict="France keeps French. The veto covers Brussels, not Warsaw.">
    France's constitution says the language of the Republic is French ([Article 2](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr/en/constitution-of-4-october-1958)), and the [Toubon law of 1994](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/JORFTEXT000000349929/) requires French in public contracts and official documents. Nothing here asks France to change that: the proposal is an additional working language, not a replacement, and every member state keeps its own. At Union level France can block a change to Regulation No 1, because that vote is unanimous. That is exactly why the path runs through states adopting it from below, where no French vote is taken.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="It costs too much: teachers, translators, forms, courts." verdict="The expensive option is the current one.">
    The cost is mostly teaching, and the teaching is already paid for: nearly every lower-secondary pupil in the Union is learning English now. What the reform adds is the right to use what school taught. The cost of not doing it never appears in a budget line: twenty-seven translations of the same form, contracts renegotiated at each border, and the companies never founded because the founders could not hire across it. No figure is put on either side here, because there is no honest one to put.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="It is already the working language. Brussels drafts in English. Why legislate it?" verdict="Then it is a formality. Do the formality.">
    Brussels drafting in English is not a citizen in Poznań filing a form in English, a contract in Lisbon standing in court without a translation, or a Bulgarian engineer hired in Vienna without a language clause. What works by habit can be withdrawn by habit, and the people who benefit from the friction have no reason to remove it. If it is already the working language, making it official costs nothing. The fact that nobody has done it is the question worth asking.
  </Objection>
</ObjectionList>

<EvidenceBox>
  Whether language is the bottleneck, or one of several beside fragmented capital markets, twenty-seven company laws and no single procurement market, this chapter does not settle; the author calls it a plausible reason, and that is the honest weight. What would settle it: a count of companies founded in the Union since 2008 that reached European scale, by the working language of their first hundred hires and the countries those hires came from, set against the same count for the United States. This chapter does not know of one.
</EvidenceBox>
