I am not against politicians. I do not think they are a worse class of human
being than the rest of us; I have met decent ones, and there are many more doing
the job quietly whom nobody writes about. My argument is about the machine we use
to pick them.

That machine has one measurable flaw: **it cannot tell competence from
popularity.** It was never built to. Ask it to sort people by how much they are
liked in a district and it does that superbly. Ask it which of them understands
energy pricing, how an army is supplied, or what a hospital's finances look
like, and it has no opinion, because nothing in it ever asks.

[Steering](/book/steering) is about how information reaches a representative
once they hold the seat. This chapter comes one step earlier in the chain: how
they were selected, and for what.

## A teacher gets the economy, a farmer gets defence

A teacher in a small town is popular, because he is a good man and everybody's
children passed through his classroom. He goes on a list, collects preference
votes, and enters parliament. A doctor does the same, because people trust
doctors. A farmer does the same, because the countryside sends someone.

Then a government is assembled and the portfolios are handed out by negotiation
between parties. The doctor may get health, which is at least a coincidence in
the right direction. The teacher may get the economy and the farmer defence —
not because anyone judged them the best available person for the job, but
because their party needed the seats filled and they were the people it had.
Those are people who should not be there.

I voted for that man because he is from my district and I like him. I did not
vote for him to run defence procurement. Nobody asked me that question, and
there was no answer I could have given.

## The missing thing is a procedure

Two separate things were decided in that sequence — *who is popular* and *who
runs what* — and there is no connection between them. The first is decided by
millions of people. The second is decided by a handful in a room over a weekend.
My vote enters the first and never reaches the second.

<Callout kind="demand" label="The demand">
  A procedure that admits competent people to an office and keeps incompetent
  ones out — enforced by the system, not by anybody's goodwill.
</Callout>

We do not have one. People hear this argument and answer with examples of good
ministers, as if a good outcome proved a good mechanism. It does not. **A system
that sometimes produces the right person by accident is not a selection
procedure. It is a lottery with a good story attached.**

Two things are missing, and they are different:

- **No path from the vote to the office.** If I identify the person in this
  country who best understands defence and vote for them, nothing in the system
  carries that judgement to the defence ministry. The vote becomes a seat, and
  the seat becomes a bargaining chip.
- **No floor.** Nothing stops a person with no background in a field from taking
  charge of it — not a hearing they can fail, not a qualification they must hold,
  not a body that can say no. Goodwill is expected to handle it: the prime
  minister's judgement, the party's sense of shame, the press noticing. Goodwill
  is not a mechanism. It works on the days it works.

The system has to do this, because everything that depends on people behaving
well eventually meets people who do not.

## Vote by portfolio

Stop electing an undifferentiated pool of people and dividing the state between
them afterwards. **Elect by field.** This is a shape, not a costed draft; the
parts of it that already exist somewhere are listed further down.

<Playbook title="The mechanism" steps={[
  { action: "Divide the ballot by field, not by district alone.", why: "Ten ministries, or thirty fields — the number should follow how the state is actually divided, not a round figure." },
  { action: "Give each field its own seats.", why: "Twenty people in one field, five in another, whatever the work requires." },
  { action: "Vote within each field, for the people judged best in it.", why: "Defence candidates stand against defence candidates; health against health. The question on the ballot becomes the question that matters." },
  { action: "Let the government form from that pool.", why: "Those elected still have to negotiate a coalition that can work together. That part is not automated away." },
]} />

What comes out is not a government. It is a pool of people, each chosen *for
the thing they will be doing*. The difference from today is not that experts
appear — some are already in the building. The difference is that the ballot
stops asking "do you like this person" and starts asking "is this the person
you want running this".

Three of the five rows deserve a sentence more:

- The Union already applies to its own executive a filter most member states do
  not apply to theirs. Every Commissioner-designate is questioned in public by
  the Parliament's committees, the scrutiny [has been strengthened
  repeatedly](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/topics/en/article/20240910STO23870/how-parliament-s-scrutiny-of-commissioners-designate-has-evolved),
  and candidates have actually been withdrawn or rejected — Buttiglione in 2004,
  Bratušek in 2014, Goulard in 2019 ([LSE's summary of the
  record](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/europpblog/2024/11/04/what-to-expect-from-this-weeks-eu-commissioner-hearings/)).
- Poland's [open list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_list) means the
  "choose a person, not a party" part is already ours, and it comes with a
  documented defect: on Finnish open lists, candidates near the top of the
  ballot paper collect disproportionately more preference votes, because
  [ballot position works as a shortcut for less informed
  voters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379421000500).
  Choosing a person is not automatically choosing well.
- Sortition — Ostbelgien's [permanent citizens'
  council](https://oidp.net/en/practice.php?id=1237) of twenty-four people drawn
  by lot, Ireland's assembly of ninety-nine — answers "how do we get people who
  were not selected for popularity" in the opposite direction: it removes the
  selection contest altogether.

So what is new here is not hearings, open lists, vetting or rejecting an unfit
candidate. Those are all in use. The new part is the combination: the
electorate votes per field, and a floor of competence is a condition of standing
in that field rather than a courtesy extended afterwards. The research found no
country that elects its executive by portfolio. That counts against the idea as
well as for it — either nobody has thought of it, which is unlikely, or it has
problems not yet met.

  <Objection claim="This is technocracy wearing a rosette." verdict="Half an answer, at best.">
    Democracy's point is that people choose their direction, including badly,
    including against expert advice — and "only the competent may stand" is how
    you get a state run by credentialled people who went to the same schools and
    agree about everything. Expertise answers *how*; it has no special authority
    about *what for*. A defence expert can tell you what an army costs, not
    whether you should have one. The answer available on this site is that
    direction is set by everybody, through the [binding
    votes](/book/direct-democracy) the author demands, and execution handed to
    people who can execute. But the line between a direction and its execution
    is not clean, and anyone who says it is has not tried to draw it.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="Who defines competent?" verdict="A reduction of the risk, not its elimination.">
    Whoever holds that pen holds the real power, and can exclude opponents while
    calling it a standard. "Relevant experience" is soft enough to fit a friend
    and exclude an enemy. One safeguard, offered here: criteria that are public,
    written in advance, hard to change quickly — and a refusal that requires a
    reasoned decision with a name on it, in daylight.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="It could create a closed profession of governing." verdict="A real failure mode, seen before.">
    If the entry conditions are formal, the route into them becomes a career
    track, and the track becomes a caste that reproduces itself. That is roughly
    what happened to the senior civil service in several European countries. A
    system meant to keep out the unqualified can end up keeping out the
    unconnected.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="The competent are not automatically honest, nor democratic." verdict="Conceded.">
    Competence and integrity are different properties. This buys a filter
    against one kind of failure and not against the other.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="The popular teacher might be right." verdict="Unpriced.">
    Somebody from outside a field can see what those inside it stopped seeing;
    every field builds its own blind spots and outsiders have broken real ones.
    A hard filter costs you those people, and nobody has said what that costs.
  </Objection>
</ObjectionList>

None of these is resolved here. The present arrangement does not resolve them
either — it hides them, by never asking the question, and then hands defence to
whoever was available on the day.

## Why I read the fifty-fifty split as evidence

This is my reading, not a measured fact. Look at how many democracies end up
split roughly down the middle, election after election. People call it balance.
I read it as the output of a machine that optimises for votes rather than for
results: a party's survival depends on being elected, not on being right, so it
positions itself where the votes are, its opponent does the same from the other
side, and two mirrored centres fall out of the arrangement almost mechanically.
In an age when a party can test in advance which sentence moves which group, the
thing being answered may no longer be what people need but what they can be
brought to want. A democracy of that kind divides people by construction,
because it has to manufacture two sides in order to run.

## Peacefully, democratically, and not by me

This has to be done by democratic means. I want the political system redesigned
so that it admits the right people to power, and I want that done through votes,
argument and persuasion — nothing else. A system taken by any other route is
worth nothing, because the next people will take it the same way.

**We are the sovereign, and this is owed to us.** We are not asking a favour of
anybody.

And I am not proposing to do this myself. I am not a candidate for anything and
I am not building a party. I am trying to persuade you that it is worth doing —
that how we select the people who run things matters more than which of them we
are arguing about this month, and that it is a question with answers, some of
which are already running somewhere in the world.
