Capital will not build you a large flat. It has no reason to. Rent is the point
of the exercise, and rent is highest where space is scarce, so scarcity is the
product. That is the housing form of the question this site asks of every
proposal — <T slug="who-captures-the-gains">who captures the gains</T> — and
this chapter is about the one actor that can decide otherwise, and what I think
it should decide.

It is an anti-capitalist idea and I will not pretend it is anything else:
capital drains rent out of us, year after year, for the ground we stand on. The
state can take a field in the middle of nowhere — buy it, or use land it already
holds — and say: here, on this nothing, we are building a town under new rules.
Or it sells the land for development on the condition that the new rules apply.
Either way the rules come first and the ground is cheap, which is the only
moment when any of this is affordable. Whether that makes the programme
communist is argued once, in [Who It Earns
For](/book/who-it-earns-for#is-this-communism), not here.

## Build beside the cities that exist

The empty field is the clean case. The useful case is next to a city that is
already there: take the land at its edge and build a district of towers, or of
ten-storey blocks.

<Callout kind="demand" label="The rule">
  Set a minimum height rather than a maximum — below this many floors you may
  not build here at all — and lay the tram or the metro with the sewers, before
  the first resident moves in.
</Callout>

That inversion is the whole policy in one line. Every planning system I have
lived under caps how high you may build; cap it the other way. And the rail is
not promised for the second phase, not "subject to future funding", because a
district that opens without rail is a district of cars forever, and you will
not retrofit it later at any price you are willing to pay.

## Space is the value

I want large flats. Eighty square metres, a hundred, a hundred and twenty. A
hundred and sixty. Two hundred.

That sounds extravagant until you ask what it is for. A family needs rooms. A
person who works from home needs a room that is not the kitchen table. And part
of a flat does not need to be heated to twenty-one degrees all winter: a
workshop space, heated lightly or not at all, plus a terrace. Once you stop
insisting that every square metre be a heated living room, square metres get
much cheaper — and [cheap is the goal](/book/cheap-is-wealth), not a side
effect — and you can hand people the thing they actually lack, which is room.

We are animals that did not grow up in a cave. We spent almost all of our
history outside and almost none of it there now, and I suspect that is why our
eyes are going: we look at close things all day. So when I ask for terraces,
distance to look at and something green at the bottom of the building, I am not
asking for decoration.

<SideNote label="What the research found">
  The suspicion has support: a three-year randomised trial in Guangzhou found
  that forty minutes of outdoor time at school cut the incidence of myopia in
  first-graders from 39.5% to 30.4%
  ([JAMA, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2015.10803)), and
  the [Cochrane review](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013549.pub2/abstract)
  treats time outdoors as a modest, uncertain preventive measure. Offered, not
  load-bearing.
</SideNote>

And the way to have space without eating the countryside is to build high.
Density and space are not opposites; tall buildings are how you get both. We
have no third continent of land to spread onto.

## A standard is due

Here is the spine of the argument, and it is not about towers.

After the industrial revolution we worked out what a person must be given so
that the institutions we depend on — family, school, medicine — could function
at all, and wrote it into building law. The research behind this chapter puts a
date on that: Britain's [Public Health Act of
1875](https://www.cieh.org/about-us/history-of-cieh/150-years-of-public-health-act/)
obliged local authorities to set byelaws, and new housing had to have drainage,
ventilation, a toilet, minimum space between buildings. Those were not luxuries.
They were the conditions under which the institutions could work, written into
law because exhortation had failed for fifty years.

That standard is now out of date. It says nothing about prevention, about
whether a body gets moved during the day, about daylight or distance or room to
work at home, or whether a child can get outside without being driven. We know
much more about what breaks people than we did in 1875 and have written almost
none of it into the rules for how we build.

So write a new one. Movement, space, daylight, transport and green ground
designed in at the start rather than retrofitted by a later generation that
cannot afford it. The towers and the large flats are what it looks like when
you take that seriously.

Two items in the right-hand column are Polish, or nearly. Allotment gardens —
*ogrody działkowe*, [tens of thousands of
hectares](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/10/11/3829) of them in and around the
cities, plot by plot — give a person in a flat a piece of ground, a shed, a few
fruit trees. I recommend them without reservation; if other countries want one
Polish institution, take this one. And a gym in every building: not a fitness
industry, not a membership, a room in the block, open late or around the clock,
with weights and a bike and a window that looks out at distance. It costs almost
nothing at construction and is nearly impossible to add afterwards. Schools
still have physical education; nothing in adult life replaces it.

## Asia already did this

<KeyFigure value="77%" label="of Singapore's residents lived in flats built by the Housing and Development Board in 2023, most of them owning the flat they live in" source="Statista, 2024" href="https://www.statista.com/statistics/966747/population-living-in-public-housing-singapore/" />

Singapore is the case everyone knows: a state agency built the housing stock of
a country. Malaysia is the example I keep coming back to, and here the checkable
and the seen have to be separated.

What the record shows: new homes launched in Kuala Lumpur and the Klang Valley
average [around 827 square
feet](https://www.edgeprop.my/content/1471152/five-things-you-probably-did-not-know-about-size-malaysian-homes),
mostly high-rise, while Malaysian condominiums commonly run [100 to 120 square
metres with three bedrooms](https://www.teoalida.com/world/malaysia/), and
larger units exist in quantity.

What I saw: they built those towers while labour was cheap, and they are going
to be glad of it when they are old. The flats are large and pleasant, the cities
are dense, everything is close. The country has obvious faults — it is not a
place you walk, the pavements stop and start, you get about on a scooter. But
pavements can be rebuilt later, once a city learns to run itself. Towers cannot
be built later, not easily and not cheaply. They put the expensive, irreversible
thing in first and left the cheap, fixable thing for afterwards. That ordering
is correct, and we have it backwards.

<ObjectionList title="The objections, which are good ones" intro="The four the research behind this chapter found strongest. The answers are offered; where a number was missing it has not been invented.">
  <Objection claim="Who pays?" verdict="Unanswered until someone costs it on real land.">
    Land, rail and buildings, at scale, up front. The number is not in this
    chapter and will not be invented. What can be said about the shape: buying
    the field before it is a town is the cheapest moment to buy anything, and
    the rise in its value afterwards is created by the public decision to build
    there. Whether that uplift can fund the works is the question that decides
    whether this is a plan or a wish.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="People do not want towers." verdict="A convenient answer; build some and see.">
    Survey after survey finds Europeans saying they want a house with a garden.
    Part of the answer is that the choice on offer today is a house far out or a
    small flat in town, and a large flat in a well-served dense district has
    rarely been one of the options. That is a convenient answer, and the way to
    test it is to build some and see who moves in and who leaves.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="Europe did this before, and it went badly." verdict="It went both ways, and the height was never the variable.">
    Britain built [32 new
    towns](https://www.tcpa.org.uk/resources/explore-the-uks-new-towns/) between
    1946 and the 1970s, now home to about 2.8 million people: not a catastrophe,
    not loved, criticised as paternalism from the first week, with rents that
    excluded many of the poorest. France's *grands ensembles* went far worse —
    built fast, left without services or transport, now [synonymous with poverty
    and exclusion](https://metropolitiques.eu/Grands-Ensembles-Retracing-a.html),
    many demolished. Vienna is the counter-case: the city has been the landlord
    for a century and [roughly 60% of
    residents](https://socialhousing.wien/policy/the-vienna-model) live in
    municipal or subsidised housing without any of it being a byword for
    failure. The common failure is building the housing and not building
    everything else — which is why the tram goes in on day one.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="A mistake in a standard is a mistake repeated ten thousand times." verdict="The risk to take most seriously.">
    A mistake in a single block is a bad block. A mistake in a standard is
    repeated in concrete for eighty years. Cheap construction, bad management,
    one monotonous design, one income group in one district — every one of
    those was a real failure mode last time.
  </Objection>
</ObjectionList>

<EvidenceBox>
  Not an essay. Two or three districts under these rules — minimum heights,
  large flats with unheated workshop space, rail before occupancy, a gym in
  every building, allotments at the edge — in different countries, with
  different builders and tenure models, and the cost and the occupancy
  published. Then ten years of the boring measurements: who leaves, what it
  costs to maintain, how far children get outdoors, what happens to the price
  of everything around it. If the districts empty out, the chapter is wrong,
  and it will be visible.
</EvidenceBox>

I would rather we build this while it is still cheap to build, and argue about
the details afterwards, than have the argument perfectly and build nothing. The
towers cannot be added later. The pavements can.
