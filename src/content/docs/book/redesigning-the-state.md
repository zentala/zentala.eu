# Redesigning the State

I had a dream once. We were flying through space on a ship, and we were the
fourth generation aboard. Our parents were around forty-two; we were
twenty-three, twenty-four. We were patching the ship. Pipes, mostly — with
two-part universal glue, the kind that is meant for everything, and grey
insulating tape. They leaked. The ship was breaking down around us, coming apart
while we watched. Parts rusting. A handrail came away and somebody fell.
Pressure escaping somewhere, constantly. It was hot, and we could not regulate
the temperature.

Here is the part of the dream that stayed with me. The people who designed that
ship designed it **well**. So well that the next two generations never had to
service it. One generation did not have to think about it at all, and only the
one after that started having any problems. But because it had been so good for
so long, the knowledge of how to tune the thing so it did not produce those
problems in the first place — how to maintain it, how to rebuild parts of it,
how to repair — did not survive. So the fourth generation could not cope. Glue
and tape were all they had.

It is a little like that with the European Union, and with every other political
system. A system goes out of date, because the times and the circumstances it
was built for have changed. But something else happens too: the incentive
systems get used up, or they get hacked. Everybody reaches the finish line, and
then something unsettling starts — corruption, elbowing in at the trough. At
some point competition disappears and what counts is the access you inherited,
handed down from one generation to the next.

**That is when you have to restart the game.** We are living in those times. We
will be restarting this game, and it is worth thinking hard about how to set it
up properly, so that the incentives are right for the generations after us — and
maybe even for us, if we happen to live a long time.

## The redesign is not a takeover

I want to be precise about what I am asking for, because "redesign democracy"
can be read as "capture it", and that is the opposite of the point.

We need to redesign democracy so that it serves our interests — not so that
anyone dominates it. It has to be a balance between public servants and society,
and the balance has to pay off on both sides. Good for the people who want to
work and get somewhere. Good for the people who want to serve the state well —
they should get something out of doing it well. **A motivating package for
citizens and for officials alike.**

This is not a generous afterthought. It is the whole mechanism. A system where
serving the public honestly is a worse deal than the alternatives will select
for people who take the alternatives, and no amount of complaining about their
character changes the arithmetic. If you want competent people inside the
machine, the machine has to be worth being inside. If you want citizens to
accept a redesign, the redesign has to leave them better off. Anything that only
works for one of the two sides is not a design, it is a fight — and fights get
won back.

So: rethink the state from scratch, on both sides of the counter at once.

## Popularity contests, not contests of solutions

What counts in politics today is popularity. Popular views — you have to match
them — and that is how groups form: around popular opinions, or popular ways of
feeling. These are popularity contests. *I feel this way, you feel that way.*

There is no contest of solutions. Nobody runs one.

I am a positivist, so my question is a different one. **How do you see this being
solved, so that both you and I end up satisfied?** Where is the line? Where is
the mechanism? How do we find the core, and build from the beginning — from
values, from what actually matters to us?

Those values are under pressure now, and some of the pressure comes from the
institutions that were supposed to guard them and from the people who work
inside them, who do not respect us and who trampled the fact that we are the
sovereign. So we start from values again. Democracy, for instance: where is it,
how do you build it. Not complaining, not shouting — doing the groundwork, and
the groundwork is designing a state.

I think we can design one. I do not think it is that hard, with artificial
intelligence to help. The problem is simply that almost nobody is working on it.
The [manifesto](/manifesto) is the short version of the method: state the
outcome, state the instrument, state how you would know it failed.

## Who designs the digital state

This is the thesis I hold most strongly in this chapter, and I would rather it
were argued against than ignored.

A digital state can be totalitarian — [The Total
State](/book/the-total-state) is the long version of why. Which means it is
dangerous for officials to be the ones deciding what it looks like and what its
architecture is. They will build it so that steering us is convenient for them,
so that they can do it easily, along the path of least resistance.

**That is not malice. It is normal.** Everyone optimises the energy they spend.
Give any group the job of designing the system they will operate, and they will
design it around their own working day. You would. I would. The mistake is not
theirs; the mistake is handing them the pen.

The nation's interest points the other way, and it is worth writing out as a
design brief rather than as a complaint. A political and digital system should:

- **Extract the most value out of the state machine** — not be the simplest
  thing to steer, but the thing that produces the most.
- **Force competition and advancement among the people who govern**, instead of
  letting access harden into inheritance.
- **Reward competence**, and reward good ideas, including ideas that arrive from
  outside the building.
- **Support European diversity while integrating** — those are not opposites,
  and a system can be built to do both.
- **Leave room to experiment**, in enough places at once that we learn something
  from the failures.

Notice that every item on that list makes the system harder to operate from a
console and better at producing outcomes. That tension is the point. If a
proposed architecture is easy to run and produces little, somebody optimised for
the operator. Ask who wrote the brief.

## A shared core, your own deployment

Here is the shape I would build.

There is one thing worth taking from China: their regions have a lot of
autonomy. They do not have a common digital state. I would build one.

Every country and region gets its **own deployment**, and decides how to
customise it. But the **core** — where we have an enormous amount in common —
can simply be shared. That way the whole European digital state works together,
because we are all running the same infrastructure. And European digital
startups get built on top of it, on European digital infrastructure, which is
currently a thing that does not exist for them to build on.

Then the part I find more interesting than the country layer. Ideally, digital
systems would be distributed **where they are needed, rather than along
administrative borders**. An old Italian hill village where you cannot drive a
car, a Portuguese one, a town in Albania — the same city system may be the best
answer for all three. A Scandinavian port city needs a different one. Mountain
tourist towns need another; industrial towns another again. Different systems,
deployed where they fit, built and shared in common.

The same goes for legal and administrative arrangements: they can differ between
regions according to the **function** the region performs. It does not always
have to be "this is one country, so here we do it this way". It can be more
functional than that.

## An offer to Germany

Now a proposal, and I want to label it clearly: **this is my idea, not an agreed
plan, and nobody has been asked.**

What the Germans built — that whole complex German administration — could be
carried into the digital world with its complexity intact, and I suspect it
would then be comparatively light. I think the Germans could take this on. At
least, it is what I would expect of them, and I think it is worth us expecting it
of them.

Germany is a bit lost right now, and we should have expectations of them. Offer a
hand and say: listen, this is the role we would like you to play. Europe is short
of that kind of leadership. As it stands, it may be the United States that says
who leads, what Germany's role is, what Germany does, what we allow them.
Germany does not entirely know what to do with itself either. So give them an
idea — one they gain from and one we all gain from. Win–win.

I assume we would end up considerably more uniform than China, and customise
where we want to. Or perhaps not uniform at all, if the function-based picture
above turns out to be the better one. I do not know yet which way that goes.

## Longevity changes the equation

One more thing, and I flag it as **speculation**: I am guessing at a future, not
reporting a fact.

It is possible we will significantly extend life, and rejuvenate. If that
happens, there probably is no labour-force problem at all in the shape we
currently argue about it — not if we can biologically rejuvenate and
bioengineering goes through a boom.

Whether *we* get access to those benefits — to bioengineering and to a long life
of decent quality — depends on whether we organise ourselves, whether we arrange
Europe so that it happens here. We are the sovereign; we have to organise it.
Waiting for one party or another to do something does not work, and not because
parties are bad — because the system is set up this way. We have to change the
settings. We have to work out which system creates the right incentives for
those in power.

## What this means for you

**Treat the system as a design, not as weather.** It was built by people, for
conditions that have since changed, with incentives that have since been used up
or gamed. Things that were designed can be redesigned. That is a more useful
attitude than either loyalty or despair.

**When somebody proposes a digital system, ask who wrote the brief.** If the
answer is "the people who will operate it", expect a system that is convenient
to operate and produces little. Ask instead what it forces: competition,
advancement, competence, diversity, room to experiment. Those are testable
questions, and they are the ones nobody in the room is being asked.

**Ask for the solution, not the feeling.** When you are handed an opinion to
agree with, the useful reply is *how do you see this being solved, so that both
of us come out satisfied?* Most positions do not survive that question, and the
ones that do are worth your time.

The ship in the dream did not fail because it was badly built. It failed because
it was built so well that nobody kept the knowledge of how to run it. We are the
fourth generation, and the glue and tape are not going to hold. Better to sit
down and design the next one.
