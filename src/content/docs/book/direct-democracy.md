# Direct Democracy

We vote every four years. For twenty years it has got worse.

That is not a mood, it is a record. Start at the crash of 2008 and follow the
line: one crisis, then another, then a third, and at the end of it a continent
talking about war rather than about what it is going to build. Every few years
we were offered a new set of names and we took it, and the direction did not
change. **How much more evidence do we need before we say that representative
democracy is not delivering for Europe?**

I am not asking that question rhetorically. I have been waiting for the election
that turns this around since I started writing about reform, and it has not
come. At some point the honest conclusion is that the problem is not which
people we elect. It is the arrangement we elect them into.

## Why it fails — the fair version

I do not have a conspiracy to offer you, and I am suspicious of anybody who
does. What I have is four explanations, and I do not know which of them weighs
most.

**Maybe some of them mean badly.** Possible, and unprovable at the scale people
usually claim it.

**Maybe they simply cannot cope.** The problems are large, slow and technical;
the job rewards speed, visibility and the next cycle. A person can be decent and
still be outmatched.

**Maybe a body of a few hundred people is just an easy thing to capture.** This
is the explanation I find hardest to argue with, and it needs no villain.
Wherever a country's decisions are made by three hundred or six hundred
identifiable individuals, influencing that country means influencing three
hundred people. That is a cheap, well-understood operation — for lobbies, for
foreign states, for anyone with money and patience. The structure invites it. Of
course it happens.

**And maybe the capable people do not go.** In Poland a well-paid programmer
earns more than the president, and does not have strangers throwing filth at him
for a living. If the job pays badly, costs you your reputation and grinds you
psychologically, you should expect a filtered pool of applicants. We built a
selection mechanism and then act surprised by what it selects.

Whichever mix is true, the conclusion is the same. This is a system problem, and
the fix has to be structural.

## Where the lever is

If I am going to fight for something, I want it to be the thing that moves the
most.

Most political energy online goes into a loop: a government does something
foolish, we name it, we prove it is foolish although everyone already knows, we
build enough outrage to raise the cost, and then it happens again next month
with a different subject. That loop is exhausting and it does not compound. So I
started asking a different question — not *what is wrong*, but **where are the
levers in this system?**

I find two.

**The first lever is direct democracy.** The ability to decide specific
questions ourselves, often, with a result that binds. It changes every other
fight at once, because it changes who has to be persuaded.

One piece of precision before I go on, because the honest name for this matters
and teaching it is part of the job. What Switzerland has, and what I am
proposing, is **semi-direct democracy**: parliament and government carry on
doing their work, and citizens hold instruments to intervene in it. Nothing is
abolished, something is added. There are two instruments and they are worth
learning by name. A **citizens' initiative** lets citizens propose a change and
force a vote on it — that is the accelerator. An **optional referendum** lets
citizens strike down a law parliament has already passed — that is the brake. I
will go on saying "direct democracy" through the rest of this, because it is
the phrase everyone uses and it names the thing we are adding. Now you know
what sits underneath it.

**The second lever is English as a working language.** That one is economic —
the floor under a European digital economy, European media, European companies
whose capital is not French or German but simply European. I make that argument
elsewhere; I mention it here because people ask which change matters most and
the honest answer is that these two are not in the same category. Language is
the economy's lever. Direct democracy is the political one, and without it the
rest is a wish list handed to people who have not delivered for twenty years.

## It is also the democratic answer to the anger

There is a reason this is urgent rather than merely correct.

I hear voices calling for aggression — against migrants, sometimes against
politicians. That anger is real and much of what caused it is real too. But
there are democratic ways to settle this, and if we do not put one of them in
front of people, the other kind of answer is what fills the space.

**Direct democracy is the democratic answer.** It takes the same anger and
points it at a mechanism instead of at a group of human beings. It gives a
person who feels ignored something to demand that is specific, legal and
winnable. Discontent with nothing to do becomes contempt; discontent with an
instrument becomes reform.

## The playbook

So here is what I think we should actually do, in order.

**1. Do not only demand that this or that policy stop.** When something outrages
the public, the reflex is to demand a reversal of that one thing. Fine — but the
wave passes and the machine is unchanged. On the wave of that anger, demand
direct democracy itself. Every scandal is an argument for it.

**2. Treat this as the real dividing line.** The division that matters is no
longer left against right. It is whether a person is willing to give power to
people or not. Demanding direct democracy is the test that shows who is actually
a democrat — and everyone in public life will have to answer it, because
refusing to answer is itself an answer. So push and vote for whoever is for it,
from any camp, and take note of who suddenly finds reasons why the public cannot
be trusted with decisions.

**3. Demand it of everyone.** Of the government, of the opposition, of the ones
you like and the ones you do not. Make it the question every politician is asked
until answering it is unavoidable.

**4. Ordinary referendums first, digital referendums later.** Paper works. Start
with the instrument that already exists and that nobody can dismiss as a
technology experiment. Digital voting comes when the state's identity and
records layer is trustworthy enough to carry it, and it makes frequent voting
cheap rather than merely possible.

**5. Decide every three months, not every four years.** This is the heart of it.
A mandate you cannot correct for four years is not control, it is a receipt.
Regular, frequent decisions on real questions are what turn a citizen from an
audience into a principal.

**6. Copy the Swiss model. Do not get clever.** Switzerland has run binding
citizen votes at federal, cantonal and communal level for well over a century.
It is the one large, long-running, working example we have of a country where
citizens routinely decide policy directly and the state still functions. I am
not going to invent numbers about it and I am not going to pretend it has no
costs — turnout varies, campaigns can be bought, and some results age badly. But
it is proven, it is European, and it is documented. **Adopt it broadly. Do not
reinvent it.** Every hour spent designing a novel mechanism is an hour not spent
demanding one that already runs.

Two things follow from copying rather than inventing, and I want them stated
rather than left to interpretation. **The votes are binding** — both the
citizens' initiative and the optional referendum. A vote that the government may
read as advice is the instrument we already have, and it is the instrument that
failed. And **the thresholds are shaped by Switzerland's** — the number of
signatures that puts a question on the ballot, the majorities that carry it —
worked out from their share of the electorate rather than guessed at, and then
rounded to something more demanding: **2% of the electorate to launch an
initiative, 1% to force an optional referendum.** The safeguards that make this
survivable come from the same place and are set out in
[safeguards](/book/safeguards).

### The safeguards we copy too

Copying the model means copying its safety mechanisms, not just its ballot
papers. Switzerland has spent well over a century building these, and they are
the answer to "what stops a crowd voting something terrible into the
constitution":

- **A double majority of people and cantons** for constitutional change, so a
  populous core cannot outvote the periphery on the rules themselves.
- **Unity of subject** — an initiative may not bundle unrelated matters, which
  kills the rider that carries an unpopular clause through on a popular one.
- **A validity check against binding international law**, so an initiative that
  would breach the non-derogable core is void before it reaches a ballot.
- **A parliamentary counter-proposal on the same ballot**, so the choice is
  between two real options rather than a forced yes or no.
- **No turnout quorum**, which sounds like a missing safeguard and is the
  opposite: a quorum makes a boycott campaign cheaper than an argument.
- **Cantonal and communal layers**, where an electorate learns the instrument on
  questions where a mistake is cheap.
- **Fixed voting dates, a few times a year**, which caps the cost and means no
  single vote is ever the last word.

[**The Safeguards We Copy Too**](/book/safeguards) maps each of these against
the thing it protects against, proposes the European and Polish equivalent with
the arithmetic shown — including where my 2% and 1% part company with the Swiss
numbers — and sets out honestly what the Swiss model costs and where it is
criticised.

**7. Demand it across the whole Union at once.** This is the part people get
wrong, and the cost of getting it wrong is historical. A single country that
reforms itself into something better while its neighbours do not is a country
that gets rolled over. We wrote the Constitution of the Third of May in 1791, we
were the second country in the world to write one, and within four years Poland
did not exist. **One state reforming alone is a target. A continent reforming
together is a fact.** So the demand is European from the first day — school
councils, municipalities, regions, national parliaments, the Union — everywhere
at once, by peoples who have understood that they have the same problem.

And if some countries do not want it? Then they do not want it. Nothing happens
to them. We aim at doing it together anyway, the same way we should aim at a
common working language: by making it obviously worth joining rather than by
compelling anyone.

**8. Understand that doing it together is part of the point.** A demand made
across the continent at the same time is itself an act of European identity —
Europeans changing something together, by their own decision. I can think of few
things that would build a political sense of Europe faster, and it is something
even a Europe of nations can own, because it takes nothing away from any nation.
It would show us how much we already have in common.

## Build something to vote for

A mechanism is not a programme. If we win the instrument and have nothing to put
through it, we will have replaced one kind of drift with another.

So the second half of the playbook is to **build technocratic political
movements across the whole Union** — patriotic and national as much as European,
because these are not opposites and pretending they are has cost us a decade.
Their offer should be real work rather than grievance: rebuild the Union
digitally, on a common working language, with digital administration, digital
healthcare, digital courts and a serious industrial programme in robotics.

That is a concrete thing to campaign on. It is also the thing most likely to
survive contact with the public, because people can picture it: an office that
answers, a court file you can actually read, a machine doing the job nobody
wants to do for a wage nobody can pay. We have roughly thirty years of working
economy left to build it. Nobody currently in charge is building it.

## Elections do not stop

To be clear about what I am not proposing: I am not proposing that we abolish
parliaments or stop voting for governments.

We keep electing representatives. We keep having parties. That is what makes
this semi-direct democracy rather than a replacement of the institutions.
**Direct democracy is how citizens force things through in between** — the
initiative to push something on, the optional referendum to stop something
passing. It is the correction mechanism,
not the replacement — and the presence of a correction mechanism changes how
representatives behave long before it is used, which is most of its value.

And if the representatives will not deliver it? Then people demand it in the
street until a government that refuses it stands down. I would rather write that
sentence plainly than leave it implied. It is a normal, legitimate part of how
democracies have won every instrument they have.

## Brexit, and what it proved

Consider what happened in Britain.

An enormous social mobilisation, years of argument, a referendum, a vote to
leave — and afterwards, immigration from elsewhere went up rather than down.
Whatever you think of the decision itself, look at the mechanics: the population
mobilised at maximum intensity, won the vote, and still did not get the thing it
had voted for.

If that is possible, then the sovereign does not have effective control over its
own state. That is not an argument about Britain. It is an argument about how
little a single vote every few years actually holds.

## The Union of Nations

What I am proposing is a **Union of Nations**.

Nations get the tools to steer themselves — real instruments, held by their own
citizens, at every level from a school council upward. And at the same time we
integrate deeply where integration actually pays: at the level of digital
administration, of how our states function as software.

Those two are not in tension, and the reason is economic. The economy we are
walking into has few workers, and the ones it has must be able to move. A worker
should be able to take a job in another member state without losing their
records, their qualifications, their healthcare history or their pension
entitlement, and without a month of paperwork in a language they do not speak.
That requires common digital plumbing. It does not require a nation to stop
being itself — which is exactly the trade that has made the last twenty years of
integration so hard to sell.

Self-steering nations, common infrastructure. That is the shape.

One thing this is not: a replacement for the Union we have. The Union of Nations
rebuilds the present Union from the inside. Nobody has to leave anything, and
nothing has to be dissolved first.

## What I cannot settle, and what would settle it

I would rather name the holes than paper over them.

- **Legal force.** What it takes for a binding citizen vote to have real effect
  at national and at European level, and what the existing citizens' initiative
  instrument can and cannot do today. A lawyer could answer this; I cannot.
- **Which questions.** Where the boundary sits between what citizens decide
  directly and what needs people working on it full-time. I am not proposing a
  weekly referendum on subjects nobody has read about, and I do not have a
  principled line to offer yet.
- **Capture of the instrument itself.** A referendum can be bought, framed or
  flooded, and frequent votes multiply the surface. Which rules in the Swiss
  practice actually hold that down, and which of them transfer to a country of
  forty million, is a study somebody should do and publish.
- **Turnout and fatigue.** Voting every three months only helps if people keep
  voting. The Swiss record on this is documented and should be read honestly,
  including the parts that argue against me.
- **Digital voting.** What would have to be true of identity, verification and
  audit before a digital referendum is trustworthy enough to bind. Until that is
  answered, the paper version is the one to demand.

If you can close any of those, that answer is worth more to this argument than
another chapter of mine. I would like to be corrected in public.

## The words, in one place

These terms come up across this chapter, [the
safeguards](/book/safeguards) and [the first move](/book/first-move). They are
the standard vocabulary of the field, and using them precisely is part of what
this site is for.

- **Semi-direct democracy** — a system where parliament and government keep
  working as they do now, and citizens hold instruments to propose law and to
  overturn it. Switzerland's system, and what this site proposes.
- **Citizens' initiative** — citizens collect signatures to propose a change
  and force a national vote on it, whether or not parliament wants one. In
  Switzerland: 100,000 signatures within 18 months, for a change to the federal
  constitution. The accelerator.
- **Optional referendum** — citizens collect signatures to put a law parliament
  has already passed to a national vote, which can strike it down. In
  Switzerland: 50,000 voters or eight cantons within 100 days of publication.
  The brake.
- **Mandatory referendum** — a vote that happens automatically, with no
  signatures collected, because the subject requires it. In Switzerland: every
  amendment to the federal constitution, accession to a collective-security
  organisation or a supranational community, and emergency federal acts without
  a constitutional basis lasting over a year.
- **Double majority** — a proposal passes only with a majority of the votes
  cast *and* a majority of the cantons. It applies to constitutional change and
  to joining supranational communities, not to the ordinary citizen veto.
- **Unity of subject** — an initiative may not bundle unrelated matters
  together, and may not mix a general proposal with a specific draft. Breach it
  and the Federal Assembly declares the initiative invalid. It kills the rider.
- **Turnout quorum** — a rule making a result valid only if a set share of the
  electorate takes part. Switzerland has none; Poland requires over half of
  those entitled to vote, which is why Polish referendums keep producing
  nothing. A quorum makes a boycott cheaper than an argument.
- **European Citizens' Initiative (ECI)** — the Union's existing instrument: 1
  million signatures from at least 7 member states within 12 months, after
  which the Commission must formally respond and is not obliged to propose
  anything. A citizens' initiative in name, with no binding force.

## We are the sovereign

Underneath all of it is a claim of right, and I want to state it as a claim
rather than as a complaint.

We are the sovereign. Not the parliament, not the administration, not the
governments that have failed us in one country after another. The power is ours
and it is lent. Given what the last twenty years have produced, taking more of
it back is not a radical act — it is the ordinary response of an owner who has
watched a manager underperform for two decades.

So: demand direct democracy. From everyone, everywhere, at every level, across
the whole Union, starting with the next thing that makes you angry.
