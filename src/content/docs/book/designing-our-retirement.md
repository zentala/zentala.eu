Everyone talks about the demographic crisis. Nobody does anything about it.

That sentence is the reason this site exists. We have known the numbers for
thirty years. Birth rates across Europe are below replacement. The generation now
in its thirties will retire into a continent with far fewer workers than
pensioners. This is not a forecast that might not happen — it already happened,
in the maternity wards, decades ago. The people who will not be working in 2056
were not born in 1996.

And still the response is a shrug, a headline, and a return to quarterly
business.

## The thing that connects us

I was looking for a problem common to the whole Union. Not a Polish problem, not
a German one — something every member state has, whatever its politics.

That search had a practical motive. The Union is transforming, and it is
transforming toward the right and toward the national. I take that as given
rather than as something to argue with. If we want to stay together through it —
and from where I sit, in Poland, staying together is the priority — we need
something everyone can look at and call sensible, regardless of how they vote.

Demographics is that thing. Fertility is below replacement in every member
state. No national policy has reversed it anywhere. It is the one condition we
genuinely share, which makes it the strongest available argument for acting
together.

## What actually breaks

A pension is not a savings account. Whatever the paperwork says, it is a claim on
the work of people who are alive when you retire. Money buys nothing by itself;
it buys somebody's labour, or the output of somebody's machine.

So the question for 2056 is not whether the pension fund has enough zeros in it.
The question is: **who is going to do the work?**

Somebody has to care for an eighty-year-old. Somebody has to grow, move and
deliver food. Somebody has to run the water, the power, the waste, the buses.
When the working-age population shrinks and the dependent population grows, those
services do not merely get expensive — they stop being available. You stand there
with money and nobody comes.

That is the specific thing worth being afraid of. Not an abstraction about GDP: a
pension that buys nothing, because the system still assumes workers who no longer
exist.

## Two sources of work, and only two

If the work has to be done and there are not enough people, the labour can come
from exactly two places: **more people, or machines.**

Europe's reflex has been the first. It is failing, and Europeans are turning
against it — not marginally, but decisively, election after election. The honest
version of why has three parts, and none of them is comfortable.

It does not add up economically. Migrants arriving without the skills the economy
needs cost more in support than they contribute, and the highly skilled ones, who
are genuinely scarce, do not come to Europe to work in elder care.

It has not worked at scale anywhere. We cannot point to a European country where
large-scale immigration solved a labour shortage without creating problems that
now dominate its politics. And we would need it at scale — millions, not
thousands.

And there is something rotten in the premise. Planning to import people so they
can do the intimate, physical care work we will not do ourselves is not a policy
anyone should be proud of. It treats human beings as an input for the job we
consider beneath us.

I should name my own bias here. I am a programmer, so I see a nail everywhere,
because I have a hammer. Read what follows knowing that.

## The case for machines

We are lucky in one specific way: the technology arrived in time.

Robotics is not a bet on something that might exist one day. Machines that move,
sense and manipulate the physical world are being deployed now, and the humanoid
form — the one that matters for care and for services built around human spaces —
is close enough that the open question is no longer *whether*. It is whether
Europe deploys it at scale, and whether ordinary people see the benefit.

The targets, roughly in the order they are ready:

- **Municipal and communal services.** The most mechanical, the most heavily
  staffed, the first to lose workers. Cleaning, maintenance, waste, basic
  logistics.
- **Food production and delivery.** Autonomous farming and autonomous transport
  are already the most advanced civilian robotics we have.
- **Predictive medicine and healthcare.** The cost that grows fastest as a
  population ages, and the field where prevention beats treatment by the widest
  margin.

Said plainly, as a strategy a country could adopt: **we will automate as much of
old-age provision as we can, so that the few young people left are not crushed
carrying it.** And alongside it, systems that let older people support each
other, live together, work if they want to, and stay connected — because being
cared for by a machine and being alone are not the same thing, and we get to
design which one we build.

We are building the world we will retire into. That is a strange sentence to
write at thirty-five, and it is the most practical thing I know.

## Who pays for it

Two answers, and both turn the question around.

**We already pay.** Twenty-seven member states each fund their own
digitalisation, their own systems, their own procurement, their own mistakes.
Doing it once, together, is not a new budget. It is a less wasteful use of the
one we have.

**And we already saved.** Pension contributions are money set aside for exactly
this future. Part of it can be invested in building the robotics that will serve
that future — so that we *own* it, publicly, rather than renting it later from
whoever got there first. Unlike most pension investments, this one delivers the
service itself: machines paid for by contributions doing the work those
contributions were meant to buy.

**And ordinary life during the transition is paid for the way it is paid for
now.** People asking who funds thirty years of daily living are asking a
question that already has an answer: we live on what was paid for earlier, and
nothing about that changes. What changes is whether there is anything to buy at
the end of it. There will be, if we spent those thirty years building rather
than destroying, and if we built cheaply rather than expensively.

## Who ends up owning it

This question decides whether any of the above is worth doing.

If automation replaces the labour our pensions depend on and the returns flow to
a handful of owners, we will have solved production and destroyed the
distribution that made solving it worthwhile. A continent with abundant robots
and no purchasing power has not fixed its demographic problem.

My position is that ownership is the second question, not the first. Models will
be mixed, and they should be: public or cooperative for municipal services,
private where competition works, dividend-based inside companies. Arguing about
the ideal structure in advance is how twenty years get spent.

**What matters more is that robots are cheap.** Affordable, easy to implement,
built on open standards, hard to monopolise. Technology that cannot be enclosed
and rented out distributes its benefits almost by default. Technology that can
be enclosed will be, whatever the ownership law says.

## The window

We are not old yet. We have roughly thirty years of functioning economy — enough
tax base, enough workers, enough industrial capacity — to build the thing that
will carry us once we have none of them.

This is the last generation that can pay for it. That is the urgency, and also
the reason for optimism: this is not a catastrophe we can only brace for. It is a
construction project with a deadline.

## What this means for you

If you are an engineer in Europe, you are not the audience for this argument. You
are the condition of it.

None of it waits for an institution to approve it. Cheap robotics, open
standards, automated public services, a digital state worth the name — these get
built by people who decide to build them, and they become politically possible
when enough people obviously want them. That is how such things have always
happened. Nobody was given permission first.

So: the wall is real, the date is roughly known, and for the first time in
history the tools exist. What is missing is a decision to treat this as a
construction project rather than a topic.

Start by carrying the argument. Then pick a piece and build it.
