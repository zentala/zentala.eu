# Cheap Is Wealth

When something is expensive, nobody can afford it and the people who supply it
take the gain. When it is cheap, you can buy it, and everybody ends up better
off: the people at the bottom live well, and the people above them live better
still.

That sentence sounds obvious and almost nothing in our industrial policy acts on
it. We subsidise producers, protect margins, and count success in the size of a
sector rather than in the price its customers pay. I want to turn that around.
**Making things cheap is the goal, not a side effect of chasing some other
goal.** European transport has to be cheap and automated. Made-to-measure goods
have to be cheap. Care hours have to be cheap. If the machines arrive and prices
stay where they are, we built them for somebody else.

[Who It Earns For](/book/who-it-earns-for) argues that the system we build must
produce a surplus that reaches society. This chapter is about the other half of
the same problem: the surplus reaches people mostly through the price, and the
price is decided by whether we deploy at scale or keep the thing rare.

## Rare means they win, cheap means we win

Take any new capability and follow it two ways.

Keep it rare and it stays expensive. A handful of firms supply it, they set the
price, and the value of the invention lands on their balance sheet. Everyone
else reads about it. That is not a moral failure by anybody; it is what scarcity
does.

Push it to mass scale and it gets cheap. The invention is still worth the same,
but now the value is spread across everybody who uses it, because they each keep
the difference between what it used to cost and what it costs now. Wealth in a
society is mostly that accumulated difference — the long list of things that
used to be a luxury and are now unremarkable.

So "democratising the benefits of technology" is not a slogan about ownership
papers. In the first instance it is a decision about volume and price. Build for
scale, keep the standards open, refuse to let the thing be enclosed, and the
distribution happens through the till.

This is the same argument [Robotic
Reindustrialisation](/book/robotic-reindustrialisation) makes about machines —
cheap, open, hard to monopolise — applied to everything else we are about to be
able to make.

## Technological unemployment: build what we never offered

A lot of people are going to lose work to AI. I am not going to pretend
otherwise, and the honest chapters on this site do not.

But I am a positivist about the technological sciences, and by that I mean
something specific. **Do not put the effort into how work is being taken away.
Put it into producing value we never offered before.** That is where the
employment is. Every previous jump in productivity ended with people doing
things that did not exist as jobs beforehand, and the jobs appeared where a new
thing became deliverable, not where an old thing was defended.

So the policy question is not "how do we protect this occupation". It is "what
could people have, that they cannot have today at any sane price, and what would
it take to deliver it at scale". Search for that. Fund the search. That is a
better use of a ministry than another retraining scheme aimed at the jobs that
are leaving.

## A worked example I am building myself

I built an application that generates made-to-measure furniture through a chat.
You describe what you want, it designs it, and you order it online.

Made-to-measure furniture has always been rare and expensive. It was hard to
find someone good, hard to explain what you wanted, and the design work cost
more than the wood. Most people gave up and bought a box that nearly fitted.

I expect the startup to create **more** work for the people who assemble
furniture, not less. Once the design step is nearly free, made-to-measure stops
being a luxury and becomes an ordinary thing to order, and there will be far
more orders than there ever were. The value being delivered here could not be
delivered at scale before. That is the pattern I am describing: a cheap thing
that did not exist, paid for by volume, with hands needed at the end of it.

I should say the other half, because it is true. **This is also the uberisation
of work.** The fitter becomes a contractor taking jobs off a platform, and the
platform holds the customer relationship, the pricing and the ratings. That is a
real loss of position, and pretending otherwise would make the rest of this
chapter cheap in a way I do not intend. Both things are happening at once: more
work, on worse terms, unless we decide the terms. Which is the next section.

## Share the gains from capital, and start now

A technological revolution is when the division of the gains gets decided. It
gets decided early, quietly, by whoever is in the room, and then it holds for a
generation. We are in that room now, which is why this belongs in a political
programme rather than in a paper about the future of work.

What I want is not one new ownership law. It is **experiments in how people
organise, funded properly**: cooperatives, mutuals, employee-owned firms,
private capital, and mixed forms nobody has tried yet. Give them support and
grants, let most of them fail, and look at what the survivors do to prices and
to the people who work in them.

I think it is unfair that we subsidise private capital so heavily and put almost
nothing into cooperative forms. I want to be careful about how strong a claim
that is, so: I have not found a clean euro-for-euro comparison of public support
to cooperatives against public support to private firms in the EU, and I am not
going to invent one. What is documented is narrower and still points the same
way. The Commission's own study on state aid and access to finance for social
enterprises finds that state aid rules on access to finance — the age limits on
eligible firms, the requirement to match public money with private investment —
create hurdles for social economy enterprises; that they lean on small *de
minimis* schemes which cap their growth; and that national authorities often
avoid state aid routes altogether because the rules are complex. ([Study on
state aid, access to finance for social enterprises and recruitment of
disadvantaged
workers](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/study-state-aid-access-finance-social-enterprises-and-recruitment-disadvantaged-workers-form-wage_en);
[Commission expert group's call for
action](https://ec.europa.eu/docsroom/documents/24501/attachments/1/translations/en/renditions/native);
[state aid and social
investment](https://competition-policy.ec.europa.eu/state-aid/social-investment_en).)

A rule that pays out in proportion to the private investment you can attract is
a rule written for firms that have shareholders. A cooperative does not have
them, by construction. That is a structural tilt even if nobody intended one.

What would settle the wider claim is a straightforward piece of work: total
public support — grants, tax expenditure, guarantees, subsidised credit — per
euro of turnover and per employee, split by legal form, across a few member
states. If the numbers come back showing no tilt, I will drop the claim. Until
somebody runs them, treat it as my argument and not as a finding.

## "In effect communist, except not by central control"

I will say it before anyone else does. What I am describing is in effect
communist — except that we will not do it by central control.

I am leaving that sentence standing because both halves of it are true and the
tension between them is the design problem. The end I want is the communist end:
the gains of a productive revolution reaching everybody, rather than settling on
whoever financed the machines. The means are the opposite of the communist
means. No ministry deciding what gets made. No single owner holding it on our
behalf. Many forms competing, prices falling, people holding the power directly
and correcting the course often.

I have written out the fuller version of this argument — where it genuinely
parts company with communism, and the three terms that fit the programme better:
**predistribution**, a **citizens' wealth fund**, and the **social market
economy** already written into Article 3 of the Treaty on European Union — in
[Who It Earns For](/book/who-it-earns-for). I am not going to re-argue it here. I
only want the uncomfortable sentence on the record in my own words, because a
programme that cannot survive being named is not a programme.

## Write the proposal

[The Shorter Week](/book/the-shorter-week) is the other answer to the same
pressure: take the productivity as time rather than as unemployment. This
chapter is the first answer — take it as cheapness, and keep hunting for the
value nobody has been able to deliver yet. They are not alternatives. Fewer
hours only feel like freedom if the things you buy with the rest of your life
are cheap.

Neither happens on its own, and neither happens because a machine arrived.

I think a better vision is enough to take power honestly and democratically.
That is what an election is supposed to be: whoever has the better proposal for
the voters. Not a better campaign, not a better enemy — a better proposal.
Nobody has one for the decade we are walking into, which is an opening, not a
complaint.

We have AI. We have time, because the work is ending. So let us write the
proposal.
