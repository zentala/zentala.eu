# European English — unpublished research sidecar

Status: internal working material. This file is part of the new content tree but remains a draft and is not intended to be published directly.

## Purpose

The public article is deliberately concise. This sidecar preserves the larger reasoning space behind it so future work does not have to reconstruct the project from a polished summary.

The original idea was stronger and more political than a language guide:

- English as an additional official language in every EU member state;
- a common language for administration, business, courts, science, education and media;
- a European English Council able to resolve practical disputes such as `chips` versus `fries`;
- a way to deepen European identity while preserving national languages;
- a long-term infrastructure for mobility, pan-European companies and common political debate;
- a possible bridge between continental Europe and the wider English-speaking world, without making the UK the owner of the project.

The current proposal translates this into a staged, evidence-driven language infrastructure project. The political ambition should not be lost merely because the first deliverable is a vocabulary pilot.

## Core framing options

These are not identical proposals and should remain separate in future discussion:

1. **EU institutional English** — a drafting and terminology standard for EU bodies.
2. **European English Standard** — a public interoperability standard for multilingual Europeans.
3. **English as an additional official language** — a legal and administrative reform in member states.
4. **European English as a cultural project** — a shared identity and media space.
5. **English-first digital infrastructure** — forms, identifiers, APIs and services that can work across borders.

The first project can be tested without politically committing to all five. The eventual argument may be that the low-cost standard and infrastructure layers are a practical path toward the larger legal and cultural reform.

## What the public article currently does not say in detail

The article keeps these claims implicit and they may deserve future treatment:

- A common second language could reduce the fixed cost of serving multiple national markets, especially for small companies.
- The largest gain may come from shared digital systems, where the language layer can be versioned, translated and reused.
- A shared language can support a European media market and a European political public sphere, but only if media incentives, distribution and funding also change.
- Language is not merely a communication tool: the choice of language affects who can participate in politics, education and economic opportunity.
- The reform has distributional effects. High-proficiency, urban and younger populations benefit first; rural, older and poorer populations need explicit support.
- A policy that calls English “official” without funding teachers, interpreters, plain-language services and legal safeguards would create a status symbol, not useful access.

## Working principles

1. **Enhance, do not replace.** National, regional and minority languages remain legally and culturally protected.
2. **Interoperability before identity.** First make people understand forms and instructions; only later discuss whether a distinct European identity has emerged.
3. **Non-native users are the primary constituency.** Native-speaker intuition is evidence, not the sovereign source of correctness.
4. **Clarity beats symbolic uniformity.** `potato crisps` is better than winning a political argument over `chips`.
5. **Separate registers.** A public notice, statute, scientific paper and conversation need different guidance.
6. **Evidence is versioned.** Every recommendation has a source, sample, confidence level and review date.
7. **Do not freeze living language.** The Board publishes a recommendation layer, not a final constitution for English.
8. **Make disagreement visible.** Preserve regional variants and explain where each one works.

## Hypotheses to test

### Adoption hypotheses

- H1: A neutral EES style is accepted more readily by multilingual Europeans when it is framed as an additional capability rather than a replacement for national languages.
- H2: Users prefer explicit compounds over short regional forms when they are told the audience is pan-European.
- H3: People care more about comprehension and task completion than about whether a form is labelled British, American or European.
- H4: The strongest early adopters are cross-border workers, students, businesses, public servants, researchers and digital-service users.

### Linguistic hypotheses

- H5: A small set of lexical variants causes a disproportionate amount of avoidable confusion.
- H6: False friends and opaque translation calques are more costly than most BrE/AmE vocabulary differences.
- H7: Short, explicit sentences improve comprehension across language families more reliably than trying to standardise accent.
- H8: A two-layer vocabulary is useful: common plain words for public communication and precise domain terms for law, medicine, technology and finance.

### Political hypotheses

- H9: A council gains legitimacy if it is visibly European and multilingual rather than simply a renamed British or American authority.
- H10: The project will be attacked as linguistic imperialism unless the safeguards and benefits for national-language communities are part of the initial design.
- H11: The legal reform is much harder than the language standard; the standard can therefore act as a low-risk demonstration project.

## Research corpus plan

Use separate evidence layers. Do not merge them into one popularity ranking.

| Layer | Material | What it can answer |
|---|---|---|
| EU law | EUR-Lex, DGT Translation Memory | Which formulations are stable in legislation and translation. |
| EU terminology | IATE, institutional glossaries | Which concepts already have validated multilingual equivalents. |
| ELF speech | VOICE and comparable multilingual recordings | What people actually accommodate in cross-cultural interaction. |
| Public services | Government forms, portals, notices from 10–12 EU countries | What citizens encounter outside Brussels. |
| Business | Cross-border hiring, banking, logistics and software documentation | Which terms reduce operational friction. |
| Education | Textbooks, exams and university material | Which forms learners are trained to recognise. |
| Media | News, subtitles, podcasts and pan-European publications | Which language reaches ordinary audiences. |

Record country, first-language family, age, domain, date, register and whether the writer is native or multilingual. A raw count without this metadata will produce misleading decisions.

## Pilot design in detail

### Sample

Initial target: 12 countries, at least 5 participants per country, 60 participants total. Stratify by:

- Germanic, Romance, Slavic and other first-language backgrounds;
- younger mobile users and older public-service users;
- high, medium and lower self-rated English proficiency;
- people who use English at work and people who mainly encounter it online or while travelling.

This is a feasibility pilot, not a representative EU survey. Its job is to detect bad candidates and improve the instrument.

### Tasks

For each card:

1. show the isolated term and ask for the likely meaning;
2. show two variants in a public-service sentence and ask which is clearer;
3. ask the participant to rewrite the sentence for an international audience;
4. ask for a confidence score;
5. repeat a subset after 24 hours;
6. collect a free-text explanation when the answer is uncertain.

For spoken candidates, add an audio task with multiple European accents. Score intelligibility separately from accent preference.

### Metrics

```text
comprehension_rate       correct interpretation without help
choice_consensus         agreement on the preferred form
paraphrase_quality       preservation of meaning
delayed_recall            comprehension after 24 hours
clarification_rate       requests for repetition or explanation
task_time                time to complete the task
misunderstanding_cost    severity if misunderstood
confidence                participant's certainty
```

Do not turn these into one opaque score too early. A low-frequency term can still be excellent for a high-risk domain if its meaning is explicit and consistently taught.

## Decision algorithm

For each candidate:

1. Define the concept, not just the word.
2. List all regional variants and likely false friends.
3. Search each corpus layer separately.
4. Run comprehension testing without leading participants.
5. Estimate the cost of a wrong interpretation.
6. Prefer the shortest form that is sufficiently clear.
7. If no short form is safe, use an explicit compound or definition.
8. Publish the rejected alternatives and the reason.
9. Assign confidence and a review date.

Suggested outcome labels:

- `preferred`: default in EES for the stated domain;
- `allowed`: valid regional alternative where context is safe;
- `compound`: use an explicit multi-word form;
- `avoid`: likely to cause confusion, false-friend transfer or unnecessary opacity;
- `domain-specific`: valid only in law, technology, medicine or another named register;
- `unresolved`: evidence is insufficient or genuinely divided.

## Expanded decision areas for the next 200 cards

The first 60 cards cover obvious BrE/AmE variants, false friends and plain-writing rules. The next batch should add:

- public administration: `application`, `form`, `permit`, `certificate`, `residence`, `registration`, `appeal`;
- employment: `employee`, `worker`, `contractor`, `leave`, `holiday`, `sick leave`, `salary`, `wage`;
- mobility: `platform`, `carriage`, `coach`, `reservation`, `departure`, `arrival`, `route`;
- health: `appointment`, `prescription`, `medicine`, `drug`, `treatment`, `emergency`, `symptom`;
- finance: `invoice`, `bill`, `tax`, `fee`, `charge`, `turnover`, `revenue`, `profit`;
- company law: `undertaking`, `company`, `firm`, `shareholder`, `director`, `board`, `merger`;
- digital services: `account`, `profile`, `sign in`, `log in`, `register`, `upload`, `download`, `delete`;
- accessibility: `people with disabilities`, readable dates, units, warnings and error messages;
- education: `course`, `module`, `subject`, `degree`, `qualification`, `assessment`;
- climate and energy: `renewable`, `emissions`, `waste`, `recycling`, `energy efficiency`.

These should not be selected only because they are interesting. Select them because they occur in high-volume or high-risk cross-border tasks.

## Grammar and style backlog

Keep these as recommendations, not claims that Europeans have one shared grammar:

- active voice when the actor is known;
- short sentences and one main action per sentence;
- concrete verbs instead of nominalisations;
- explicit subjects and agents;
- careful use of articles and countability;
- stable treatment of modal verbs: `must`, `should`, `may`, `can`;
- no unexplained acronyms on first use;
- unambiguous dates, numbers, units and decimal separators;
- no idioms, culture-specific humour or unexplained sports metaphors in public information;
- avoid phrasal verbs where a high-risk instruction needs one explicit interpretation;
- distinguish legal obligation from recommendation;
- preserve the difference between a translation-friendly formal register and natural conversation.

The project should be careful with proposals to accept forms such as `feedbacks`, `advices`, missing third-person `-s` or `the document who`. Such forms may occur in ELF interaction, but describing them is not the same as recommending them in formal writing. The correct policy is register separation and intelligibility testing.

## Pronunciation policy backlog

Do not make pronunciation a first-phase standardisation target. Instead:

- teach clear consonant contrasts that materially affect comprehension;
- permit regional accents and non-native phonetic realisations;
- teach repair strategies: repetition, rephrasing, spelling and confirmation;
- test recordings with multiple European accents;
- avoid measuring “native-like” pronunciation as the main outcome;
- create a pronunciation guide for public announcements only where safety requires it.

The goal is intelligibility and accommodation, not accent erasure.

## Board architecture

Possible internal structure:

```text
European English Board
├── Evidence and Corpus Unit
├── Terminology and IATE Liaison
├── Plain Language and Accessibility Unit
├── Legal and Institutional Register Unit
├── Education and Assessment Unit
├── User Research and Comprehension Testing
└── Editorial Council with rotating country representation
```

Minimum governance artifacts:

- charter and scope;
- public decision register;
- versioned dictionary and style guide;
- evidence and corpus policy;
- conflict-of-interest policy;
- consultation and appeal process;
- accessibility and minority-language impact assessment;
- annual review and change log.

The Board could begin as a non-governmental or academic consortium, then become an EU-supported centre if the pilot demonstrates value. This avoids waiting for treaty-level agreement before testing useful tools.

## Technical system architecture

The long-term system is more than a PDF style guide:

```text
corpora + surveys + terminology sources
              ↓
        evidence registry
              ↓
       decision objects
              ↓
 dictionary / style guide / API / teaching material
              ↓
     forms, portals, contracts, media and education
              ↑
      usage reports and new test results
```

Each decision object should have a stable ID and machine-readable fields:

```json
{
  "concept": "fried potato strips",
  "preferred": "fries",
  "explicit_form": "French fries",
  "allowed": ["chips"],
  "avoid": [],
  "domains": ["food", "consumer"],
  "evidence": [],
  "comprehension": {"sample": 0, "rate": null},
  "risk": "low",
  "confidence": "hypothesis",
  "version": "0.1",
  "review_date": null
}
```

The public interface should expose definitions, examples, alternatives and the reason for a decision. The API could later support editors, translation tools, government form builders and language-learning systems.

## Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Linguistic imperialism | Explicitly preserve national and minority languages; frame EES as an additional layer. |
| Native-speaker capture | Majority multilingual governance and user testing across language backgrounds. |
| Artificial language design | Recommend existing forms; only coin or combine words when clarity requires it. |
| Brussels jargon becoming the norm | Separate institutional terminology from plain public language. |
| Unequal access | Invest in schools, adult learning, translation and assisted administration. |
| False confidence from small samples | Publish uncertainty and replicate across countries and genres. |
| Legal ambiguity | Keep EES guidance separate from authoritative legal language until reviewed by lawyer-linguists. |
| Political backlash | Start with useful tools and measurable service improvements, not symbolic renaming. |
| Stale dictionary | Version everything and schedule reviews. |
| Accent discrimination | No accent ranking; test intelligibility and repair strategies instead. |

## Open questions

- Should the public name be `European English`, `EU English`, `Continental European English` or `European English Standard`?
- Is `English as an additional official language` the right political demand, or should the first demand be a right to English-language administrative access?
- Should Irish/British spelling remain the default in EU institutions, or should EES allow a documented dual spelling policy?
- Is `fries` actually more comprehensible than `French fries` in all surveyed countries?
- Which countries and language groups are currently underserved by English-first public services?
- How should legal equivalence work when an English version and a national-language version differ?
- What minimum English proficiency is needed for a citizen to use a public service safely?
- Which parts of the standard can be implemented by software before legislation changes?
- How do we measure economic benefit without attributing every cross-border gain to language?
- How can the project include citizens who do not speak English yet?

## Sources captured so far

- European Commission press release on the 2024 language Eurobarometer: 47% of Europeans speak English as a foreign language; 7 in 10 young Europeans can converse in English; 98.3% of lower-secondary students learn English.
- EU language policy page: 24 official languages, multilingualism as a founding principle, English remaining official after Brexit, Regulation No 1.
- European Commission English Style Guide: institutional convention, Irish/British usage as current spelling reference, plain and reader-friendly writing, distinction between EU terminology and a separate natural language.
- EU Interinstitutional Style Guide: common style conventions created through cooperation among language services.
- IATE: shared terminology database used by EU institutions and bodies.
- DGT Translation Memory: aligned multilingual EU-law sentences useful for terminology extraction and translation research.
- Modiano, “Identity and standards for English as a European Union lingua franca” (2024): Europeanisation, local accents, substrate transfer, mainland-European lexis, and the case for focusing on multilingual interaction rather than native-speaker imitation.
- FRA Style Guide for authors: examples involving `controls`, `competence`, `transposition`, false friends and British spelling conventions.
