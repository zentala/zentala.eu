This is the top of the stack, and it only works because of what sits underneath
it. [The demographic argument](/book/designing-our-retirement) says why we
need it: there will not be enough people to do the work our retirement depends
on. A [common working language](/book/european-english) is what lets the
engineers who build it work with each other. A [digital
state](/book/european-egovernment) is what it plugs into. Robotics on its
own, without those, is a trade fair.

What follows is how Europe could actually get there.

## Europe needs a new industry anyway

The old engine was the car. It is being taken apart — by electrification, by
Chinese manufacturing, by a shift in what a vehicle even is. Germany knows this
better than anyone, and so does every supplier town from Bavaria to Silesia that
grew up around it.

Robotics is the natural successor, and the fit is unusually good. It needs
precision manufacturing, which Europe has. It needs industrial engineering
culture, which Europe has. It needs software, which Europe has been losing at —
and which is exactly where Central Europe can contribute something the rest of
the continent needs.

So this is not only a demographic defence. It is the reindustrialisation Europe
was going to have to attempt regardless, aimed at a problem that makes it urgent.

## What Poland and Central Europe bring

Every member state asks what it can offer the Union that the Union actually
wants. For Poland the honest answer is software, and a share in building the
machines.

The layer around robotics — control systems, fleet coordination, simulation,
safety, the unglamorous integration work that decides whether a machine is
deployable or a demo — is software work at industrial scale. It is the thing our
engineers are already good at, and it is not a subordinate role. In robotics the
software *is* the product; the metal is a commodity.

That is worth stating plainly, because Central Europe has spent twenty years as
the place where other people's systems get maintained. This is a chance to own a
layer instead.

## The path runs through the war

Here is the uncomfortable part, and skipping it would make everything above
wishful.

Europe is in a conflict with Russia, and that conflict is already doing what
peacetime industrial policy never managed: it is pushing serious money into
autonomy, sensing, drones, and the manufacturing capacity to build them at
volume. Defence budgets are funding robotics research right now, at a scale no
civilian programme was ever going to get approved.

**That capacity converts.** Autonomous navigation, machine perception,
teleoperation, ruggedised actuators, and above all the factories and supply
chains that produce them — these are the same capabilities the civilian problem
needs. A drone company and an agricultural robotics company are separated by
their customers, not by their engineering.

So the strategy is explicit rather than accidental: **stimulate the market now
through defence, and convert the sector to civilian purposes after the war.** No
committee needs to schedule the conversion. When the wartime customer goes away,
the capacity looks for a new one — and demographic collapse will be the largest
market in Europe. Industrial policy's job is to know this in advance, and to
make sure the civilian demand is waiting: procurement, standards, and pilot
deployments ready on the other side.

What this is not: an argument for prolonging anything. It is an argument for
making sure that the industrial base built under one pressure survives to answer
the next one, instead of collapsing with the contracts.

## Where the machines go first

In the order they are ready, not in the order they are exciting:

**Municipal and communal services.** Cleaning, maintenance, waste, groundskeeping,
basic logistics — heavily staffed, highly mechanical, already short of workers,
and operating in spaces a city controls. The easiest place to deploy and the
fastest place to feel the benefit.

**Food production and delivery.** Autonomous farming and autonomous transport are
the most advanced civilian robotics we have. Food security stops being
theoretical when the people who grow and move it are retiring.

**Predictive medicine and healthcare.** The cost that grows fastest as a
population ages. Robotics here means less a machine at the bedside than an entire
system that catches what would have become a hospitalisation.

**And the part that is not machinery at all:** systems that let older people
support each other, live together, work if they want to, and stay in contact.
Being cared for by a machine and being alone are not the same thing. We are
designing the world we retire into, and it should be one worth arriving in.

## Cheap beats owned

The gains from this can be captured by a handful of firms, and by default they
will be. The usual answer is to argue about ownership — public, cooperative,
private, dividends. I think that argument is second.

**The first requirement is that robots are cheap.** Affordable to buy, easy to
implement, built on open standards, and hard to monopolise. A machine that a
municipality can procure from four suppliers, repair locally, and run on software
that is not a rental does not need a complicated ownership theory to distribute
its benefits. A machine that arrives as a locked platform with a per-hour licence
will concentrate its returns no matter who signs the contract.

Ownership models will be mixed, and that is fine: public or cooperative for
municipal services, private where competition works, dividend-based inside
companies. Different machines, different answers. **Price and openness are the
things that have to be decided as policy**, because they are the things the
market will not decide in our favour.

This is the point where a European approach has a real advantage. Europe
regulates. For once, that instinct has an obviously productive target: mandate
interoperability, insist on repairability, fund open reference designs, and buy
in ways that keep more than one supplier alive.

## What has to happen

- **Treat robotics as strategic industrial policy**, the way energy and defence
  are treated — not as a research topic.
- **Plan the conversion before it is needed**: civilian procurement, standards
  and pilot programmes waiting for the capacity built under defence contracts.
- **Write cheapness and openness into the rules early**, while the market is
  small enough that the rules still bind.
- **Deploy in municipal services first**, where the public sector is both
  customer and beneficiary, and where results are visible to citizens within a
  budget cycle.
- **Let Central Europe own the software layer**, deliberately, as a matter of
  industrial strategy rather than subcontracting.

## What this means for you

If you build things — control systems, perception, fleet software, mechanical
design — this is the largest problem available in Europe, and it is not
theoretical. It has a deadline, a customer, and a funding source that already
exists.

The Union will not commission this from the top. It will happen the way
industries always happen: people build the pieces, the pieces turn out to work,
and the policy catches up to what is already running. Start with a piece.
