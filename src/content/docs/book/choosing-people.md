I want to start by clearing away the thing people will assume I am saying.

**I am not against politicians.** I do not think they are a worse class of
human being than the rest of us. I have met decent ones and I expect there are
many I will never hear about, because doing the job quietly and well has never
been newsworthy. My argument is not about their character.

It is about the machine we use to pick them, and that machine has one
measurable flaw: **it cannot tell competence from popularity.** It was never
built to. Ask it to sort people by how much they are liked in a district and it
does that superbly. Ask it which of them understands energy pricing, or how an
army is supplied, or what a hospital's finances actually look like, and it has
no opinion, because nothing in it ever asks.

[Steering](/book/steering) made the case that our representation runs on one
axis — geography — and that the channels which should carry information from
sectors into the state have been built and then left without teeth. This
chapter is about the other half of the same problem, and it comes earlier in the
chain. Before you ask how a representative is informed, ask **how they were
selected, and for what**.

## A teacher gets the economy, a farmer gets defence

Here is the ordinary shape of it, and I do not think it is a caricature.

A teacher in a small town is popular, because he is a good man and everybody's
children passed through his classroom. He goes on a list, gets a lot of
preference votes, and enters parliament. A doctor does the same, because people
trust doctors. A farmer does the same, because the countryside sends someone.

Then a government is assembled, and the portfolios are handed out by
negotiation between parties. The doctor may get health, and that at least is a
coincidence in the right direction. But the teacher may get the economy, and
the farmer may get defence. Not because anybody decided they were the best
available person for those jobs — nobody assessed that, there was no moment at
which it was assessed — but because their party needed those seats filled and
they were the people it had.

I voted for that man because he is from my district and I like him. I did not
vote for him to run defence procurement. **Nobody asked me that question, and
there was no answer I could have given.**

Look at what actually happened in that sequence. Two separate things were
decided — *who is popular* and *who runs what* — and there is no connection
between them at all. The first is decided by millions of people. The second is
decided by a handful in a room over a weekend. My vote enters the first and
never reaches the second.

## The missing thing is a procedure

So here is the demand, and it is smaller and harder than it sounds.

> **We need a procedure that admits competent people to an office and excludes
> incompetent ones — enforced by the system, not by anybody's goodwill.**

We do not have one. That is the whole point and I want it stated flatly,
because people hear this argument and answer with examples of good ministers,
as if a good outcome proved a good mechanism. It does not. **A system that
sometimes produces the right person by accident is not a selection procedure.
It is a lottery with a good story attached.**

Two things are missing, and they are different.

**First, there is no path from my vote to the relevant office.** If I identify
the person in this country who best understands defence, and I vote for them,
nothing in the system carries that judgement to the defence ministry. My vote
becomes a seat, and the seat becomes a bargaining chip.

**Second, there is no floor.** Nothing stops a person with no background in a
field from taking charge of it. Not a hearing they can fail, not a qualification
they must hold, not a body that can say no. Goodwill is expected to handle it —
the prime minister's good judgement, the party's sense of shame, the press
noticing. Goodwill is not a mechanism. It works on the days it works.

The system has to do this, because everything that depends on people behaving
well eventually meets people who do not.

## What I want instead: vote by portfolio

Here is the shape of the alternative. I am giving you the shape rather than a
finished constitutional text, and I will say plainly further down which parts of
it already exist somewhere and which do not.

Stop electing an undifferentiated pool of people and then dividing the state
between them afterwards. **Elect by field.**

Say there are ten ministries. Or thirty fields, if thirty is the right number —
the number should follow how the state is actually divided, not a round figure.
Each field has a number of seats: twenty people in one, five in another,
whatever the work requires. Then we vote **within each field**, for the people
we judge best in it. Defence candidates stand against defence candidates.
Health against health.

What comes out is not a government. It is a pool of people, each of whom was
chosen *for the thing they will be doing*. The government then forms from that
pool, by negotiation among those elected — because a government still has to be
a coalition of people who can work together, and I am not pretending that part
can be automated away.

The difference from today is not that experts appear. Experts already exist, and
some of them are already in the building. The difference is that **the question
on the ballot becomes the question that matters**: not "do you like this person"
but "is this the person you want running this".

I will not pretend I have costed this or drafted it. I have not. What I am
certain of is the direction: the ballot should ask about the job.

## Which parts of this already exist

I went looking for prior art before publishing this, because a proposal that
has never been tried anywhere should be treated with suspicion, and because
several pieces of what I am describing turn out to be ordinary practice
somewhere. Here is the honest accounting.

**Hearings that a candidate can fail — this exists, in the European Union
itself.** Every Commissioner-designate is questioned in public by the European
Parliament's committees before the Commission can take office, and the
Parliament [has strengthened that scrutiny
repeatedly](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/topics/en/article/20240910STO23870/how-parliament-s-scrutiny-of-commissioners-designate-has-evolved)
over the last two decades. It is not theatre: candidates have actually been
stopped. Rocco Buttiglione was withdrawn in 2004, Alenka Bratušek failed to
convince the Parliament in 2014, and Sylvie Goulard was rejected by committee
vote in 2019 ([LSE EUROPP's summary of the
record](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/europpblog/2024/11/04/what-to-expect-from-this-weeks-eu-commissioner-hearings/)).
So the Union already applies to its own executive a filter that most member
states do not apply to theirs. Worth sitting with for a moment.

**Confirmation hearings for national ministers — this exists too, outside
Europe.** In Kenya every Cabinet Secretary nominee is vetted in public by a
parliamentary Committee on Appointments before appointment, [a process run under
the constitution rather than by
convention](https://www.parliament.go.ke/the-national-assembly/committees/committee-on-appointments),
and nominees have been rejected. South Korea holds [confirmation hearings for
ministerial
nominees](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/politics/20260910/decision-on-appointments-of-justice-gender-ministers-to-be-made-after-confirmation-hearings-official)
as a standard step. The United States Senate confirms cabinet secretaries. None
of this is exotic. In much of Europe a prime minister simply names ministers and
that is the end of it.

**Voting for individuals rather than for a party list — this exists, and we
have it.** Poland uses [open-list proportional
representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_list): I must pick a named
candidate, and their preference votes decide who takes the party's seats. So the
"choose a person, not a party" part is already ours. Note carefully what it does
**not** do — it does not connect that person to a portfolio, which is exactly my
complaint. And it comes with its own defect worth knowing about: research on
Finnish open lists finds that candidates near the top of the ballot paper
collect disproportionately more preference votes, because [ballot position works
as a shortcut for less informed
voters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379421000500).
Choosing a person is not automatically choosing well.

**Formal qualification requirements for holding an office — this mostly does not
exist, and where it does it is thin.** The comparative work on ministerial
eligibility finds that [the rules governing who may enter a cabinet are largely
informal](https://academic.oup.com/book/32249/chapter/268436667) — party
standing, seniority, factional balance — rather than written professional
criteria. Even for judges, where you would expect it most, the United States
constitution [sets out no qualifications at
all](https://judicature.duke.edu/articles/the-u-s-constitution-and-judicial-qualifications-a-curious-omission/);
what exists instead is a strong convention. This is the piece I am asking for
and it is the piece that is genuinely rare.

**Ordinary people deciding by lot — this exists, and it is the other known
answer to the same problem.** The German-speaking community of Belgium created
[the world's first permanent citizens'
council](https://oidp.net/en/practice.php?id=1237) in 2019: twenty-four people
drawn by lot, serving eighteen months, setting the agenda for citizens'
assemblies that sit alongside the parliament. Ireland's Citizens' Assembly of
ninety-nine randomly selected people produced the recommendation that led to the
2018 abortion referendum. Sortition answers "how do we get people who are not
selected for popularity" in the opposite way to mine: it removes the selection
contest altogether.

**So what is new in what I am proposing?** Not hearings, not open lists, not
vetting, not rejecting an unfit candidate. Those are all in use. The new part is
**the combination**: that the electorate votes per field, and that a floor of
competence is a condition of standing in that field rather than a courtesy
extended afterwards. I have not found a country that elects its executive by
portfolio. That should count against the idea as well as for it — either nobody
has thought of it, which is unlikely, or it has problems I have not yet met.

## The objections, which I am not going to settle for you

I would rather hand you the strongest arguments against this than have you find
them and conclude I was hiding them.

**This is technocracy wearing a rosette.** The deepest objection. Democracy's
point is that people choose their direction, including badly, including against
expert advice — and "only the competent may stand" is how you get a state run by
credentialled people who all went to the same schools and agree about
everything. Expertise answers *how*. It has no special authority about *what
for*. A defence expert can tell you what an army costs; he cannot tell you
whether you should have one. My honest answer is only half an answer: I want the
direction set by everybody, through frequent binding votes on questions, and the
execution handed to people who can execute. But the line between a direction and
its execution is not clean, and anyone who says it is has not tried to draw it.

**Who defines competent?** Whoever holds that pen holds the real power, and can
use it to exclude opponents while calling it a standard. "Relevant experience"
is soft enough to be shaped to fit a friend and to exclude an enemy. I do not
have a clean answer. What I would insist on is that the criteria are public,
written in advance, hard to change quickly, and that refusing a candidate
requires a reasoned decision somebody can be attacked for — in daylight, with a
name on it. That is a reduction of the risk, not its elimination.

**It could create a closed profession of governing.** If the entry conditions
are formal, then the route into them becomes a career track, and the track
becomes a caste that reproduces itself and recognises its own. That is not
hypothetical; it is roughly what happened to the senior civil service in several
European countries. A system meant to keep out the unqualified can end up
keeping out the unconnected.

**The competent are not automatically honest, nor democratic.** Competence and
integrity are different properties. I would be buying a filter against one kind
of failure and not against the other.

**And the popular teacher might be right.** Somebody from outside a field can see
what those inside it stopped seeing. Every field builds its own blind spots, and
outsiders have broken real ones. A hard filter costs you those people, and I do
not know how to price that.

I am not resolving these. I am saying the present arrangement does not resolve
them either — it just hides them, by never asking the question at all, and then
handing defence to whoever was available on the day.

## Why I read the fifty-fifty split as evidence

One more thing, and I want to mark clearly that this part is **my reading and
not a measured fact**. I have not proved it and I am not going to dress it up.

Look at how many democracies end up split roughly down the middle, election
after election. People call that balance. I have started to read it as
something else: as **the output of a machine that is optimising for votes
rather than for results**.

The argument is simple. A party's survival depends on getting elected, not on
being right. So it positions itself where the votes are, and the votes are near
the middle, and its opponent does the same from the other side. Two mirrored
centres, each leaning just far enough to hold its own side. A near-even split
falls out of that arrangement almost mechanically — and if so, the split is not
telling us what the country believes. It is telling us what the competition
produced.

And in an age when a party can test, in advance, which sentence moves which
group, the thing being answered may no longer be **what people need**. It may be
**what people can be brought to want**. Those are not the same question, and only
one of them is worth a state's time.

If that is right, then a democracy of this kind divides people by construction —
it has to manufacture two sides in order to run — and then we stand around
wondering why the country is split in half. That is why my own method, set out
in the [manifesto](/manifesto), is to hunt for the things nearly all of us
already agree on and work those out properly, rather than to go looking for the
line that divides. On most concrete questions there is far more agreement than
our politics has any way of showing.

I could be wrong about this. It is a hypothesis about a mechanism, offered as
one.

## Peacefully, democratically, and not by me

The last thing, because it decides what kind of document this is.

This has to be done by democratic means. I want the political system redesigned
so that it admits the right people to power, and I want that done through votes,
argument and persuasion — nothing else, and I will not be talked out of that
condition when someone points out how slow it is. A system taken by any other
route is worth nothing, because the next people will take it the same way.

**We are the sovereign, and this is owed to us.** We are not asking a favour of
anybody. The arrangement under which we choose the people who run our states was
designed by people who are dead, for a world with no telephones, and we are
entitled to look at it and say it no longer does its job.

And I am not proposing to do this myself. I am not a candidate for anything and
I am not building a party. I am trying to persuade you that it is worth doing —
that the question of *how we select the people who run things* is more important
than which of them we are arguing about this month, and that it is a question
with actual answers, some of which are already running somewhere in the world.

If you think the design I have sketched is wrong, work out a better one. That is
also winning, as far as I am concerned.
