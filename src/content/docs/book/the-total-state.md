I want to argue two things in one chapter, and they cut against each other.

The first is that our public administration still runs on the habits of the
typewriter era, that this wastes enormous amounts of human life, and that we
could fix it inside a decade. The second is that my own complaint is unfair,
because digitising a state is not a technical job. **A state becomes total as it
becomes digital.** The more of your life runs through its systems, the more
completely somebody at a console can reach into it.

Both halves are true at once. Anyone who only says the first is selling
software. Anyone who only says the second ends up defending the fax machine. The
interesting question is what has to be built so that the first can happen
without the second eating us.

## The opportunity we are sitting on

Go into a public office and watch what happens to a piece of paper. Somebody
retypes what somebody else already typed. A form is checked against a register
that the clerk cannot query directly. A case moves at the speed of an envelope.
None of this is anyone's fault; it is a set of procedures designed before the
machines existed that would make them unnecessary, and never redesigned after.

The potential sitting in digitalisation and automation is enormous, and it is
not mainly about saving money. It is about quality of life: work that nobody has
to do by hand any more, decisions that arrive in days instead of seasons, a
citizen who can see the state of their own case. Public administration and the
courts get something extra out of being rebuilt properly — a system that records
what it did, in order, as a by-product of working at all. **Transparency you get
for free because the thing was built well is worth more than transparency
somebody has to promise.** Build it right and the case history exists whether or
not anyone is in the mood to publish it.

In Poland there is also a clock on this, and it is not a metaphor. The people
who staff that administration are leaving. My mother retires in two years. That
is roughly the window we have to reform public administration before the
knowledge that keeps it running walks out of the building with the people who
hold it — the undocumented rules, the exceptions, the "ask her, she knows".
Nobody is queuing to replace them at those wages, and most of that work does not
need a human being at all. Either we automate it deliberately in the next few
years, or it degrades by itself as the staff go.

I would not take one of those jobs. I do not know many people who would. That is
the honest measure of how long this can be postponed.

## The objection I have to make against myself

Then somebody asks me the question I cannot answer with code: **who rules the
data, and on what terms?**

Once the register is the truth, whoever controls the register controls what is
true about you. Once identity is a credential rather than a face, the credential
can be suspended. Once money is an entry in a ledger, the entry can be frozen,
expired, or made conditional on behaviour. None of those are science fiction and
none of them require anyone to have evil intentions; they are properties of the
system, available to whoever holds it next.

This is why people protest against digital ID and digital currency, and I think
the protests are more rational than their opponents admit. The objection is not
technophobia. The objection is that **a switch exists, and somebody can turn a
person off.** You do not have to believe today's government would do it. You
have to notice that today's government is not the last one.

So my complaint about the typewriter-era state was unfair — not wrong, unfair.
Digitalisation was never a project for engineers to deliver and politics to
approve afterwards. The governance question is the harder half, and it has to be
settled first, in public, by the people whose lives run through the thing.

## Why a digital state is a total state

I use "total" carefully. I do not mean totalitarian. I mean total in reach: a
state that touches everything, sees everything, and can act everywhere at once.

The old state was limited by friction. Surveillance cost people. Enforcement
cost time. If a government wanted to make your life impossible, somebody had to
do the work, and the work was expensive enough that it was reserved for people
who mattered. Those costs are what protected ordinary citizens — not laws,
friction.

A digital state removes the friction. That is the whole point of it, and it is
why it delivers real benefits: the same property that lets a service reach you
in a second without an office visit lets a decision about you take effect in a
second without a hearing. You cannot keep one and refuse the other. They are the
same capability seen from two ends.

**The conclusion is not to stop building.** A state that refuses to digitalise
does not stay free; it stays slow, and eventually it buys the systems anyway,
late, from someone else, on someone else's terms. The conclusion is that the
power has to be balanced at the same time as the capability is built.

## The counterweight

The balance I want is direct democracy, at every level where a decision is made:
the school, the municipality, the region, the nation, the Union.

Not as a mood, and not as consultation. As two mechanisms with teeth: a
**citizens' initiative** to propose a change and force a vote on it, and an
**optional referendum** to strike down a law parliament has passed. The
accelerator and the brake. Parliaments carry on — the proper name for the
arrangement is semi-direct democracy — but citizens decide specific questions,
often, and the result binds. Then the relationship
inverts. A state with total reach and citizens who can overrule it between
elections is a tool. A state with total reach and citizens who can only complain
for four years is something else, and we should say the word out loud rather
than discover it later.

From that follows a demand I would defend in front of anyone:

**Digital identity should arrive together with universal direct democracy across
the Union.** Same package, same decade. Not the capability first and the
accountability "in a later phase", because later phases are where accountability
goes to die.

And the mirror of it, which is the uncomfortable part: **while no counterweight
ships alongside these systems, citizens are entitled to resist them.** Not
because resisting is wise — I think refusing digitalisation forever is a losing
position — but because the resistance is a correct reading of the deal on offer.
Governments across Europe have spent two decades failing at the ordinary things,
crisis after crisis, and are now asking for more direct power over people's
lives. Declining that trade is not superstition. It is arithmetic.

I would rather the demand were the other one: give us the vote and we will help
you build the system.

## Keep the paper

There is a cheap way to take most of the fear out of this, and I do not know why
it is not the default position.

**Keep paper as the emergency layer of the state.** Not only for voting — for
the basic functions generally. The procedures we run today on paper stay in
place, simplified if you like, as the fallback that works when the systems are
down, when somebody has been switched off by mistake, or when a government has
to be shown that the old route still exists. A state that cannot operate without
its database has handed whoever holds the database a power nobody voted to give
them.

Voting is the clearest case. We already vote in one room in a school; a town
hall can do the same. Real votes on an initiative or a law will not draw crowds so large that a
school hall cannot hold them, and if the cost of running frequent votes is the
objection, then the honest response is to work out how to run them cheaply, in
public, with the arguments on the table. That is a discussion we should be
having rather than a reason to postpone.

**Digital gets built in parallel, as learning.** We start now, we make mistakes
in places where mistakes are survivable, and the digital layer earns its way up
to anything important. It should carry something serious only once it has
proved, in the open, that it deserves to.

Build it as Europeans, together. One set of systems for identity, for records,
for voting, designed jointly, with each country deploying and running its own.
The engineering gets done once. The sovereignty stays where it is. And nobody
ends up dependent on a platform they cannot inspect.

I have not seen this done anywhere at Union scale, so I will not pretend to know
it works. What would settle it: whether a binding citizen vote can be given
legal force at national and European level without rewriting the treaties;
whether a jointly built identity system can be deployed nationally without
creating one central operator with a switch; and whether frequent voting at
municipal and regional level actually changes outcomes, which is a question the
Swiss experience can answer better than my speculation. Those are real pieces of
work, and they matter more than another chapter from me.

## What Central Europe puts in

This is also where I think my part of Europe stops being a recipient and starts
being a contributor, and I would like that said plainly for once.

Poland and Central Europe have the software engineers. Not a marketing claim — a
labour market fact anyone in the industry recognises. If the new Union needs a
digital administration, an autonomous health service, digital courts and
offices, a voting system nobody can quietly rig, then that is work we can
actually do, and it is what we should put into the common system rather than
another round of asking what we get out of it.

Germany built SAP: software that runs companies, sold to the world. **Europe
could build the system that runs a state.** Digital administration, digital
democracy, predictive healthcare, digital courts — built here, deployed here
first, and then exported, because every other democracy on earth is going to
face the same problem and most of them would rather not buy the answer from a
superpower.

That is the difference between plumbing and an industry. We should aim at the
industry.

## What this means for you

Two things are worth holding at the same time, and most public argument makes
you pick one.

**Want the digital state.** The waste in the current one is real, it costs
people years of their lives, and the staff who keep it standing are leaving
within the decade. There is no version of the future where we run a shrinking
workforce on paper.

**Demand the counterweight in the same breath.** When somebody proposes digital
identity, digital money, a national register, the question to ask is not whether
it is convenient. It is: what can I overrule, and how often? If the answer is
"nothing, every four years", the correct response is no.

That is the whole argument. Build the capability, and build the thing that holds
it, together, or you will get the first one alone — and by the time that becomes
obvious, the instrument for objecting will be running on the same system.
