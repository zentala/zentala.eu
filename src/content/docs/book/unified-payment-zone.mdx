# Unified Payment Zone

I want to send €500 from my Polish bank account to a freelancer in Barcelona. It should
take a tenth of a second. It should cost nothing. The money should arrive before I finish
closing the laptop.

Instead, depending on which bank I use, it takes up to three business days and costs
between 0.5% and 3% of the transfer amount. If I go through a third-party service like
Wise or Revolut — designed specifically to paper over this gap — I pay a fee to a
private intermediary for solving a problem the EU should have already solved itself.

This is not a technology problem. The technology exists. It has existed since 2017.
This is a legacy system problem — the same structural inertia that keeps Europe running
on processes designed for the pre-internet era.

## The Toll Booth Between Member States

The EU is supposed to be a single market. One of the four fundamental freedoms is the
free movement of capital. But when money crosses a border — even between two eurozone
members — it often passes through a tangle of correspondent banks, intermediary clearing
houses, and exchange systems that each take a cut, add latency, and introduce opacity.

For individuals, this is an irritant. For businesses, it is a structural tax. A Polish
SME with clients in Germany, France, and the Netherlands is paying a per-transaction
toll every time money moves. A Romanian freelancer with clients in Amsterdam is losing
1–2% on every invoice settled. These costs are invisible in macroeconomic statistics
but very visible in household accounts and startup balance sheets.

The Draghi report on European competitiveness, published in 2024, identified fragmented
financial infrastructure as one of the structural disadvantages holding Europe back. This
is not a fringe position — it is the formal diagnosis of the EU's own analysis.

## What Already Exists — And Why It Is Not Deployed

SEPA Instant Credit Transfer (SCT Inst) has been technically available since 2017. It
enables euro transfers between bank accounts within the SEPA zone — settled in under
ten seconds, any time of day, any day of the year. The ECB's TARGET Instant Payment
Settlement platform (TIPS) processes these transfers at a cost of €0.002 per transaction.
Not 0.2%. Two hundredths of a euro cent.

The infrastructure is there. The capacity is there. The cost is effectively zero.

So why isn't every bank in Europe using it?

Because 70 banks missed the January 2025 deadline. Despite the scheme existing for eight
years, market adoption was sluggish. Banks had no competitive incentive to invest in
24/7 infrastructure when customers weren't demanding it and regulators weren't enforcing it.
This is the European pattern: we build technically excellent systems, then fail to deploy
them uniformly because each member state and institution operates on its own timeline.

The 2024 Instant Payments Regulation changed that. Starting from January 2025 for
receiving and October 2025 for sending, all eurozone payment service providers are
required to be capable of instant payments. Fees for instant transfers cannot exceed fees
for standard SEPA transfers. This is the legislative override that the market refused to
provide organically.

Non-eurozone EU members — Poland, Sweden, Czech Republic — must comply by April 2026.

## The Digital Euro: Sovereignty Infrastructure, Not a Consumer Product

The digital euro is scheduled for a first issuance in 2029. The ECB estimates the build
cost at approximately €1.3 billion, with annual operating costs of around €320 million.
This is not cheap. But the argument for it is not primarily about cost — it is about
strategic autonomy.

Visa and Mastercard handle approximately 70% of EU card payments. Both are US companies.
Alipay and WeChat Pay are Chinese. Europe's retail payment infrastructure — the pipes
through which everyday economic life flows — is owned by non-European entities subject
to non-European jurisdiction.

I find this alarming in a way that has nothing to do with technology. It is the equivalent
of having your electricity grid controlled by a foreign power that can flip a switch.
Sanctions, geopolitical crises, and policy changes in Washington or Beijing all become
leverage points against European economic activity. This is not a hypothetical — it has
happened with payment systems in other contexts.

The digital euro, if designed correctly, would be a direct claim on the ECB — like cash,
but digital. A public, sovereign payment rail that does not extract interchange fees and
does not depend on the willingness of foreign corporations to keep processing European
transactions.

## The Privacy Design Problem

I have one serious concern about the digital euro, and I want to name it directly rather
than bury it in a footnote: surveillance risk is real.

The ECB's stated design includes cash-like privacy for offline small transactions and
tiered privacy for online payments. The holding limit is set at €3,000 per person, with
automatic overflow to commercial bank accounts. The ECB says it will not track individual
transactions.

I believe the current ECB leadership means this. I am less confident about what a future
government in a future political climate would do with the same infrastructure. The
technical architecture that enables privacy by default also enables monitoring by policy
change. China's experience with e-CNY is instructive: even in a less privacy-conscious
society, users resisted government-visible payment monitoring. European users — with
stronger civil liberties traditions — will be more sensitive, not less.

The digital euro will need legally binding technical constraints on surveillance, not just
policy commitments. Privacy architecture should be built into the protocol, not bolted
on as a promise.

## What Unified Means

A Unified Payment Zone is not just "instant payments exist somewhere." It means:

**Universal deployment** — every bank account in the EU can send and receive instant
payments. Not 67%, not "all major banks", but every account. The 2024 regulation is a
start. Enforcement with real penalties is the follow-through.

**Uniform pricing** — sending money from Warsaw to Barcelona costs the same as sending
money within Warsaw. Regulation 924/2009 already mandates this for card payments. The
same logic should apply to all euro transfers across the single market.

**Cross-border reach** — the SEPA zone currently covers 36 countries. But real-time
cross-border settlement between non-eurozone EU members and eurozone members is still
not seamless. The regulatory framework needs to catch up with the geographic reality.

**Sovereign infrastructure** — a payment rail that is publicly owned, interoperable,
and not dependent on private US or Chinese companies to keep European commerce running.

This is infrastructure. Like roads, like the electrical grid, like the internet backbone.
Nobody asks whether roads should turn a profit on each journey. The economy runs on them.
Payment infrastructure is the same category of thing.

## The Pattern We Keep Repeating

The EU builds excellent technical standards and then deploys them incompletely. SEPA was
created in 2008. Instant SEPA was created in 2017. Full mandatory deployment is arriving
in 2025–2026. That is an eight-year gap between a working protocol and universal
availability — eight years during which European businesses paid unnecessary fees and
European freelancers sent money through private intermediaries.

I am not interested in building new systems. I am interested in actually deploying the
ones we have, uniformly, with deadlines and consequences for non-compliance.

The Unified Payment Zone is not a vision of the future. The technical foundation exists
today. What is missing is the political commitment to treat payment infrastructure like
the public good it is — and to enforce that commitment with the same seriousness we
applied to GDPR.
