The [manifesto](/manifesto#the-work-is-going-away-and-that-is-the-time-we-need)
says the work is going away and that this is the time we need. This chapter is
the practical form of that sentence. If the time is real, somebody has to say
where it comes from, who pays for it and what it is for. Otherwise it arrives
as unemployment, which is the same hours with none of the money and none of the
dignity.

There are two ways a society can receive a jump in productivity. It can take it
as fewer people working, with the rest thrown out and told they failed. Or it
can take it as everyone working less. The second is a choice, not a mechanism.
Nothing about a machine decides which one you get.

## Lifelong learning is a slogan until you free the days

Every serious document about the next twenty years says the same thing: people
will have to keep learning, skills will go stale faster than careers, and we
are genuinely asking whether we can keep up with AI at all. Then the same
document leaves the working week at five days and says nothing more.

You cannot learn a new trade in the evening after a full week, with children in
the house. Almost nobody does. Lifelong learning is not a question of will or
of platforms or of another funded programme of certificates. It is a question
of days. If we mean it, we have to take the days out of the week and give them
to people.

<Callout kind="demand" label="The demand">
  Two days a week for something else. Study, if you want to study. Your own
  projects, if you are building something. Your children. Rest. It is not the
  state's business which. The state's business is that the days exist.
</Callout>

## Where to cut, and why there

Not everywhere at once, and not by decree in the abstract. Cut the week where
the machines demonstrably took the work.

Start with services, where AI already took a great deal of it — support, back
office, translation, routine analysis. The output is being produced with fewer
hours. Shorten the week there by a day and the same work still gets done,
because it is already getting done that way.

Then the factories, as the robots arrive. [Robotic
Reindustrialisation](/book/robotic-reindustrialisation) argues that Europe has
to build those lines anyway, because we will not have the workers. When a line
is automated, the hours it used to need do not disappear from the economy; they
disappear from somebody's life. Shorten the week on that line.

The end state I have in mind is four days for most people and three for some.
Not one uniform week. A range, with four as the default.

## Pay: make four days the normal week in law

The objection is immediate and fair: a four-day week where the fifth day is
simply unpaid is a pay cut of a fifth with a friendly name. That is how this
reform fails.

The answer is to move the legal anchor. **Redefine the minimum wage as pay for
four days, not five.** The fifth day is paid on top. A person working five days
earns more than a person working four, but the floor — the number the law
protects, the number a full-time job is measured against — covers four.

That is the whole mechanism: a set of incentives arranged so that the cheapest,
most ordinary thing an employer can do is offer four days. Today the incentives
point the other way, and that is the only reason five days feels natural.

## Flexibility that runs both ways

A shorter week must not become a new uniform. Some people want more hours, not
fewer.

Take my own case. I have not worked for seven months. I spend my days building
things with AI — including this site. In the system I am describing I could go
and work Friday, Saturday and Sunday at weekend rates, three long days, and
keep four for what I am actually doing. I would earn a living and keep the
project. Right now the labour market offers me either a full week or nothing,
so I take nothing and live off savings. That is not flexibility; that is a
market with one setting.

And there are people on the other side of it — building a business, saving for
something, simply wanting the money — who should be able to take five days or
six and be paid properly for them. The point is not to force anyone into
leisure. The point is that the default should be four, and that the deviations
in both directions should be priced honestly.

## Why this is not about leisure

This site argues for [direct democracy](/book/direct-democracy): that citizens
should hold an initiative and a veto over the laws that govern them, and decide
the direction of the Union rather than ratify it every four years. The
strongest objection to that argument is that people do not have time to think.
They are tired, they are working, they will vote on a headline.

That objection is correct under a five-day week. It is a statement about
schedules, not about people's intelligence.

A citizenry that decides things needs time to read, argue and change its mind.
Nobody is going to hand that time over out of civic good will. It has to come
from somewhere, and the only place it can come from is the working week — which
is precisely where automation is about to create a surplus. The freed hours and
the democratic deficit are the same problem seen from two sides. The same goes
for the rest of what this book asks for: rebuilding the state, [paying for our
own retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement), learning fast enough to stay
useful next to the machines. All of it needs a population with hours to spare.
We are about to have the hours. The question is whether anyone arranges them
into anything — and whether the gains that free them reach the people whose
hours they were, which is the question of <T slug="who-captures-the-gains">who
captures the gains</T>, argued in [Cheap Is Wealth](/book/cheap-is-wealth).

<ObjectionList title="The holes, in order of size" intro="Three objections the proposal has not closed. The first is the real one.">
  <Objection claim="A nurse's output is hours." verdict="The genuine hole in the argument.">
    A nurse, a bus driver, a carer, a teacher in a classroom: their output is
    not going to be automated in the next decade, and a four-day week there
    means hiring more of them, in professions already short-staffed. Two honest
    answers exist — pay much more and treat the extra staff as a public
    investment, or accept that the shorter week arrives later in those sectors
    than in the automated ones — and the chapter does not choose between them.
    Anyone saying it is costless there is not being straight with you.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="Who pays for the fifth day?" verdict="The cut follows the automation, sector by sector.">
    If the productivity gain is real, the gain pays for it — plausible in an
    automated service company, not in a firm where the machines have not
    arrived. Cutting the week there means raising prices or cutting profit, and
    somebody has to say which. That is why the demand reads "where the machines
    demonstrably took the work" and not "everywhere by 2030". What counts as
    demonstrably, and who measures it, is open.
  </Objection>
  <Objection claim="A floor can be eroded." verdict="Needs closing, by someone who knows the law.">
    The minimum-wage redefinition moves the floor rather than relying on good
    employers, which is the right shape. But a floor can be got around by hiring
    people as contractors, splitting jobs, pushing work off the books. Anyone
    who has watched labour law knows those moves. They would need closing, and
    how is not in this chapter.
  </Objection>
</ObjectionList>

## What has actually been tried

The evidence exists and the research behind this chapter found it; here is
what it shows and what it does not.

The honest caveat, which the headlines dropped: Iceland's trials cut the week
to roughly 35–36 hours, not to four days, and [researchers writing in The
Conversation](https://theconversation.com/the-success-of-icelands-four-day-week-trial-has-been-greatly-overstated-164083)
argued the result was widely overstated for exactly that reason. The [Autonomy
and Alda report](https://autonomy.work/portfolio/icelandsww/) is evidence that a
shorter week can be done without losing output. It is not evidence for four
days.

The UK pilot is closer. [The
results](https://autonomy.work/portfolio/uk4dwpilotresults/) report revenue
broadly steady, sharp falls in resignations and absence, and large drops in
reported burnout; a [follow-up a year
later](https://autonomy.work/portfolio/making-it-stick/) found most
participating organisations had kept the policy and about half had made it
permanent. What that is: strong evidence that firms which volunteered for a
four-day week mostly kept it and did not fall apart. What it is not: evidence
about firms that did not volunteer, about hospitals and buses, or about what
happens when the week is shortened by law rather than by choice. I am arguing
for something larger than has been tested, and I would rather say so.

<EvidenceBox>
  Not an essay. Pick a sector where AI has measurably taken hours, cut the week
  there with the four-day minimum wage attached, and publish output, wages,
  staffing and sick leave before and after. Then do the same in a hospital and
  publish the bill.
</EvidenceBox>

I am not a politician and I do not have the legislative text. I am someone who
thinks the freed time is the largest thing about to happen to Europe, and that
we are on course to waste it as unemployment, resentment and eventually war —
which is what the [manifesto](/manifesto) says, and the reason I am writing any
of this down.
