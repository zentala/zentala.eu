## The Protocol Layer Nobody Talks About

The EU has one currency. One passport for 450 million people. A single market that allows goods,
services, capital, and labor to move freely across 27 borders. This is a genuine engineering
achievement — decades of institutional work compressed into functioning infrastructure.

And then there are 24 official languages.

I think about this like a distributed system architect. You have a beautifully designed network:
the nodes can connect, the routing works, the legal API is clean. But the communication protocol
layer is fragmented into 24 incompatible dialects. Every message sent between nodes requires
translation overhead. Every handshake takes longer. The effective throughput of the whole system
is a fraction of what the hardware should allow.

This is not a cultural observation. It is a systems diagnosis. And the cost is measurable.

## 44% Trade Friction, Built In

Here is the number I keep coming back to: **44%**.

That is the average trade uplift from sharing a common language, derived from a meta-analysis of
701 estimated effects across 81 academic studies (Egger and Lassmann, 2012). When two trading
partners speak the same language, their bilateral trade increases by 44% on average. Some
specifications in the literature reach 65%.

In other words: the EU has a 44% trade friction tax baked into every cross-language transaction
between its member states. Not a tariff — tariffs were removed decades ago. Not a regulation —
this friction exists even when the legal framework is identical. It is latency built into the
protocol. The packet loss of speaking different languages.

The EU single market analysis puts a euro figure on this. ECIPE estimates that language
fragmentation costs the EU approximately €228 billion annually in foregone goods trade, plus
€279 billion in services. That is roughly **€507 billion per year** in economic activity that
never happens because two parties cannot communicate without friction costs.

The EU spends €1 billion per year on translation and interpretation across its institutions.
The US spends nothing equivalent — because it does not need to. The €1 billion is not even
a rounding error on the €507 billion cost of the problem it is failing to solve.

## Free Movement With No Runtime Support

The EU legal framework guarantees free movement of workers. I consider this a legal API with
no runtime support. The function signature is there. The documentation says it works. But when
you actually call the function — when a Polish engineer moves to Munich, when a Czech startup
tries to hire from Bucharest — the runtime throws errors the API specification never mentioned.

Those errors are measured in years of wages lost.

Dustmann and Fabbri found that English language proficiency is associated with 21–23% higher
wages among immigrants in the UK. The IMF's analysis of the German labor market found that an
immigrant needs approximately 25 years in Germany before earning wages comparable to a native
with equivalent technical credentials. Twenty-five years. That is not a rounding error in the
labor market — that is an entire career spent paying the language friction tax.

The OECD frames it directly: language deficiency can "motivate return migration or circular
migration, even of high-skilled migrants." Europe loses the talent it legally invited in.
The free movement architecture is real. The runtime stack that would make it actually work is
not deployed.

Compare this to the US. A software engineer moving from Austin to Boston encounters zero language
barrier. Their credentials transfer immediately. They can compete in the local labor market from
day one. The EU's equivalent of this journey — Warsaw to Munich, Prague to Amsterdam — currently
costs years of earning power, regardless of technical skill level. This is not a political
problem. It is a missing infrastructure component.

## The Startup Funding Gap Is a Market Size Problem

US Series A funding rounds average $20 million. European Series A rounds average $10 million.
Half the ticket size, roughly the same risk environment, dramatically different capital access.

I do not think this gap is primarily about investor risk appetite or cultural conservatism.
I think it is a market size calculation. A US founder pitching to a US VC addresses a market of
335 million people who share a language, a legal system, and broadly a consumer culture. One
pitch deck, one go-to-market, one addressable total market calculation.

A Polish founder pitching the same product addresses 38 million people. To reach 335 million
equivalent scale in Europe, that founder needs to build separate go-to-market strategies for
German speakers, French speakers, Spanish speakers, Italian speakers, and Scandinavian markets —
before achieving the same addressable scale the US founder gets for free. VCs are pricing this
complexity into the round size. This is rational behavior on their part. The systemic problem
is that the complexity exists at all.

I have watched this play out in the startup ecosystem. Dutch founders in the Sifted research
specifically noted that "operating in any language other than English would have severely limited
the company's ability to trade internationally." The founders who have figured this out switch
to English early. They are hacking around the missing protocol layer. The hack works at the
individual company level; it does not solve the structural problem for the other 99% of European
SMEs that do not reach that decision point.

EU SMEs lose an estimated €100 billion in export contracts annually due to inadequate language
skills. That figure is from 2006 and has not been revised — but EU enlargement since then has
added more language-pair complexity, not less.

## The EU Already Knows This. It Just Has Not Said It Out Loud.

Here is the detail I find most revealing: 86% of European Commission internal documents are
written in English. The institution that officially maintains 24 languages and funds a €1 billion
annual translation apparatus runs almost entirely on one language internally.

This is not hypocrisy. It is evidence. The Commission has discovered, through operational
necessity, what economists have been quantifying for 30 years: common language is the most
efficient communication protocol available, and when productivity matters, it wins. The EU's
own working practices are a natural experiment showing what happens when you remove language
friction from institutional operations.

The EU language technology study from 2025 models this directly: machine translation alone
could add €300 billion to intra-EU trade for SMEs, bringing the total potential from language
technology deployment to €360 billion. This is a proxy for what common language would achieve
natively — without the translation overhead, without the machine error rate, without the
latency. The technology is an approximation of the infrastructure. The infrastructure is what
I am proposing.

## What I Am Actually Proposing

I am not proposing to replace anyone's native language. I am proposing to add a shared runtime
layer.

Every software architect understands this pattern: you do not delete the legacy system on day
one. You define the interface, build the new layer, route through it gradually. You do not force
Polish families to stop speaking Polish. You build a Europe where every Polish child grows up
with English as natural as reading — because the economic return is measurable, the technology
exists, and the alternative is continued fragmentation of a market that should be the most
powerful economic bloc on the planet.

If common English reduced EU intra-market trade friction by 20% — well below what gravity models
predict for full language unity — the gain would exceed €100 billion annually. That is enough
to fund the EU's entire translation apparatus 100 times over, every year. The refactoring cost
is real. The ROI on completing it is larger than almost any other single policy lever available.

## What Changes for a Polish Developer or a Czech Startup

Let me be specific, because I think the argument needs to land at the personal level.

I am a Polish software architect. I know exactly what the language barrier costs me — not in
some macroeconomic abstraction, but in concrete daily friction. I know what it costs to build
something in Poland, write documentation in Polish, and then discover that my addressable market
is 38 million people instead of 450 million. I know what it costs to watch the same product,
built in San Francisco, raise a Series A at $20 million while my European equivalent is valued
at half that — not because the product is worse, but because the market it can address without
translation friction is smaller.

If language were not a barrier: a Polish developer could apply for a job in Amsterdam, Berlin,
or Stockholm and compete on technical merit from day one — not after years of language
investment. A Czech startup could launch once, in English, and immediately address the entire
EU market without building 6 localized versions before finding product-market fit. A Romanian
researcher could publish in the same knowledge network as their MIT counterpart and have their
work discovered at equivalent rates. A Bulgarian SME could bid on contracts across the EU
without needing to maintain a team of language specialists to produce the required documentation
in 5 different languages.

This is not a vision of cultural homogenization. It is a vision of a market that functions as
one — where your technical ability to solve a problem is the primary variable, not your
geographic and linguistic starting point. It is the EU that was promised in the founding
documents, finally running on infrastructure that can deliver what the legal architecture
already guarantees.

The legacy system was built 70 years ago for a different era. The refactoring is overdue.
I believe the first commit should be language.
