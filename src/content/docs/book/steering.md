Watch anyone ride a bicycle. They are never upright. They lean left, correct,
lean right, correct, and the correcting never stops — and that is what holds
them up. Stand the same bicycle still and it falls over in two seconds.

**Balance is not a position. It is a series of corrections made while moving
forward.** The middle cannot be held. It can only be caught, again and again,
by somebody who is going somewhere.

I keep meeting the opposite belief in politics: that stability means keeping
still, that a good government is one that does not move, that the sign of a
serious state is that nothing changes for four years. That is the belief that
puts states on the ground. A state that refuses to lean is not stable. It is
stalled, and a stalled bicycle is a falling bicycle.

## We do not fall because we are wrong. We fall because we are slow.

Here is the pattern, and once you see it you will see it everywhere.

A direction gets set. It is held for years, past the point where everyone
involved can see it is not working. Then it is reversed at enormous cost —
after the money is spent, after the factories have retooled, after the
competitors have taken the market. And often enough, the thing the public said
at the start turns out to be roughly what happens in the end.

Notice what is broken there. It is not that a mistake was made. Every system
that does anything makes mistakes. **The failure is in how long it took to
correct one.** The cost is not in the wrong turn; the cost is in the distance
travelled before the wheel moved.

## The cars

The clearest example we have is the car industry, and I want to walk through it
with the dates, because the shape of the story is the argument.

In March 2023 the Union adopted Regulation (EU) 2023/851, which required new
cars and vans sold from 2035 to emit no CO2 at all — in effect, the end of the
combustion engine in new cars. Even that adoption was not clean. Germany held
the final vote up for about a month and extracted a carve-out for vehicles
running only on e-fuels, [a last-minute side deal agreed at the end of March
2023](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2023/03/28/in-win-for-germany-eu-agrees-to-exempt-e-fuels-from-2035-ban-on-new-sales-of-combustion-en).
A law that has to be renegotiated in the last week before it passes is a law
that was never settled.

Then came the reversal. In December 2025 the Commission proposed replacing the
100% cut with **90%**, which means plug-in hybrids, range extenders and
combustion cars can go on being sold after 2035, with the remaining tenth
offset through EU-made low-carbon steel and fuels such as e-fuels and biofuels
([Euronews, 16 December
2025](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2025/12/16/eu-carmakers-to-comply-with-90-emissions-reduction-by-2035-as-full-combustion-engine-ban-s)).
Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Poland and Slovakia had
pressed for exactly this, and the European carmakers had pressed harder.

Two honest qualifications, because I would rather be accurate than rhetorical.

**First, this is a proposal, not yet law.** It still needs the member state
governments and the European Parliament, and the final text may differ from
what I have described. If you read this later than I wrote it, check where it
landed.

**Second, "the citizens were against it from the start" is my reading, not a
measured fact.** I have not put a poll in front of you and I am not going to
invent one. What I can say without inventing anything is that the reversal
happened, that it happened under pressure from industry and from national
governments, and that nobody ever asked the public directly whether this was
the transition they wanted and at that speed.

Everything else about the episode is on the record, and it is bad enough. We
set a direction, told an entire industry to spend against it, held it for two
and a half years, and then softened it to stop European manufacturers going
under. **That is not a policy. That is a swerve.** I think it is an
embarrassment for the Union's administration, and I think the embarrassment is
not that they changed their mind — changing your mind is the correct response
to new information — but that it took this long and cost this much.

And the cost was paid twice. Once by the companies that invested in the
direction we announced, and again by the ones that hesitated because they
suspected we would not hold it. Both bets turned out to be reasonable. That is
the real damage of a slow system: **it stops being rational to trust it**, and
after that nobody plans on anything it says.

## A controller must correct at least as fast as the thing it steers

There is a name for this problem, and it is older than the European Union.

Ross Ashby's **law of requisite variety** says that a controller must have at
least as much variety in its responses as the system it is trying to control
throws at it — the usual short form is that [only variety can absorb
variety](https://www.businessballs.com/strategy-innovation/ashbys-law-of-requisite-variety/).
Stafford Beer spent his career applying this to organisations and to states,
and built the [Viable System
Model](https://www.businessballs.com/strategy-innovation/viable-system-model-stafford-beer/)
around it.

I raise the name once and then drop it, because the part that matters here is
simple enough to say in ordinary words. **A steering system that corrects once
every four years can only steer a world that changes once every four years.**
Ours does not. Technology, energy prices, supply chains and wars move faster
than that, and a four-year loop against a six-month world is not governance, it
is hoping.

## Direct democracy is the faster loop

This is why [direct democracy](/book/direct-democracy) is not a matter of taste
for me. It is the correction mechanism.

Frequent votes on specific questions do two things at once. They make
corrections **more often**, and because they happen more often, they make each
correction **smaller**. A wheel you touch every few seconds needs a nudge. A
wheel you touch once every four years needs a yank, and a yank at speed is how
you come off.

The Swiss electorate votes about four times a year. That is not, as people
assume, a country in permanent crisis from constant voting. It is a country
whose steering never has to move very far, because it never goes very long
without moving. The instruments and the safety mechanisms are set out in
[direct democracy](/book/direct-democracy) and in
[safeguards](/book/safeguards); I will not repeat them here.

What I want to add in this chapter is that speed is the point. Not
participation for its own sake, not fairness in the abstract — **error
correction, fast enough to matter.**

## The other loop: who talks to whom

Voting is the loop between citizens and the state. There is a second loop that
is just as broken, and it runs between the state and the people who actually
build things.

Talk to anyone running a business in Poland and you will hear a version of the
same complaint: **there is no way to get through.** They cannot tell the
government what growth actually requires — which rule is strangling a sector,
which permit takes nine months, what an investment decision is waiting on. And
from the other side, there is no shared understanding between entrepreneurs and
everybody else about taxes and about how the burden should be divided. Each
group suspects the other of freeloading, and neither has a room to say so in.

I think this is a plumbing problem before it is a political one. Information
that should flow does not flow, so the state legislates blind and business
lobbies in the dark, and lobbying in the dark is how you end up with laws that
serve whoever had the best access rather than whoever had the best argument.

What I would want instead is straightforward, and it runs in both directions.
**Business sends someone to talk to the state** — chosen by the firms in a
sector, accountable to them, openly doing that job. And **the state sends
someone to talk to business**, not to supervise it and not to plant an informer
in it, but to carry information back. The state's role in this is neither owner
nor referee-with-a-whistle. It is closer to a switchboard with checks and
balances attached: the place where the different interests are made to meet,
argue in public, and be seen doing it.

## What to learn from China, stated carefully

I will say the uncomfortable part plainly, and then bound it.

I think we can learn something about the management of a state from China, and
the thing I want to learn is **not the party**. Not one-party rule, not the
absence of a free press, not what happens to people who become inconvenient.
None of that is on offer here and none of it is what I am pointing at. I am a
direct-democracy man; I want more power in the hands of citizens, not less.

The part worth stealing is narrower, and it is a question rather than an
answer: **where do representatives come from, and what do they represent?**

Our answer is almost entirely geography. You represent a district. That is a
real thing to represent, but it is one axis, and a modern economy has several.
Nobody in our parliaments sits there as the person the machine-tool sector sent,
or the person the care workers sent, or the person the small-logistics firms
sent, with a mandate from them and a duty to report back to them. Those people
exist in the building, but they arrive as lobbyists, paid by whoever pays them,
visible to nobody.

China's system routes representation through sectors and organisations as well
as places. I am not holding up how those representatives are selected — that is
the part I have just refused. I am saying that the *shape* of the question is
one we have stopped asking, and that we should ask it again in a democratic
form: elected, transparent, revocable, and answerable to the people who sent
them.

This is speculation on my part. I do not have a designed mechanism to hand you,
and anyone who tells you they can bolt a second chamber of sectors onto a
European democracy without consequences has not thought about it long enough.

## Except we already built it, and left it toothless

Here is the turn in the argument, and it is the strongest thing in this chapter.

Europe already has institutions of exactly this shape. I did not have to invent
them. We have them, they sit in our capitals, and almost nobody can name them.

**The European Economic and Social Committee (EESC)** is a Union body of
representatives of employers, workers and civil society, which gives opinions on
EU legislation. It has existed since the Treaty of Rome. Its opinions are
**advisory and not binding** — it enriches the process, and it has no veto and
no formal power to stop anything. It issues on the order of a couple of hundred
opinions a year. In March 2026 the European Court of Auditors found that the
Union's advisory bodies [deliver their opinions late and do not measure their
own
influence](https://www.lovguiden.dk/en/det-offentlige/den-europaeiske-revisionsret/2026-03-24-eus-raadgivende-organer-leverer-udtalelser-for-sent-og-maaler-ikke-effekt)
on legislation at all, tracking visibility and volume instead of outcomes. If a
body cannot tell you whether it changed anything, that is usually because it
did not.

**In Poland, the Rada Dialogu Społecznego** — the Council for Social Dialogue —
is the national forum where unions, employers and the government are supposed
to meet. The idea is right. The practice is the complaint you hear from every
side of the table: draft laws never reach it, or reach it too late to matter;
bills are routed through parliament in ways that bypass its opinion; government
seats sit empty at meetings. Union and employer representatives alike have
described it publicly as [stuck — operating, but producing no results and no
shared vision](https://www.pulshr.pl/zwiazki-zawodowe/rada-dialogu-spolecznego-w-impasie-brakuje-efektow-i-wspolnej-wizji-dzialan,115185.html).

So the honest conclusion is not "Europe needs a new institution for this". It
is worse and more useful than that: **the institution exists, and it has no
teeth.** We built the channel and then made sure nothing has to come out of the
other end.

That reframes what to demand. Not a new body with a new logo — another forum
nobody has heard of is exactly what we do not need. What these bodies lack is
consequence. Things that would give them some, none of which require a treaty
fantasy:

- **A duty to answer.** If a draft law goes to these bodies and they object, the
  legislator must publish a reasoned reply before proceeding. Not obedience — a
  written answer, on the record, that a journalist can quote back later.
- **A duty to ask.** A law touching a sector does not move without that sector's
  representatives having been given a real chance to see it. Bypassing the
  council should be a defect in the process, not a tactic.
- **Publication by default.** Every meeting, every position, every name. Most of
  what lobbying buys is privacy.
- **Visible people.** If a sector sends a representative, the public should know
  who they are and be able to read what they asked for. A lobbyist nobody can
  name is worth more to his employer than a representative everybody can.

Whether any of that is best done by reforming the EESC and the national
councils, or by wiring the same duties into the ordinary legislative procedure,
I do not know. I lean towards fixing what exists, because a body with a hundred
years of institutional memory and no power is a better starting point than a new
body with neither.

## Why the two loops need each other

I have described two feedback loops: citizens correcting the state through
frequent binding votes, and producers and the state exchanging information
through channels that actually carry.

They are not alternatives and they are not the same thing. The votes decide
**direction** — what kind of country this is going to be, who the gains go to,
what we will not accept. The dialogue supplies **information** — what a decision
will actually do once it hits a factory, a ward, a haulage firm.

A state with fast votes and no information corrects quickly towards the wrong
place. A state with good information and no votes knows exactly what to do and
does what the best-connected interest wants instead — which is a fair
description of the car file. **You need the speed and you need the sensors.**
The bicycle needs a rider who can steer and a rider who can see.

## What this means for the rest of the argument

If you take one thing from this chapter, take the reframing. The question to ask
of any proposed reform is not only "is this the right direction" but:

**When this turns out to be partly wrong, how long will it take us to find out,
and how expensive will it be to correct?**

That question kills a lot of grand plans and rescues a lot of modest ones. It is
the reason I argue for instruments rather than for programmes, and the reason
[the total state](/book/the-total-state) insists that the correction mechanism
ships alongside the digital machinery rather than after it. A state that can see
everything and cannot be corrected is not a well-steered state. It is a fast
bicycle with no handlebars.

We are not falling because we chose badly. We are falling because we choose
slowly, hold too long, and then swerve. Lean, correct, keep moving. That is all
steering has ever been.
