# Digital Democracy: Shortening the Chain Between Citizen and Power

I want to tell you how a hospital director gets appointed in Poland.

A minister appoints a regional health authority official. That official appoints a hospital
board. The board appoints a director. The director runs the hospital for five or ten years,
regardless of what the doctors think, regardless of what the nurses think, regardless of what
the patients think — because none of them had a vote. The chain from citizen to decision is at
least five levels of indirection. In software, we call this a legacy architecture: every added
layer was a reasonable local decision at the time, and together they form a system nobody
designed, nobody understands, and nobody can fix without touching everything at once.

I propose we refactor it.

## The Distance Problem

Every institution you interact with daily — your hospital, your school, your city transport
authority, your local police station — is governed by people you did not choose and cannot
remove. Not because direct democracy is impossible. Because we never built the infrastructure
for it.

Switzerland runs 12–17 federal referenda per year across four voting days. Swiss citizens have
voted on UN membership, corporate tax reform, face covering bans, the Swiss Army's abolition,
and universal basic income — all within the last twenty-five years. The administrative cost is
roughly CHF 40–50 million per voting day, about €0.008 per citizen per ballot question. This
is not a price no society can afford. It is a design choice every society has made not to make.

The EU Citizens' Initiative — the closest EU-level equivalent — has collected over 100
registered initiatives since 2012. Eleven crossed the one million signature threshold. Zero
produced directly binding legislation. Zero. The mechanism exists to give the appearance of
participation while the Commission retains full discretion. That is not democracy. That is
advisory theater.

I believe we can build something different.

## What I Propose

Monthly digital votes on the decisions that shape daily life. Not a referendum on every EU
directive — that way lies noise and voter fatigue. A tiered architecture with three levels.

**EU level**: Treaty changes, major directives, budget priorities. Once or twice a year.
Supermajority requirements to prevent bare-majority capture of fundamental rights.

**National and regional level**: Parliamentary laws go forward, but citizens retain the
Swiss-style optional referendum: collect enough signatures and any law faces a popular vote.
Brazil's Mudamos platform gave 1.5 million citizens direct access to submit legislative
proposals with binding signature thresholds. Operational infrastructure, already deployed.

**Municipal and institutional level**: Monthly. School budget. City transport routing.
Hospital director appointment. Police chief performance review. These are the decisions
closest to citizens, affecting them most directly, and currently decided furthest from them.

## Liquid Democracy for Ministers

Representative democracy was designed for a world without instant communication. That world
is gone. I propose adapting the model.

Under liquid democracy — deployed in practice by the German Pirate Party's LiquidFeedback
platform — your vote is yours to cast directly, or to delegate to someone you trust for a
specific domain. Delegate your energy-policy vote to an engineer you respect, your health-policy
vote to a doctor you follow, cast your urban planning vote yourself. Revoke any delegation
instantly. At peak, 60% of LiquidFeedback users participated in high-salience votes — far
above typical representative body rates. The engagement problem is a design problem.

I propose applying this logic to ministerial accountability. Today, ministers govern with
near-total immunity between elections. A minister can spend four years making decisions the
electorate opposes at 70% in polls — and face no mechanism until the next election. A
four-year feedback loop. No engineer would ship a system this slow.

My proposal: parliamentary elections remain. But ministers face continuous approval tracking,
with a structured recall mechanism when approval falls below a defined threshold after a
review period. Citizens who elected a government should be able to recall a specific minister
who has lost their confidence, without bringing down the entire government.

## Democracy Inside Institutions

The most under-discussed reform I propose: internal democracy within the institutions
citizens depend on daily.

Doctors should vote on hospital operational matters. Teachers and students should vote on
school rules within constitutional limits. Residents should vote on neighborhood development
decisions. Workers should sit on the supervisory boards of the companies they work for.

Germany's Mitbestimmung — codetermination law — has required 50% worker representation on
supervisory boards of companies with 2,000+ employees since 1976. The research shows: higher
wages, longer investment horizons, lower within-firm inequality, and no destruction of firm
value. Five million German employees have real institutional voice. This is not radical. It is
700 German companies including Volkswagen, Siemens, and Deutsche Bank. It works.

I call this organized anarchy within constitutional limits. Communities, institutions, and
workplaces should self-govern on matters that primarily affect them — subject to constitutional
rights floors that courts enforce. The hospital director appointed by an official who has never
visited that hospital should be the exception, not the rule.

## The Evidence Base

I am not proposing a vision. I am proposing an implementation, and the evidence for its
components is already accumulated.

Ireland's Citizens' Assembly in 2016–2018 took 99 randomly selected citizens, gave them
structured expert input over five weekends, and asked them to deliberate on abortion law —
a question Irish politicians had avoided for thirty years. They recommended legalizing abortion
by 64%. The 2018 referendum passed at 66.4%. Statistically identical. Citizens deliberating
with real information produce decisions that match actual public preference more accurately
than elite political forecasting. The deliberation problem is a design problem: give people
time, structure, and expert access, and they make sound decisions.

Taiwan's vTaiwan platform ran approximately 30 policy processes between 2015 and 2019 using
Pol.is — a platform that surfaces cross-partisan consensus by identifying statements that
majorities in every identified opinion cluster support simultaneously. On Uber regulation,
online alcohol sales, FinTech policy: every proposal that achieved consensus became government
policy. Adoption rate: 100%. The platform works at stakeholder scale, not population scale —
but the design principle is proven.

Belgium's G1000 (2011): 1,000 randomly selected citizens, one weekend, 55-point policy
agenda rated substantively coherent. Government ignored it entirely. **Lesson**: participatory
mechanisms require binding outcomes. Advisory processes change nothing.

Estonia runs 51% of its votes online — up from 1.9% in 2005 over eighteen years. The
security risks are real. But digital voting at national scale is not a future state. It is
Estonia's present.

## The Minority Rights Floor

I will not pretend this is without risk. Switzerland's direct democracy has produced: a
minaret ban (2009, 57.5%); a face covering ban (2020, 51.2%); repeated tightening of asylum
law. The structural problem is well-documented: majorities voting on rules that constrain
minorities produce predictably biased outcomes compared to court-mediated rights protection.

Any EU digital democracy architecture requires an explicit constitutional floor that courts
can enforce. Fundamental rights — freedom of religion, prohibition of discrimination,
due process — cannot be put to popular vote. This is not optional. The Swiss evidence
demonstrates what happens when it is not enforced by design.

I am proposing distributed power with strong constitutional constraints. Not majority rule
with no limits. Distributed power within a rights architecture that protects everyone,
including the people who would lose a given vote.

## What This Means for Warsaw

Here is the concrete version.

You live in Warsaw. You are fed up with the city transport authority's decisions — the routes
that don't connect, the investments that seem to serve contractors more than commuters, the
director who has held the position through three different city governments and appears
accountable to none of them. Under the current system, your options are: complain online,
vote in the next city election four years from now, or accept it.

Under the architecture I propose: monthly city decisions go to a digital vote with a 72-hour
window, using the eID infrastructure Estonia already built and Poland's mObywatel system
partially implements. You vote on the three transport budget proposals on the table. You
delegate your environmental vote to a council of experts whose reasoning you can inspect.
If the transport director's six-month approval rating falls below a defined threshold after
a structured review process, a recall procedure begins.

This is not "everyone votes on everything." That produces noise and fatigue. This is
tiered, delegated, weighted — the software architecture equivalent of moving from a
monolithic application to a microservices model where each decision is handled at the
appropriate level, by the people with appropriate information and appropriate stakes.

The hospital director appointed by a party official answering to a ministry official answering
to a coalition government — that is a five-level call stack for a local operational decision.
Digital democracy collapses that stack. Not to zero — governance requires expertise,
continuity, and constitutional protection. But from five levels of indirection to two.

The generation that builds this infrastructure will retire into the institutions it creates.

I prefer to live in the version we designed.
