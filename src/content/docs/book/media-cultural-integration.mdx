# Language Integration Happens Through Media Before Policy

The Netherlands is the most English-proficient country on Earth — not because a government passed a law, but because Dutch television was never dubbed. That single infrastructure decision, made decades ago by broadcasters rather than politicians, produced millions of informal learning hours per citizen per year, at zero cost to the state. Today, the Netherlands holds the #1 global position on the EF English Proficiency Index with a score of 624. Italy — which dubs everything — scores 513, ranking 59th globally. The gap between them is not intelligence, not investment, not educational ambition. The gap is subtitles.

I think this is one of the most underappreciated data points in European policy discussion. We argue endlessly about language curricula, bilingual education mandates, teacher training pipelines. All of that matters. But a silent variable has been shaping European English proficiency for fifty years: whether the television in your childhood home played foreign voices in the original, or replaced them with dubbed local actors.

## The Correlation Is Not Subtle

The pattern holds across the continent with a consistency that would embarrass most social science findings. Countries with subtitling cultures — Netherlands, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Portugal, Romania — cluster at the top of European proficiency rankings. Countries with dubbing cultures — Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Austria, Hungary — cluster in the middle and bottom, despite comparable education investments and, in Germany's case, a school system most Europeans would consider excellent.

Three-quarters of young Europeans aged 15–24 engage with English weekly, up from 62% just ten years ago. The engine driving this is not school — it is streaming platforms, social media, and gaming. Netflix, YouTube, TikTok, and Discord are doing more for European English acquisition than a decade of EU language policy. The question for any serious reform proposal is: how do we build infrastructure that amplifies this, rather than passively benefiting from it?

## What the EU Can Actually Do

I am not proposing that the EU mandate subtitles across member states. Telling France or Germany how to broadcast is a fight that would consume political capital better spent elsewhere, and it would lose. The point of the dubbing observation is not to legislate dubbing out of existence — it is to understand what conditions produce language integration, and then design policy that creates those conditions indirectly.

There are three levers the EU could realistically pull.

**First: pan-European co-productions.** The EU already funds culture through Creative Europe. But the funding model rewards national productions that happen to receive EU money, not genuinely European productions that audiences across multiple countries watch together. I propose reorienting a significant share of Creative Europe funding toward productions that are distributed simultaneously across at least ten member states, made in English as their production language, and involve creative teams from at least three countries. Not English to replace Polish or Italian — English as the working language of a shared European creative space, the same way English is already the working language of European science, European technology, and European business.

The economic logic is compelling. A drama series produced in English, with Finnish and Romanian actors, about a story set across Brussels and Warsaw, can be distributed to 450 million potential viewers. The same series produced in Finnish reaches 5.5 million. Production budgets that are impossible at national scale become viable at European scale. HBO built a global audience for European storytelling with "The Wire" and "Succession." There is no structural reason a European institution could not build an equivalent funding mechanism for European-made English-language content.

**Second: a pan-European public media platform.** The BBC is the most successful public media institution in democratic history. It was built at national scale, funded by a universal license fee, and became the foundation of English-language global culture. Every European country has a public broadcaster. None of them talks to the others.

I am proposing something more modest than a European BBC, because a European BBC is not politically achievable in the current decade. What is achievable is a joint platform — call it EuroPublic, call it something less administrative — where Europe's national public broadcasters pool a portion of their English-language content, subtitle it, and make it available across the continent. The BBC already licenses content internationally. ZDF and ARD co-produce with European partners. France Télévisions funds films seen across Europe. The infrastructure for cooperation exists. What is missing is the platform that aggregates it and makes it searchable, free, and subtitled in every EU language.

The precedent for this kind of joint European institution exists. Euronews operates in thirteen languages. Eurovision (the broadcasting union, not the song contest) is already a content-sharing cooperative of 56 broadcasters across 56 countries. The technical problem is not hard. The political problem — getting national broadcasters to cooperate, getting member states to fund it, getting the Commission to resist turning it into an institutional communications channel rather than a genuine cultural platform — is the real obstacle.

**Third: incentives for original English-language European content.** The EU should create a specific funding track for European content made in English from the ground up — not translated, not dubbed, but conceived and produced in English by European creators. This is already happening organically. "Dark" was made in German, but its creators have since moved to English-language production. Polish director Pawel Pawlikowski makes films in English. European directors, writers, and actors are already code-switching into English when they want global reach. EU policy should recognize this reality and fund it systematically, rather than treating English-language European content as a somehow lesser form of European culture.

The UK Open University — which the T3.1 research documents in detail — proves that English-language education at continental scale is operationally feasible. It enrolled 25,000 students in its first year of operation in 1971, without the internet, using television, radio, and postal materials. Today it serves over 170,000 students annually, making it the UK's largest university. A pan-European equivalent — a European Open University teaching in English, offering EU-recognized credits — is not a utopian idea. It is a logistics and governance problem. The binding constraint, as the case study notes, is not technology: it is mutual recognition of academic credits across member states. That is a policy problem. It is solvable.

## Cultural Integration Is Not Imposition

I want to be precise about what I am and am not proposing, because this argument is frequently misunderstood in the direction of cultural imperialism.

I am not proposing that Polish teenagers stop watching Polish television. I am not proposing that Italian cinema switch to English. I am not proposing that any European language be diminished in status, prestige, or public funding. Every national language in Europe is worth preserving and developing, and I say this as someone who writes in Polish when I want to reach the people I care most about.

What I am proposing is that we build the shared cultural infrastructure — the co-productions, the platform, the funding incentives — that makes it possible for a European shared story to exist. A story is not a mandate. Nobody forced the Dutch to watch "Friends" in English rather than dubbed Dutch. They preferred the original. When the original is available and the subtitles are good, audiences often prefer it. That preference is not cultural imposition — it is cultural choice.

The distinction between imposition and infrastructure matters enormously for the political viability of this argument. Cultural integration as imposition is what the French government fears and fights. Cultural integration as shared infrastructure is what makes sense to a practical-minded engineer looking at the problem. The EU's job is to build the infrastructure and get out of the way. The market — meaning actual European audiences making actual viewing choices — will do the rest.

## What 11% Tells Us

Research on European labor markets documents an 11% wage premium for English proficiency in Poland. That number appears in the T1.4 data alongside similar figures across Central and Eastern Europe. The premium is higher in Southern and Eastern Europe — where English is less common — and lower in Northern Europe, where it is near-universal. As European English proficiency rises, the individual salary premium shrinks. But the systemic benefit — the ability to work across borders, compete for EU-wide jobs, consume content from the full breadth of European culture — increases for everyone.

The 11% premium is also, if you look at it from the other direction, a measure of what language barriers currently cost. A Polish worker without English earns 11% less over a career. Scaled to 450 million people, that is not a small number. It is a structural tax on European human capital, levied by the accident of which country you were born in and what language your television spoke.

## What a Polish Teenager Watching a German Series Means

I want to end with a more concrete picture, because this argument is easy to make abstract when it should be vivid.

Imagine a 16-year-old in Wrocław in 2045. She has grown up watching European productions — a Finnish-French thriller set in Helsinki and Paris, a Romanian-Polish drama about cross-border families, a German series about the reconstruction of Kyiv, all made in English, all subtitled in Polish, all available on a platform that her parents pay a small fee for alongside their national public broadcaster subscription. She has never thought of English as a foreign language — it is the language of the stories she grew up with, alongside Polish, which is the language of her family and her street.

She applies to a university in Cracow and simultaneously to one in Munich. She can do this because her Wrocław degree would transfer credit. She applies to a company in Warsaw and simultaneously to one in Amsterdam. She can do this because her English is strong enough that the Amsterdam company treats her application exactly like a Dutch applicant.

The German series she watched at 14 featured an actor from Berlin she found compelling. She knows something about contemporary German life — not the Germany of textbooks, not the Germany of political summits, but the Germany of someone her age navigating a world she recognizes. That German teenager, watching the same series, knows something equivalent about her.

This is what shared cultural infrastructure produces over a generation. Not uniformity. Not the erasure of Polish or German or Finnish identity. Something more interesting and more durable: familiarity. The sense that the person on the other side of a job application, a trade negotiation, a political argument, or a friendship is not a foreign other but a neighbor whose stories you have partly shared.

European integration has been built on law and economics for seventy years. Law and economics are necessary. They are not sufficient. The shared story — the cultural layer that makes the legal and economic structures feel like they belong to people who actually know each other — is what has been underfunded, underdeveloped, and consistently treated as secondary to the "real" integration work.

I think it is the most important work. And I think it starts with whether your television dubbs or subtitles.
