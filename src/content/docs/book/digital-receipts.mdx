# Digital Receipts

Every card payment you make produces a digital record at the moment of the transaction.
The bank has it. The retailer's system has it. The payment network has it.
What nobody has is you.

That record disappears from your practical reach within seconds of the payment clearing.
You walk out of the store with a strip of thermal paper that fades in six months
and cannot be filed, searched, or proved to anyone automatically.
This is not a technical limitation. It is a design choice nobody ever made consciously —
it just grew that way, like most legacy systems.

I propose we change it with one regulation: every card payment in the EU automatically
generates a digital receipt, stored in the citizen's digital identity wallet.
Not a new technology. Not a new infrastructure. An instruction to use what already exists.

---

## Germany Already Did It — Partially

Germany introduced the *Belegpflicht* (receipt issuance obligation) in January 2020.
Every business using a cash register must issue a receipt after every transaction.
The intent was tax fraud prevention: if every transaction must produce a paper trail,
manipulating cash register software becomes harder.

Germany solved the right problem with the wrong instrument.
Thermal paper fulfills the legal obligation but defeats the practical purpose —
a receipt that fades in the customer's pocket does not create an audit trail.
It creates waste.

As of the most recent measurement, only 26% of German retailers offer any form of digital
receipt option — compared to 84% in the UK. Among those that do, 45% require an email address
just to receive it. The others use QR codes. None of this is integrated with any identity wallet,
tax system, or warranty registry.

Germany proved the mandate is politically achievable and legally uncontroversial.
What it did not prove is that paper receipts are the right outcome.
The EU can mandate the same behavior — but specify the right output format from the start.

## What the Proposal Actually Is

Simple version: when you pay by card in any EU country, your bank's infrastructure
receives transaction data. Today, that data contains merchant name, amount, timestamp,
and a transaction ID. It does not reliably contain the product line items — that information
stays inside the retailer's point-of-sale system.

The change I am proposing has two layers:

**Layer 1 — What exists today:** Mandate that card payment data already flowing through
the banking system is automatically forwarded to the citizen's eIDAS-linked digital wallet.
Transaction amount, merchant, date, category. No new infrastructure required.
The data already moves. We just need a rule saying it must land somewhere the citizen can access.

**Layer 2 — What requires retailer cooperation:** For purchases where itemized detail matters
(electronics, appliances, anything with a warranty), mandate that retailers transmit structured
line-item data alongside the payment. This is the harder part — it requires POS system upgrades
and a standardized data format across 27 member states. Doable, but not instant.

Start with Layer 1. It costs almost nothing and creates immediate value.
Add Layer 2 over a five-year implementation window, the same way the EU mandated
SEPA Instant Payments — passing the regulation in 2024 for infrastructure that had
existed since 2017, with hard deadlines and real penalties for non-compliance.

## The Systemic Impacts Are Larger Than They Look

### Tax authorities get an automatic VAT audit trail

Every digital receipt in the citizen's wallet is simultaneously a VAT record.
Right now, VAT compliance relies on self-reporting and spot-check audits.
A digital receipt mandate means every B2C card transaction is automatically
cross-referenced against the declared VAT return — no manual records request needed.
Cash fraud survives. Card fraud does not.
Estonia's e-Tax system demonstrates what follows: when transaction records are
structured and machine-readable, audit becomes algorithmic rather than investigative.

### Retailers eliminate thermal printing as an operating cost

Thermal paper is a supply chain, a maintenance cost, and an environmental problem —
much of it unrecyclable because of the BPA coating used for thermal printing.
For large retail chains, the cost of paper, printer maintenance, and staff time managing
roll replacements is non-trivial at scale. Digital receipts eliminate this entirely.

### Consumer protection becomes effortless to enforce

The EU has strong consumer protection rules. Directive 2019/771 guarantees a two-year
minimum statutory warranty on all goods sold in the EU. The practical obstacle to exercising
that warranty right is almost always the same: proving you bought the product, from that
seller, on that date.

A receipt you cannot find is a right you cannot exercise.
The retailer knows this. The warranty gap is not primarily a legal problem —
it is a documentation problem. A digital receipt stored automatically in your wallet
closes that gap completely. The product is in your wallet, linked to the purchase date,
the retailer, the amount paid. The claim is unfakeable and unarguable.

This is low-hanging fruit in the legal sense: no new rights created,
no new courts needed. Just a mechanism that makes existing rights actually usable.

## The Infrastructure Is Already There

The eIDAS 2.0 regulation, agreed in 2024, mandates that all EU member states offer
a Digital Identity Wallet to their citizens by 2026. The wallet is explicitly designed
to hold transaction records alongside credentials, qualifications, and licences.

The payment rails are also already there. SEPA Instant Payments settles transactions
in under 10 seconds at a per-transaction infrastructure cost of €0.002.
The data flow that digital receipts require rides on top of existing payment messaging —
a metadata addition, not a new protocol.

This is why I call it low-hanging fruit: not because it is politically simple,
but because it does not require inventing anything new.
We need to route existing data to a new destination.
Compare that to building a digital euro from scratch at an estimated €1.3 billion.

## What "All Your Receipts, Forever, in One Place" Actually Means

Let me be concrete. I bought a coffee machine three years ago. I need the receipt
for a warranty claim because the heating element failed. I have no idea where
the physical receipt is — it was probably in a shopping bag with other receipts,
folded twice, left in a drawer, and thrown away during some cleaning session.
The retailer's records exist, but getting them means calling a support line,
explaining the situation, waiting for a case number, and hoping someone in their
back-office system can reconstruct the purchase from my loyalty card data —
which I may or may not have had.

That is three hours of my time to exercise a legal right that takes the retailer
thirty seconds to confirm or deny once they can see the record.

Under the system I am proposing, this conversation does not happen.
I open my digital wallet, search "coffee machine", and see the receipt: date, store,
model, price, transaction ID. I share a verified digital copy with the manufacturer's
warranty portal in one tap. The claim is automatically validated against the purchase date.
The repair or replacement process starts.

The friction removed is significant not because each case is unbearably hard —
it is annoying but survivable — but because the aggregate effect, multiplied across
hundreds of millions of EU consumers making millions of warranty-eligible purchases every year,
is that most people do not bother. Rights are abandoned not because people do not want them,
but because exercising them costs more energy than the benefit seems worth.

A digital receipt changes that calculation. Infrastructure cost: near-zero.
Citizen benefit: immediate, concrete, personal. Fiscal benefit to tax authorities: real.
Environmental benefit to retailers: real.

This is what refactoring legacy systems looks like when it works — not a grand transformation,
but a small routing change that makes existing rules actually function as designed.
