Capital will not build you a large flat. It has no reason to. Rent is the point
of the exercise, and rent is highest where space is scarce, so scarcity is the
product. This chapter is about the one actor that can decide otherwise, and what
I think it should decide.

It is an anti-capitalist idea and I will not pretend it is anything else. Capital
drains rent out of us, year after year, for the ground we stand on. The state can
take a field in the middle of nowhere — buy it, or use land it already holds —
and say: here, on this nothing, we are building a town under new rules. Or it
sells the land for development on the condition that the new rules apply. Either
way the rules come first and the ground is cheap, which is the only moment when
any of this is affordable.

## Build beside the cities that exist

The empty field is the clean case. The useful case is next to a city that is
already there.

Take a city, take the land at its edge, and build a district of towers, or of
ten-storey blocks — and set a **minimum** height rather than a maximum. That
inversion is the whole policy in one line. Every planning system I have lived
under caps how high you may build. Cap it the other way: below this many floors,
you may not build here at all.

Then put the tram or the metro in from day one, before the first resident moves
in. Not promised for the second phase, not "subject to future funding". Laid at
the same time as the sewers, because a district that opens without rail is a
district of cars forever, and you will not retrofit it later at any price you are
willing to pay.

## Space is the value

I want large flats. Eighty square metres, a hundred, a hundred and twenty. A
hundred and sixty. Two hundred.

That sounds extravagant until you ask what it is for. A family needs rooms. A
person who works from home needs a room that is not the kitchen table. And part
of a flat does not need to be heated to twenty-one degrees all winter: a workshop
space, heated lightly or not at all, plus a terrace. Once you stop insisting that
every square metre be a heated living room, square metres get much cheaper, and
you can hand people the thing they actually lack, which is room.

We are animals that did not grow up in a cave. We spent almost all of our history
outside, and we spend almost none of it there now. I suspect that is why our eyes
are going — we look at close things all day. That suspicion turns out to have
support: a three-year randomised trial in Guangzhou found that adding forty
minutes of outdoor time at school cut the three-year incidence of myopia in
first-graders from 39.5% to 30.4%, and the
[Cochrane review](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013549.pub2/abstract)
treats time outdoors as a real, if modest and uncertain, preventive measure. So
when I ask for terraces, distance to look at, and something green at the bottom
of the building, I am not asking for decoration.

And the way to have space without eating the countryside is to build high.
Density and space are not opposites; tall buildings are how you get both. Europe
has no third continent of land to spread onto.

The need is not theoretical either.
[Eurostat reports](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251222-1)
that 16.8% of people in the EU lived in an overcrowded home in 2023, with rates
above 40% in Latvia and Romania. That is the floor of the problem, before anyone
mentions price.

## Asia already did this

Singapore is the case everyone knows. Its Housing and Development Board houses
the large majority of the population —
[Statista puts it at 77% of residents in 2023](https://www.statista.com/statistics/966747/population-living-in-public-housing-singapore/)
— and most of those households own the flat they live in. A state agency built
the housing stock of a country. It is not a thought experiment.

Malaysia is the example I keep coming back to, and here I have to separate what I
can check from what I saw. What I can check: new homes launched in Kuala Lumpur
and the Klang Valley average
[around 827 square feet](https://www.edgeprop.my/content/1471152/five-things-you-probably-did-not-know-about-size-malaysian-homes),
mostly high-rise, while Malaysian condominiums commonly run
[100 to 120 square metres with three bedrooms](https://www.teoalida.com/world/malaysia/),
and larger units exist in quantity.

What is my impression, from being there rather than from a table: they built
those towers while labour was cheap, and they are going to be glad of it. The
flats are large and pleasant, the cities are dense, everything is close. The
country has obvious faults — it is not a place you walk, the pavements stop and
start, you get about on a scooter. But pavements can be rebuilt later, once a
city learns to run itself. Towers cannot be built later, not easily and not
cheaply. They put the expensive, irreversible thing in first and left the cheap,
fixable thing for afterwards. I think that ordering is correct and I think we
have it backwards.

## Two more things, small and not small

**Allotment gardens.** Poland has these — *ogrody działkowe*, thousands of
gardens covering
[tens of thousands of hectares](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/10/11/3829),
plot by plot, in and around the cities. A person in a flat gets a piece of
ground, a shed, a few fruit trees. I recommend them without reservation. If other
countries want one Polish institution, take this one.

**A gym in every building.** Not a fitness industry, not a membership: a room in
the block, open late or around the clock, with weights and a bike and a window
that looks out at distance, so you can look far away while you pedal. It costs
almost nothing at construction and is nearly impossible to add afterwards.
Schools still have physical education. Nothing in adult life replaces it.

## The point: a standard is due

Here is the spine of the argument, and it is not about towers.

After the industrial revolution we worked out what a person must be given so that
the institutions we depend on could function at all. Britain's
[Public Health Act of 1875](https://www.cieh.org/about-us/history-of-cieh/150-years-of-public-health-act/)
is the clearest example: local authorities were obliged to set byelaws, and new
housing had to have drainage, ventilation, a toilet, minimum space between
buildings. Those were not luxuries. They were the conditions under which family,
school and medicine could work at all, written into building law because
exhortation had failed for fifty years.

That standard is now out of date. It says nothing about prevention, nothing about
whether a body gets moved during the day, nothing about daylight or distance or
room to work at home, nothing about whether a child can get outside without being
driven. We know much more about what breaks people than we did in 1875 and we
have written almost none of it into the rules for how we build.

So write a new one. Movement, space, daylight, transport and green ground
designed in at the start rather than retrofitted by a later generation that
cannot afford it. That is the claim of this chapter — the towers and the large
flats are just what it looks like when you take it seriously.

## The objections, which are good ones

**Who pays.** Land, rail and buildings, at scale, up front. I do not have the
number and I will not invent one. What I can say about the shape: buying the
field before it is a town is the cheapest moment to buy anything, and the
increase in its value afterwards is created by the public decision to build
there. Whether that uplift can fund the works is the question that decides
whether this is a plan or a wish, and someone should cost it honestly, on real
land, before anyone promises anything.

**Whether people want towers.** Plenty do not. Survey after survey finds
Europeans saying they want a house with a garden. My answer is partly that the
choice on offer today is a house far out or a small flat in town, and a large
flat in a well-served dense district has rarely been one of the options — but I
am aware that is a convenient answer, and the way to find out is to build some
and see who moves in and who leaves.

**What happened last time.** Europe has done this before, twice, with opposite
results, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

Britain built [32 new towns](https://www.tcpa.org.uk/resources/explore-the-uks-new-towns/)
between 1946 and the 1970s, now home to about 2.8 million people. They were not a
catastrophe; they were also not loved, they were criticised as paternalism from
the first week, and their rents excluded many of the poorest households they were
supposed to serve. France's *grands ensembles* went far worse: built fast, left
without services or transport, isolated, and now
[synonymous with poverty and exclusion](https://metropolitiques.eu/Grands-Ensembles-Retracing-a.html),
with many being demolished. The common failure is not the height. It is building
the housing and not building everything else — which is exactly why I keep saying
the tram goes in on day one.

The counter-case is Vienna, where the city has been the landlord for a century
and [roughly 60% of residents](https://socialhousing.wien/policy/the-vienna-model)
live in municipal or subsidised housing without any of it being a byword for
failure. So the record says state-built housing can go either way, and the
difference lies in the detail of how it is run and maintained, not in whether the
state builds.

**Building badly, at scale.** This is the risk I take most seriously. A mistake
in a single block is a bad block. A mistake in a standard is a mistake repeated
ten thousand times, in concrete, for eighty years. Cheap construction, bad
management, a single monotonous design, one income group in one district — every
one of those was a real failure mode last time.

**What would settle it.** Not an essay. Build two or three districts under these
rules — minimum heights, large flats with unheated workshop space, rail before
occupancy, a gym in every building, allotments at the edge — in different
countries, with different builders and different tenure models, and publish what
it cost and who moved in. Then measure the boring things for ten years: who
leaves, what it costs to maintain, how far children get outdoors, what happens to
the price of everything around it. If the districts empty out, I am wrong, and it
will be visible.

---

I would rather we build this while it is still cheap to build, and argue about
the details afterwards, than have the argument perfectly and build nothing. The
towers cannot be added later. The pavements can.
