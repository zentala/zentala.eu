You can print as much money as you like. It will not make a nurse appear.

That sentence is the floor under everything else on this site. [The pension
argument](/book/designing-our-retirement) ends with a demand for work that will
not be there; this chapter is about what has to be true of the thing we build so
that the work is there, and so that the benefit of it reaches the people who
paid for it.

## Money cannot buy a service nobody supplies

We talk about the demographic crisis as if it were a funding problem. It is not.
A funding problem has a monetary answer: raise contributions, cut benefits, shift
the retirement age, borrow. Every one of those answers moves claims around. None
of them creates the thing being claimed.

When there are too few working-age people, the shortage is physical. Somebody has
to wash an eighty-year-old, drive the bus, harvest the field, answer the phone at
the clinic at two in the morning. If nobody does it, the service does not become
expensive. It stops existing. You stand at the counter with a full account and
there is nobody behind it.

So the task is not to fund the future. It is to **design the system that will
supply it** — care, food, transport, public services — with far fewer hands than
we have now. If we do not design it, no amount of money will conjure it. We can
print until the presses wear out and it changes nothing.

That is the hard version of the argument, and I think it is the honest one. It is
also why this is an engineering project with a political wrapper, rather than a
political project with an engineering appendix.

## It has to produce a surplus

A system that only feeds itself is not enough.

Whatever we build — automated municipal services, autonomous farming and
logistics, machines in care — has to earn more than it costs to run. It has to
cover its own upkeep, the people who operate and maintain it, the engineers who
keep it alive, and then **have something left over**. That leftover is the whole
point. It is what pays for the services the rest of us live on without working
for them: children, the old, the sick, and everyone between jobs.

This is not a new demand. It is what every productive economy has ever done; we
are simply going to be asking it of a smaller number of people and a larger
number of machines, which means the surplus has to be designed in rather than
hoped for.

## School meals, and why they are the example

Here is the worked case I keep coming back to.

An automated food chain — growing, processing, moving — that is genuinely cheap
can feed every child in every school in the country, free or near enough to free
that the price stops being a question anyone asks. Not as charity. As a normal
output of capacity that exists anyway.

I choose that example deliberately, and not only because it is easy to picture.

**Public services are how this technology becomes popular.** Right now, when an
ordinary person hears "robots", they hear "my job". They are not being stupid;
that is the version they have been shown. The argument that automation is good
for them is abstract, and abstract arguments lose.

A hot meal their child eats every day, for free, produced by the thing everyone
is arguing about, is not abstract. It is the difference between a technology
people tolerate and a technology people defend. If the first visible thing
automation does in a town is feed its children, the politics of the next twenty
years look very different from the politics we get if the first visible thing it
does is empty a warehouse.

So deploy it where people see it, and where the benefit is unmistakably theirs.
Municipal services first, as [the robotics
chapter](/book/robotic-reindustrialisation) argues — and food on the table of
somebody who did not have to pay for it.

## It has to earn for society, not only for capital

Now the part that decides whether any of this was worth doing.

The machines will earn. The question is whose account the earnings land in. If
automation replaces the labour our pensions depend on and the returns flow to
whoever owns the fleet, we will have solved production and destroyed the
distribution that made solving it worthwhile. A continent full of robots and
empty of purchasing power has not fixed its demographic problem; it has changed
the shape of it.

"It must earn for society" is easy to say and easy to mean nothing by. What I
mean by it, concretely:

- **Public buyers get the output, not just the invoice.** When a municipality or
  a pension system funds capacity, what it gets back is the service — meals,
  cleaning, transport, care hours — not a financial return that it then has to
  spend buying the same service on the open market at whatever price the owner
  sets.
- **Pension contributions buy the thing they were meant to buy.** Money set aside
  for our old age, invested in the machines that will care for us, is the one
  investment that delivers the service directly rather than a claim on somebody
  else's.
- **The surplus is visible and named.** A system whose surplus nobody can point
  to will be quietly captured. Whoever runs it should have to say, in public and
  in numbers, what it produced and where that went.

I am not going to relitigate the ownership question here, because I have already
set out where I stand. [Designing Our
Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement) says ownership models will be mixed
and should be — public or cooperative for municipal work, private where
competition actually works — and that arguing about the ideal structure in
advance is how twenty years get spent. [Robotic
Reindustrialisation](/book/robotic-reindustrialisation) says what matters more:
**cheap, open, hard to monopolise.** Technology that cannot be enclosed
distributes its benefits almost by default. Technology that can be enclosed will
be, whatever the ownership law says.

This chapter adds one thing to those two. Cheapness and open standards are the
best defence we have, and they are not a guarantee. Something has to keep making
the decision, year after year, in our favour.

## Direct democracy is the instrument

That something is us, and we need a mechanism for it.

This is the practical reason I care about [direct
democracy](/book/direct-democracy), beyond the general case that representative
politics has stopped delivering. A body of a few hundred people can be lobbied
into handing a public asset to whoever asks nicely and pays well. It is cheap to
do and it has been done, repeatedly, to things we built together. Decisions taken
by everyone are not incorruptible, but they are far more expensive to corrupt.

The choices this chapter is about — what the surplus is spent on, who may own the
fleet that does public work, whether school meals are free — are exactly the kind
of decision that should not be settled quietly. They are simple to state, they
affect everybody, and people have real opinions about them. That is the profile
of a question that belongs to a referendum rather than to a committee.

Direct democracy is not decoration on the robotics argument. It is the part that
keeps the answer pointed at us.

## Is this communism?

I want to deal with this myself rather than wait for someone to say it.

There is a resemblance, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. I am saying
that the state should intervene deliberately to shape who benefits from a
technology, and that the gains of automation should reach everyone rather than
the people who financed the machines. That is a claim about distribution, and it
is not a small one.

Here is where it parts company. **Communism handed the technology to the party**,
which held it on the people's behalf and decided what to do with it. The whole
design rested on trusting an unaccountable body to distribute fairly, and it did
not, anywhere, for long. What I want is the opposite arrangement: **people
holding the power directly**, and a system that balances itself — many hands on
the wheel, corrections made often and early, nobody with the standing to say they
know best on everyone's behalf.

I am also not proposing to abolish anything. Markets do most of this work better
than a ministry would; I want competition in robotics, several suppliers, firms
that go bust when they deserve to. My argument is about the floor under the
market and about who owns the assets that public life depends on — not about
replacing the market with a plan.

So: not communism. But I do not have a clean word for it either, and I would
rather admit that than reach for one that flatters me.

## Words that fit better

While looking for that word, I found three that describe parts of it precisely.
I am setting them out because they are useful, not because I have picked one.

**Predistribution.** Coined by the political scientist Jacob Hacker, it means
shaping how income and assets are distributed *before* tax and transfers, rather
than letting the market produce an outcome and then correcting it afterwards with
benefits. The contrast with redistribution is the point: raising wages rather
than topping them up, changing who owns a thing rather than taxing what it earns.
([Equitable Growth's
explainer](https://equitablegrowth.org/predistribution/); [Hacker's original
argument](https://www.newstatesman.com/politics/2013/07/meet-mr-predistribution-jacob-hacker);
[overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predistribution).) Most of what this
site proposes — cheap open machines, public capacity, who owns the fleet — is
predistributive in that exact sense. It decides the outcome upstream instead of
arguing about compensation downstream.

**A citizens' wealth fund.** The pattern exists and has run for decades. Norway
put its petroleum revenue into the Government Pension Fund Global, now the
largest sovereign wealth fund in the world, holding over two trillion dollars and
drawn down gradually to support the budget. Alaska did something different with
the same idea: the Alaska Permanent Fund pays an annual cash dividend to every
resident, and has since the early 1980s. ([Government Pension Fund of
Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Pension_Fund_of_Norway);
[Alaska Permanent Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Permanent_Fund);
[Alaska Permanent Fund
Corporation](https://www.ifswf.org/members/usa).) Both turn a national asset into
a public stake that pays out. The obvious question for us is whether automated
productive capacity can be held the same way, with the surplus going to citizens
as services or as a dividend — and whether that survives when the asset is a
fleet of machines that depreciates rather than oil that sits in the ground.

**The social market economy.** This one is already ours. Article 3 of the Treaty
on European Union commits the Union to "a highly competitive social market
economy, aiming at full employment and social progress" — language added by the
Lisbon Treaty, which deliberately went beyond the earlier formula of an open
market economy with free competition. ([Article 3
TEU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A12008M003);
[FRA's reference text](https://fra.europa.eu/en/law-reference/treaty-european-union-3).)
A market that produces, with an obligation that its gains reach society, is a
fair description of what I am asking for — and it is written into the founding
text of the thing I am asking. That is worth knowing before anyone calls the idea
foreign to Europe.

I have not settled on a label. Predistribution describes the method, a citizens'
fund describes one possible instrument, and the social market economy describes
the constitutional ground it could stand on. If one of them turns out to be the
right name, I would like to be told which.

## What would settle it

I do not want to win this by assertion, so here is what would actually decide the
open parts.

- **Does automated public provision produce a surplus at all, and how big?** A
  real municipal deployment, with published costs and outputs over a budget
  cycle, would tell us. Nobody should take my word for it, mine included.
- **Does a citizens' fund model work when the asset depreciates?** Oil sits still
  and appreciates; a robot fleet needs replacing. Somebody with the right training
  can model that, and the answer changes how the instrument should be designed.
- **What do the treaties and state-aid rules allow?** Public ownership of
  productive capacity, pension money invested in public infrastructure, procurement
  written to keep suppliers cheap and interoperable — each of those has a legal
  answer I do not know. [The Political Path](/book/political-path) lists these as
  open questions and asks for them to be closed in public. This chapter adds one
  more to the list.
- **Do public services actually change how people feel about automation?** It is
  the load-bearing claim in the middle of this chapter and it is testable. Feed
  children in one region and measure opinion there against a region where the
  first visible robot took a job.

## What this means for you

If you are building any of this, build it so the surplus is visible.

That is a design instruction, not a sentiment. Publish what it cost and what it
produced. Keep the interfaces open so the next town can run it without asking
permission. Deploy where the benefit is obvious to people who did not fund it,
and let them see it working before anyone asks them to vote on it.

And carry the argument in its hard form, because the soft form loses. Not "robots
will help the economy" — **there will be nobody to do the work, money will not
fix that, and the only question left is who the machines earn for.**
