Ask why Europe has no AI company of its own and you will get answers about
venture capital, energy prices and regulation. I think the answer is older and
duller than that.

There is no European AI because there is no European science, and there is no
European science because we do not have a working language we share.

## What we have and what we do not

We do have joint research. CERN exists, and it is real. But look at what it is:
theoretical physics, one building, one narrow field, a place people visit. Now
look at the fields that will decide the next twenty years — artificial
intelligence, robotics, bioengineering. There is no place where researchers from
every member state sit next to each other, day after day, and work on those.
There are conferences. There are consortia that file reports. There is not a
room.

Sitting in one room requires being able to talk in it. That is not a detail you
can solve later with better funding. It is the floor everything else stands on,
which is why [European English](/book/european-english) comes first in this book
and this chapter comes after it. Language integration is upstream of European
science. European science is upstream of European business. If we get the first
one, the most able people in Europe can work together; if we do not, they will
keep working for someone else, in English, somewhere else.

## What a European science would work on

Not everything. Our problems.

Europe is old and getting older — that is the whole argument of [Designing Our
Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement), and it points at a short list of
things worth putting money and talent into: predictive medicine, longevity and
healthy life, psychology, artificial intelligence, bioengineering.

Predictive medicine is the one people understand least, so it is worth saying
what it means. In the literature it usually sits inside what is called P4
medicine — predictive, preventive, personalised, participatory: instead of
waiting for disease to declare itself and then treating it, you use data to
estimate who is heading toward it and act early. Treating diabetes is medicine.
Knowing five years out that this person is heading for diabetes, and changing
the course, is predictive medicine. For a continent with a shrinking working-age
population, the difference between those two is the difference between a health
service we can afford and one we cannot.

But the interesting work is not only the medical science. It is everything
around it, and almost none of it is done:

- How do you program the systems that do this, and how do you build a health
  service around them rather than bolting them onto the side?
- How do you measure any of it — what counts as the system working?
- How do you learn from medical data safely, without the people in the data
  paying for it?
- How do you keep improving the system once it is running?
- And the question underneath all of them: are our research methods actually
  good, and are we studying the right things at all?

That last one is not a rhetorical flourish. A research programme that never
checks whether its own methods work will produce a large amount of confident
nothing.

Above all, European scientists should be the ones sitting over European medical
data. We already wrote the rules for this. The European Health Data Space —
Regulation (EU) 2025/327, in force since March 2025 — creates exactly the thing
the argument needs: a legal route for reusing health data across borders for
research and policy, through data access bodies, permits and secure processing
environments, on top of GDPR. The rules exist. The science that should be using
them does not exist in the same way. We built the door and no one built the
building behind it.

## European universities

If the point is people from every country working shoulder to shoulder, it has
to be built as such. European universities, taking students and researchers from
across the Union, with no national dominance — not a French institute with
foreign guests, not a German one, ours.

Where they go, I do not know. The obvious answer is next to the AI centres, in
the cities that want them. The other answer is that research and industry sit
badly together and they should be somewhere else. I do not have a view worth
defending here. What I am sure of is the part that does not depend on the map:
people from different countries have to work on artificial intelligence here.
The same goes for bioengineering, and the same goes for psychology.

## Mental health is an illness, not a personal failing

That last one is where I would start, because it is the largest untreated
problem we have and the one where the method can be tested fastest.

I think what we are looking at is a civilisational illness. We changed how we
live very fast, and we did not replace what the change removed. A child used to
grow up around a lot of adults — an aunt, an uncle, a grandmother to go to, cry
at, ask something of, and use to balance the power of their parents. Now a child
often has two adults and no one else, and those two are stressed by work and by
carrying the whole responsibility for the family alone, in a way that was not
true before. On top of that we keep children indoors, under a bell jar, so they
get far less social exposure than people did for most of history.

You cannot learn to read other people without other people to read.

The conclusion I draw is not that anyone failed. It is that this should be
treated the way we treat illness: something that gets diagnosed, treated and
supported, publicly, without shame. Not a private weakness you are supposed to
fix on your own time.

## Therapy at scale

The objection to treating it as illness is immediate and correct: there are
nowhere near enough therapists. There never will be. On the old model, the
supply of care is capped by how many trained humans can sit in a room for an
hour, and the need is far larger than that.

AI changes the shape of that problem. A large share of therapeutic work is
structured — assessment, exercises, tracking, the long patient middle — and it
can be automated or half-automated. My own estimate, and I want it read as an
estimate and not a finding, is that around 70% of therapeutic work can be
automated and around 30% should stay human. Nobody has measured this. Measuring
it is precisely the kind of thing a European science of psychology should do.

The 30% matters more than the 70%. It is a real therapist who meets the patient
from time to time, talks to them, and — this is the part people miss — feeds
their own read of that person back into the system. The machine gets a human
perspective it cannot generate. That therapist is also the supervisor of the
treatment. Better still, more than one: one therapist who holds the meetings and
two supervisors who follow the case and review it together weekly or monthly.
Care at scale, with humans where humans are irreplaceable, and oversight that is
plural rather than a single overworked person.

And there is the question I keep coming back to: who stands behind the chatbots?

Millions of people already confide in them. OpenAI reportedly hires
psychologists. But a company deciding, on its own terms, what happens when
someone in trouble types into a box is not an arrangement I am willing to live
with. The state should stand behind those conversations. Behind the chatbot
there should be a human therapist, publicly funded, or a panel of them — and
from inside the conversation the system should be able to say: this is the kind
of thing a therapist deals with, would you like to hand this over and talk to a
person? A digital handover, only with the person's consent. My guess is that
many people will say yes, if what is on the other side is good.

That is how the shortfall in psychological care actually gets closed. Not by
training ten times more therapists, which will not happen, but by putting the
scarce human hours where they count and letting the machine carry the rest,
under supervision.

The same logic runs outward from there. Psychologists present at the back of
every large event. Visible information about the other routes to help —
therapists from several schools, the reasonable ones, people able to spot a
problem, offer another angle, say that perhaps this would suit you better than
that. And the personal-development and psychology market regulated, because at
the moment anyone can sell anything to people at their worst.

None of this works without the research underneath it. Which is the point of the
chapter: a European science, on European problems, with European scientists over
European data — and mental health as the first thing large enough, and neglected
enough, to prove whether we can do it.
