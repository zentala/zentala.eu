I have argued that we should copy the Swiss model of direct democracy and not
get clever about it. The obvious objection is the one people put to me first:
what stops a crowd from voting something terrible into the constitution on a bad
Tuesday?

The answer is that Switzerland already thought about this, for about a hundred
and thirty years, and the model comes with its safety mechanisms attached. **We
are not buying only the referendum. We are buying the safeguards too.** That is
the honest description of what I am proposing, and this chapter is the itemised
version of it.

Everything below about Switzerland is sourced. Everything I propose for Europe
and for Poland is labelled as a proposal — my arithmetic, not a Swiss fact. When
I could not verify something, I say so rather than rounding it into confidence.

## What the Swiss system actually is

Switzerland is a **semi-direct democracy**: it has a parliament and a government
that work much as ours do, and on top of that citizens hold instruments to
propose law and to overturn it. Three instruments, in plain words. The first is
the accelerator, the second is the brake, and the third fires by itself.

**The popular initiative** — the Swiss name for a **citizens' initiative**. Citizens propose a change to the federal
constitution. Any 100,000 people eligible to vote may do so, within 18 months of
the official publication of their proposal ([Federal Constitution, Art.
138–139](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/1999/404/en)). It goes to a national
vote whether parliament likes it or not.

**The optional referendum.** Parliament passes a law; citizens can veto it. If
within 100 days of official publication **50,000 voters or eight cantons**
demand it, the law is put to a national vote (Art. 141). It covers federal acts,
long-running emergency acts, and certain international treaties — including
treaties that provide for accession to an international organisation or that
contain important legislative provisions.

**The mandatory referendum.** Some things cannot be done without asking. Every
amendment to the federal constitution, **accession to organisations for
collective security or to supranational communities**, and emergency federal
acts with no constitutional basis lasting over a year must go to the vote of the
People and the Cantons (Art. 140). Nobody has to collect a signature; the vote
is automatic.

The electorate votes [about four times a
year](https://www.aboutswitzerland.eda.admin.ch/en/direct-democracy) on roughly
fifteen questions. The federal initiative was introduced in 1891; [more than 200
have gone to a vote and around 10 per cent have been
adopted](https://www.ch.ch/en/votes-and-elections/popular-initiative/). So the
instrument is used constantly and passes rarely. Both halves of that sentence
matter.

## The safeguards, one by one

### 1. The double majority of people and cantons

**What it is.** A proposal put to the People *and the Cantons* passes only if a
majority of those voting approve it **and** a majority of the cantons approve it
(Art. 142 §2). Each canton's vote is decided by the result inside that canton
(§3), and six cantons — Obwalden, Nidwalden, Basel-Stadt, Basel-Landschaft,
Appenzell Ausserrhoden and Appenzell Innerrhoden — carry **half a cantonal vote**
each (§4). With 20 full and 6 half votes that is 23 cantonal votes in total, so
the threshold is 12.

**What it protects against.** A dense, populous core outvoting the periphery on
every constitutional question. In a federation, that is the failure mode that
ends federations.

**Note the asymmetry**, because it is the cleverest part of the design: the
double majority applies to constitutional change and to accession to
supranational communities. The *optional referendum* — the citizen veto on an
ordinary law — needs only a majority of those who vote (Art. 142 §1). Vetoing is
easy; amending the constitution is hard. That is deliberate.

**Proposed European equivalent.** Member states as the second chamber: a
European citizens' vote on anything of treaty or constitutional rank passes only
with a majority of votes cast across the Union **and** a majority of member
states — 14 of 27. A European citizen veto of an ordinary legislative act needs
only the popular majority. Whether some states should carry a half vote, as six
Swiss cantons do, I have no view on; the Swiss half votes are a historical
artefact of cantons that split, not a formula anyone derived.

**Proposed Polish equivalent.** Poland is not a federation, so this is the
weakest of my analogies and I would rather say that than dress it up. The
nearest unit is the voivodeship: a constitutional-rank vote would need a
national majority plus a majority in 9 of 16 voivodeships. The counter-argument
is real — voivodeships are administrative, not constituent, and giving them a
veto invents a federalism Poland does not have. I put it forward as the version
worth arguing about, not as a settled answer.

### 2. Unity of subject, and unity of form

**What it is.** An initiative must not bundle unrelated matters together, and
must not mix a general proposal with a specific draft. If it fails either test
the Federal Assembly declares it invalid, in whole or in part (Art. 139 §3).

**What it protects against.** The rider — the popular thing carrying the
unpopular thing through on its back. It is the single most common way a
referendum is used to launder a decision the public did not actually approve.

**Proposed European and Polish equivalent.** Copy it unchanged, and say who
rules. In the EU that is plausibly the Court of Justice on referral; in Poland,
the Constitutional Tribunal — which raises the obvious question of what happens
when the body ruling on validity is itself contested. I do not have an answer
that survives a bad-faith government.

### 3. The validity check against binding international law

**What it is.** An initiative that infringes **mandatory provisions of
international law** is declared invalid (Art. 139 §3) — the ban on torture, on
slavery, on genocide, the non-derogable core usually called *jus cogens*.

**What it protects against.** Exactly the scenario the objectors raise: a
majority voting to do something to a minority that no majority may do.

**Proposed European and Polish equivalent.** The European Convention on Human
Rights and the Charter of Fundamental Rights are the natural floor, and they are
a higher floor than the Swiss one — which is worth stating plainly, because it
cuts against a claim I am fond of. Switzerland's filter is narrow on purpose:
the Swiss electorate has passed initiatives that ran into Convention obligations
and then argued about the conflict afterwards. A European version would have to
decide in advance whether the whole Convention binds an initiative or only its
non-derogable core. Those are materially different systems and I will not
pretend the choice is obvious.

### 4. Parliament answers, and may counter-propose

**What it is.** The Federal Assembly recommends acceptance or rejection and may
put a **counter-proposal** beside the initiative. Voters then vote on both, may
vote yes to both, and answer a third question saying which they prefer (Art.
139 §5, Art. 139b).

**What it protects against.** The false binary. It is the answer to "referendums
force a complicated question into yes or no" — the Swiss answer is that they do
not, because the expert body gets to put a better-drafted version on the same
ballot paper and let citizens choose between two real options.

This is also the mechanism that fits what I actually want from technocrats.
**Specialists prepare the work; citizens intervene at any stage and approve or
reject it.** Not a menu of priced options handed to the public like a restaurant
card — that is not how you run a state. The counter-proposal is expertise
competing for a mandate, which is the right shape.

**Proposed equivalent.** Copy it unchanged at both levels. It is the cheapest
safeguard on this list and the one most likely to be dropped for looking
complicated.

### 5. No turnout quorum

**What it is.** There is none. A proposal passes if a majority of *those who
vote* approve it (Art. 142 §1). Turnout has averaged [a little over 40 per
cent](https://www.aboutswitzerland.eda.admin.ch/en/direct-democracy) in recent
decades, and that does not invalidate anything.

**What it protects against.** This looks like a missing safeguard and is in fact
a deliberate one. With a turnout quorum, the way to defeat a proposal is to tell
your side to **stay home** — abstention gets counted as a no, and a boycott
campaign beats an argument. The Venice Commission's [Code of Good Practice on
Referendums](https://www.venice.coe.int/webforms/documents/default.aspx?pdffile=CDL-AD(2022)015-e)
advises against turnout quorums for precisely this reason, while allowing an
approval quorum for questions of fundamental constitutional significance.

**Why this matters for Poland specifically.** A Polish national referendum is
binding only if **more than half of those entitled to vote take part**
([Constitution, Art.
125](https://www.sejm.gov.pl/prawo/konst/angielski/konse.htm)). That single
clause is why Poland's referendums keep producing nothing. **Proposed: drop the
turnout quorum and make the result binding on the votes cast** — with an
approval quorum only for constitutional-rank questions, along the Venice
Commission's line. This is the most consequential change on the page and the one
that will be fought hardest, because the quorum is what currently lets a
parliament hold a referendum and ignore it.

### 6. Binding force, and where Poland's current instrument fails

**What it is.** In Switzerland the result changes the law. An adopted initiative
amends the constitution; a successful optional referendum kills the act.

**What it is not, here.** Poland already has a citizens' initiative for a referendum:
500,000 citizens may petition for one ([Act on the national referendum, Art.
63](https://isap.sejm.gov.pl/isap.nsf/download.xsp/WDU20030570507/T/D20030507L.pdf)).
But the Sejm **is not obliged to call it**, and no deadline compels it to
decide. The right is to ask. That is the whole difference between the two
systems, and it is not a detail of implementation — it is the thing itself.

**Proposed: the trigger must be automatic.** Signatures verified, question
valid, vote scheduled. No discretionary step between the citizens and the ballot
box.

### 7. The layers below the federal one

**What it is.** Federal votes are the visible part. Cantons and communes run
their own initiatives and referendums with their own, generally lower,
thresholds, and in most places a citizen votes far more often on local matters
than national ones. The system is trained at the level where the stakes are
small and the feedback is fast.

**What it protects against.** A population using a powerful instrument for the
first time on the hardest possible question. Switzerland's electorate is
practised. Ours is not.

**Proposed equivalent.** This is the part I would start with tomorrow: school
councils, municipalities, districts, voivodeships or regions, then the state,
then the Union. Start where a mistake is cheap. It is also where **paper voting
in schools and town halls** is obviously affordable — if a school hall can hold
a national election, it can hold a local vote, and the number of people turning
out for a municipal question is not large.

### 8. Frequency as a safeguard

**What it is.** Four fixed voting dates a year, questions bundled onto them.

**What it protects against.** Two things at once. It caps administrative cost,
because you run four operations rather than forty. And it removes the
single-shot drama: if a question loses, it can come back — which lowers the
temperature of any individual vote, because no ballot is the last word forever.

**Proposed equivalent.** Fixed dates, same principle, at every level. Bundling
is what makes frequent voting cheap enough to be real rather than aspirational.

## The arithmetic: what the thresholds become at our scale

Start with the Swiss numbers as a share of their electorate, because that is the
only way to compare countries of different sizes. Switzerland had **5,611,289
registered voters** at the September 2024 federal vote. So, as fact:

- 100,000 signatures for a citizens' initiative = **1.78% of the electorate**
- 50,000 signatures for an optional referendum = **0.89% of the electorate**

**My proposal is not those numbers.** I round them up to something plainer and
harder: **2% of the electorate to launch a citizens' initiative, 1% to force an
optional referendum.** Two reasons. A round figure is a figure people can
repeat, and an instrument this powerful should be slightly harder to reach than
the one it is copied from, so that nobody can say it was made easy. The 2:1
ratio between proposing and vetoing is Swiss; the level is mine.

The arithmetic, shown so that anyone can disagree with the method rather than
with the number:

- Poland, 29,023,338 voters. 2% = 580,466.76, call it **580,000**. 1% =
  290,233.38, call it **290,000**.
- EU, about 359,000,000 voters. 2% = **7,180,000**. 1% = **3,590,000**.

| | Switzerland *(fact)* | EU *(proposed)* | Poland *(proposed)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electorate | 5,611,289 | ≈ 359,000,000 | 29,023,338 |
| Citizens' initiative | 100,000 *(1.78%)* | **≈ 7,180,000** *(2%)* | **≈ 580,000** *(2%)* |
| Optional referendum / veto | 50,000 *(0.89%)* | **≈ 3,590,000** *(1%)* | **≈ 290,000** *(1%)* |
| Collection period, initiative | 18 months | 18 months | 18 months |
| Collection period, veto | 100 days | *proposed* 6 months | 100 days |
| Second-chamber majority | 12 of 23 cantons | *proposed* 14 of 27 states | *proposed* 9 of 16 voivodeships |

Everything in the Switzerland column is a fact from the constitution and the
official register. Everything in the other two columns is my proposal.

Electorate figures: about **359 million** eligible voters at the [2024 European
Parliament election](https://results.elections.europa.eu/en/) — published
estimates range from roughly 350 to 361 million depending on the source and the
cut-off date, so treat the European row as accurate to about two per cent — and
**29,023,338** in Poland at the same election.

Three honest remarks about that table.

**The Polish initiative number lands close to the threshold Poland already
uses.** 580,000 against the existing 500,000 — sixteen per cent higher, and in
the same region rather than a different world. That is worth knowing, because it
means **the problem with the Polish instrument is not the threshold — it is that
the result does not bind and the Sejm does not have to act.** Almost nobody needs
to argue about the number. They need to argue about the two words that make it
real.

**The 100-day veto window does not survive translation to Union scale.** A
hundred days to collect three million signatures across 27 states and 24
languages is not the same task as a hundred days to collect fifty thousand in a
country the size of Switzerland. I propose six months, and I am aware that every
month I add is a month of legal uncertainty for the act being challenged. I do
not know the right number; I know that copying "100 days" unchanged would be
copying the label rather than the mechanism.

**The European Citizens' Initiative is not this and is not close.** It needs [1
million signatures from at least 7 member
states](https://citizens-initiative.europa.eu/how-it-works_en) within 12 months,
organised by a committee of seven citizens in seven countries, and the
Commission must formally respond within six months of submission — but it **is
not obliged to propose anything**. One million is about 0.28% of the EU
electorate: roughly a seventh of the 2% I am proposing, and a sixth of the
Swiss share, for an instrument with no binding force at all. The right reading is not "the EU already has this". It is that the
EU built the signature-collection machinery, at a *lower* bar than Switzerland
uses, and then attached it to nothing. The plumbing exists. The power does not.

## What the Swiss model costs

I said I would not pretend it has no costs, so here they are.

**Turnout is low.** A little over 40 per cent on average in recent decades. You
can read that as fatigue, or as citizens sensibly skipping the questions they do
not care about and turning out for the ones they do. Both readings are honest.
It does mean a determined, organised minority of the whole electorate can carry
a federal vote.

**Money works.** Campaigns can be funded, and well-funded campaigns win more
often than badly-funded ones. Switzerland has moved late and modestly on
political-finance transparency. Frequent voting multiplies this surface: more
votes mean more opportunities to spend. I regard this as the strongest practical
criticism of the model and I do not have a solution beyond disclosure rules that
are stricter than the Swiss ones.

**It is slow.** A law can be stopped after parliament has passed it. A
constitutional change takes years. In a crisis this is a liability, which is why
the emergency-act provisions exist — and those are themselves subject to a
referendum if they run past a year.

**Women voted federally only from 1971.** A [February 1971
referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Swiss_women%27s_suffrage_referendum)
gave Swiss women the federal vote; the first federal election they took part in
was in October 1971. In the last canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, women got the
cantonal vote only after a **Federal Supreme Court judgment in November 1990**.
This is the hardest fact on this page for my argument, and it should be. An
all-male electorate voted the question down in 1959 and took twelve more years
to reverse itself, and one canton had to be compelled by a court. If you want a
case where a majority withheld a right from a minority through the direct
instruments, it is right here, in the model I am proposing to copy.

I will not resolve that against myself with a sentence. What I will say is that
it argues for the validity filter and the rights floor in safeguard 3 being
*stronger* than the Swiss one, not for abandoning the instrument that eventually,
slowly, corrected it.

## Where I stand, and the argument against me

My position is this: **history's record of mass harm is overwhelmingly a record
of what governments did, not what electorates voted for.** The catastrophes of
the last century were organised by states, by administrations, by small numbers
of people holding a monopoly of decision. I am much less afraid of a crowd
making a bad call on a Tuesday than of a cabinet making one for thirty years
with no mechanism to stop it. That is why I want the safeguards attached to the
instrument rather than used as an excuse to refuse it.

The standard counter-argument is serious and I will state it at full strength
rather than in a version I can knock down. **Majorities have voted to restrict
minorities.** Referendums on minority rights have a bad record in several
countries, including Switzerland. A right that requires 50% plus one is not a
right; it is a permission. And the Swiss women's vote above is not a
hypothetical — it is this exact objection, evidenced, inside the model I want.

I am not going to declare myself the winner of that argument in my own chapter.
What I claim is narrower: that the objection is an argument about **which
safeguards** and **how strong a rights floor**, not an argument for leaving
decisions where they are now. Both sides of it want a limit on what a majority
may do. The disagreement is over who is more dangerous without one.

## What this plan still has to decide

Named, so that nobody has to guess what I have left open.

- **Whether the rights floor is the whole Convention or only its non-derogable
  core** (safeguard 3). Switzerland chose narrow. I do not know what we should
  choose.
- **Who rules on validity**, and what happens when that body is itself captured
  (safeguard 2).
- **The second chamber for Poland.** Voivodeships are the least bad unit I can
  name and they are not good.
- **The veto collection window at Union scale.** Six months is my guess, not a
  finding.
- **Campaign finance rules**, which have to be stricter than the Swiss ones and
  which I cannot draft.
- **Digital voting.** Paper first, with the digital system built in parallel as
  something we learn on — and paper kept permanently as the fallback for the
  basic functions of the state, not only for voting. What would have to be true
  of identity, verification and audit before a digital referendum binds is
  unanswered here.
- **Whether a European instrument needs treaty change**, or whether an
  automatic-referral obligation could be attached to the existing Citizens'
  Initiative first. That is a lawyer's question and it is the one that decides
  how soon any of this can start.
