# European Digital Democracy — movement and delivery strategy

This sidecar records new theses for later research and deliberate promotion into the public [European Digital Democracy Framework](digital-democracy.mdx). It is not part of production navigation.

## Strategic direction

Direct democracy is not merely one feature inside a broad digital-integration pillar. It is a distinct layer of citizen control over European, national, regional and local institutions.

The project should advocate a peaceful, pan-European movement for binding citizen participation between elections. Poland should initiate and lead the proposal, while inviting people and organizations from every member state to shape it.

Polish software engineers should help build the open tools, prototypes and standards required by the movement. This makes the proposal political, technical and economic at the same time: Europe gains democratic infrastructure, while Polish teams build expertise in civic technology and trustworthy public systems.

## New argument: the cost of irreversible policy

Europe is entering a period in which governments will make more frequent, difficult decisions about migration, war, public debt, spending and economic direction.

The cost of democratic participation must be compared with the full cost of these policies. A referendum has an organizational cost. A migration, war or economic policy can shape public finances, security and society for decades. The relevant comparison is therefore not “referendum versus no expense,” but recurring democratic decision-making versus the long-term cost of decisions citizens cannot stop.

A public article should quantify concrete policies separately rather than attach one unsupported number to all of them.

## New argument: concentrated representation is a capture surface

A system that concentrates decisive power in a small group of representatives creates a narrow target for influence.

Lobbying and activism can operate openly. Corruption, blackmail, surveillance and covert pressure can operate secretly. This is not an allegation that a specific decision or representative is corrupt. It is a structural claim: influencing hundreds of decision-makers is easier than capturing the independent choices of millions of citizens.

Digital surveillance increases the importance of this threat model. The long article must distinguish legal advocacy, conflicts of interest, bribery, coercion and foreign influence instead of treating them as one thing.

## Movement, not a request to government

Governments and political representatives should not be expected to surrender power voluntarily. The delivery mechanism is a mass social movement that:

- operates across Europe rather than in one isolated country;
- demands binding mechanisms, not consultations that governments may ignore;
- rejects candidates who offer no concrete transfer of decision power to citizens;
- channels anger into institutional reform rather than aggression against migrants or other people who did not make government policy;
- develops a model that can be tested locally and expanded across levels of government.

## Proposed information architecture

The current three pillars overload “Digital Integration” with administration, infrastructure, democratic control and other reforms. Test a four-part structure:

1. A shared European language and public sphere.
2. Direct democracy and citizen control of institutions.
3. Shared digital public infrastructure.
4. Robotic reindustrialization and strategic resilience.

The movement and Poland's leadership are the delivery strategy across the four areas, not a fifth subject pillar.

## Research before promotion

Before moving these theses into the public framework, establish:

- which decisions can be initiated, approved or stopped by citizens at each level;
- constitutional and human-rights limits on majority decisions;
- secrecy, end-to-end verifiability and resistance to coercion or vote buying;
- campaign-finance transparency and equal access to competing arguments;
- non-digital participation and accessibility;
- thresholds, timing and emergency powers;
- independent execution and audit of binding results;
- evidence for current public-government tensions in specific European countries;
- realistic roles for an open-source civic-technology community.
