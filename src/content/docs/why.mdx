The case for reform is made elsewhere on this site, at length: Europe is aging,
the pension model ends with generational replacement, and the work will have to
be done by machines because there will not be enough people. This page does not
argue it again. It is for the reader who accepts all of that and still objects.
The answers are my positions; where one still needs evidence attached, the page
says so instead of quoting a number nobody checked.

<Section eyebrow="The claim" title="What is being claimed, in one paragraph" lead="Stated plainly, so you know which sentence you are arguing with.">
  <Callout kind="claim" label="The claim">
    Generations no longer replace themselves: fertility sits below replacement in
    every member state, and the model that pays for pensions — growth, plus
    enough young workers behind us — ends with them. Money is a claim on labour,
    and labour comes from people or from machines. There will not be enough
    people. **So the proposal is to shift our expectations onto robotics
    deliberately, as a strategy rather than as a hope**, in the roughly thirty
    years of working economy we have left.
  </Callout>

  The whole argument is <T slug="the-frame">the frame</T>, written out in
  [Designing Our Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement). The order the
  pieces get built in — a common working language, then a digital state, then
  robotics on top of both — is on [the vision page](/vision). Neither is
  re-argued here.

<ObjectionList title="The five objections" intro="Each answer is short on purpose. The chapter it points to is where the argument lives, and where you should go to break it.">
  <Objection claim="Why not immigration instead? The obvious answer to a labour shortage is more people." verdict="Three reasons, and none of them is comfortable.">
    It does not add up economically: migrants arriving without the skills the
    economy needs cost more in support than they contribute, and the highly
    skilled ones, who are scarce everywhere, do not come here to wipe anybody's
    backside. It has not worked at scale anywhere: I cannot point to a European
    country where large-scale immigration solved a labour shortage, and what we
    would need is labour at large scale, not small. And there is something
    degrading in the premise itself: planning to import people so that they can
    do the care work we will not do ourselves is not a policy anyone should be
    proud of. What Europe should do instead — who it invites, on what published
    terms, and what it owes them — is argued in
    [Education Instead of Import](/book/education-and-migration).
  </Objection>

  <Objection claim="Who ends up owning the robots? If automation replaces the labour our pensions depend on and the returns go to a handful of owners, we have solved production and destroyed the distribution that made it worth solving." verdict="Ownership is the second question. Cheapness is the first.">
    The ownership question is the wrong one to settle first. The models will be
    mixed, and should be: state-owned or cooperative for municipal services,
    private where competition works, dividend-based inside some companies.
    **What matters more than ownership is that robots are cheap** — affordable,
    easy to implement, built on open standards, hard to monopolise. Technology
    that cannot be enclosed and rented out distributes its benefits almost by
    default, and then the ownership question largely answers itself; technology
    that can be enclosed will be, whatever the ownership law says, and then no
    ownership model saves us. Why cheapness is the design goal is argued in
    [Cheap Is Wealth](/book/cheap-is-wealth); who the surplus has to reach, and
    my own answer to whether that is communism, are in
    [Who It Earns For](/book/who-it-earns-for#it-has-to-earn-for-society-not-only-for-capital).

    <EvidenceBox title="What would move this from a position to a plan">
      Three things, none of which this site has yet: an open-standards stack for
      service robotics with the interfaces named, so that "hard to monopolise"
      becomes a specification rather than an adjective; one existing market where
      cheapness really did defeat enclosure, and one where it did not; and a
      legal answer on whether public or cooperative ownership of municipal
      automation is compatible with the Union's procurement and competition
      rules.
    </EvidenceBox>
  </Objection>

  <Objection claim="How would this ever happen politically? The reflex is to picture twenty-seven governments agreeing at once." verdict="Not anger — a vision people could agree on.">
    Dissatisfaction with the Union in its present form is already boiling. The
    missing piece is not anger; it is **a vision people could agree on**. A
    proposal built so that everyone wins can earn the unanimity that treaty
    change requires, and treaty change is then a question of political will,
    which follows conviction rather than preceding it. What happens when the institutions say
    no, which parts need no treaty at all, and the legal questions I am not able
    to answer, are in [The Political Path](/book/political-path) — the five
    questions a lawyer would have to check are
    [one table there](/book/political-path#the-treaty-question-i-cannot-answer).
  </Objection>

  <Objection claim="Who pays for it?" verdict="Two answers, and both turn the question around.">
    **We already pay.** Every member state funds its own digitalisation, its own
    systems, its own procurement and its own mistakes, twenty-seven times over;
    doing it once, together, is not a new budget line but a less wasteful use of
    the one we already have. **And we have already saved.** Pension
    contributions are money set aside for exactly this future, and part of them
    can be invested in the robotics that will serve it — so that we own it,
    publicly, and so that it delivers services from the beginning rather than
    after some distant payoff. There is a third source, and it is already
    flowing: the conflict with Russia is pushing money into military robotics
    now, and that research can be converted afterwards to civilian use. The
    arithmetic, and its limits, are in
    [Designing Our Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement#who-pays-for-it).

    <EvidenceBox title="What this answer still owes">
      What the twenty-seven states actually spend, separately, on public
      digitalisation each year, and which part of that is genuinely duplicated;
      the legal and prudential limits on investing pension contributions in
      public infrastructure, member state by member state; and one worked case
      of military robotics research converted to civilian service use, with its
      cost and its lag.
    </EvidenceBox>
  </Objection>

  <Objection claim="What if you are wrong?" verdict="Parts of this are guesses. I would rather mark them than defend them.">
    The demography is not a guess: it already happened, in the maternity wards,
    decades ago, and [the frame chapter](/book/designing-our-retirement) rests
    on that alone. The
    open question is deployment: whether Europe deploys machines at scale and
    whether ordinary people see the benefit, or whether it lands narrowly and
    the gains stay with whoever owns it. If service robotics stays expensive and
    confined to demonstrations, the plan is left with a shared language and a
    digital state — worth having, and not enough to staff a care home. What I am
    afraid of is more specific than being wrong: that governments patch the
    demographic hole with mass immigration because it is the reflex, at a cost
    we did not need to pay; that there is chaos because nobody prepared; that
    retirement arrives and the money buys nothing, because the system still
    assumes workers who no longer exist — nobody to care for us, nobody to bring
    food; and that a generation spends its best years on bullshit jobs instead
    of on the concrete thing that needed building. **That last fear is the
    reason this project exists.**
