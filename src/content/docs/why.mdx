<WhySection
  eyebrow="The claim"
  title="What is being claimed, in one paragraph"
  lead="Stated plainly, so you know which sentence you are arguing with."
>
  <WhyAnswer>
    Generations no longer replace themselves. Fertility sits below replacement in
    every member state, no national policy has reversed it anywhere, and the
    model that pays for pensions — growth, plus enough young workers behind
    us — ends with generational replacement. Money is only a claim on labour, and
    there are exactly two sources of labour: people, or machines. There will not
    be enough people. **So the proposal is to shift our expectations onto
    robotics deliberately, as a strategy rather than as a hope.** The window for
    that is roughly thirty years of working economy — enough tax base, enough
    workers, enough industrial capacity — and this is the last generation able to
    pay for it.

    Read the whole argument in [Designing Our
    Retirement](/book/designing-our-retirement). The order the pieces get built
    in — a common working language, then a digital state, then robotics on top of
    both — is on [the vision page](/vision). Neither is re-argued here.

    One piece of outside evidence is worth naming, because it is the closest
    thing Europe has to an official admission. The Draghi report (September 2024)
    found European productivity in negative territory against the United States,
    and concluded that at the growth rate held since 2015 the Union would manage
    only to keep GDP constant until 2050. It diagnosed an existential
    competitiveness problem and stopped short of saying what to do about it. This
    site treats that diagnosis as the floor of the argument, not as its headline.
  </WhyAnswer>
</WhySection>

<WhySection
  eyebrow="Objection 01"
  title="Why not immigration instead?"
  lead="The obvious answer to a labour shortage is more people. It is also Europe's reflex, and it is failing."
>
  <WhyAnswer>
    The honest version of my answer has three parts, and none of them is
    comfortable.

    **It does not add up economically.** Migrants arriving without the skills the
    economy needs cost more in support than they contribute. The highly skilled
    ones, who are genuinely scarce everywhere, do not come to Europe to wipe
    anybody's backside.

    **It has not worked at scale anywhere.** I cannot point to a European country
    where large-scale immigration solved a labour shortage without creating
    problems that now dominate its politics. And the scale we would need is
    millions, not thousands.

    **And there is something degrading in the premise.** Planning to import
    people so that they can do the intimate care work we will not do ourselves is
    not a policy anyone should be proud of.

    Meanwhile the alternative is arriving anyway. The era of machines, humanoid
    and otherwise, is not a forecast — it is a deployment question. The only open
    issues are whether Europe deploys at scale and whether ordinary people see
    the benefit, or whether it lands narrowly and the gains stay with whoever
    owns it.

    That is also an opening rather than a defensive move. Robotics is a new
    automotive industry: a way for Europe to reindustrialise while it reforms
    itself. For Germany, for Poland, and specifically for Polish software — the
    layer around the machines is where Central Europe can contribute something
    the rest of Europe needs. It is also the answer to a drain that is already
    running: the United States pulls European know-how and companies out with
    lower taxes and lighter regulation, China takes sector after sector from
    electronics to cars, and a Europe with no advantage in anything strategic
    ends up a market for other people's corporations rather than a maker of
    anything.

    I should name my own bias. I am a programmer, so I see a nail everywhere,
    because I have a hammer. Read the rest of this page knowing that.

    <WhyEvidenceNote>
      Three numbers would decide this argument, and I do not have defensible
      versions of them yet:

      - the net fiscal contribution of recent arrivals, by skill level, across a
        comparable set of member states;
      - how many care and municipal workers Europe will be short of by 2050,
        against what migration has actually delivered per decade;
      - the cost per task of a deployed service robot against the fully loaded
        cost of the worker it replaces, as a curve rather than as a snapshot.

      If that third cost refuses to fall, the whole strategy weakens, and the
      argument above is worth less than it sounds.
    </WhyEvidenceNote>
  </WhyAnswer>
</WhySection>

<WhySection
  eyebrow="Objection 02"
  title="Who ends up owning the robots?"
  lead="If automation replaces the labour our pensions depend on and the returns go to a handful of owners, we have solved production and destroyed the distribution that made it worth solving."
>
  <WhyAnswer>
    My position is that ownership is the second question, not the first. The
    models will be mixed and they should be: public or cooperative for municipal
    services, private where competition works, dividend-based inside some
    companies. Arguing the ideal structure out in advance is how twenty years get
    spent.

    **What matters more than ownership is that robots are cheap.** Affordable,
    easy to implement, built on open standards, hard to monopolise. Technology
    that cannot be enclosed and rented out distributes its benefits almost by
    default, and then the ownership question largely answers itself. Technology
    that can be enclosed will be, whatever the ownership law says — and then no
    ownership model saves us.

    This is also why buying the capability from outside Europe is not a shortcut.
    A continent that rents its critical technology rents the politics that come
    with it. Ukraine has spent two years discovering what dependence on somebody
    else's satellites and somebody else's defence systems costs when their owner
    changes his mind, and the United States has already shown that its interests
    outweigh alliance commitments when the two diverge. Owning the machines that
    will run our old age is the same argument as owning the ones that defend the
    border. It is one argument, not two.

    <WhyEvidenceNote title="What would settle this — and what it would take">
      This is the question I would most like to be beaten on in public. What
      would move it from a position to a plan:

      - a concrete open-standards stack for service robotics, with the
        interfaces named, so that "hard to monopolise" becomes a specification
        rather than an adjective;
      - evidence from an existing market where cheapness really did defeat
        enclosure, and at least one where it did not;
      - a legal answer on whether public or cooperative ownership of municipal
        automation is compatible with the Union's procurement and competition
        rules.
    </WhyEvidenceNote>
  </WhyAnswer>
</WhySection>

<WhySection
  eyebrow="Objection 03"
  title="How would this ever happen politically?"
  lead="The reflex is to picture twenty-seven governments agreeing at once, which is why the conversation usually ends within a minute."
>
  <WhyAnswer>
    Dissatisfaction with the Union in its present form is already boiling. The
    missing piece is not anger — there is plenty of anger, and it is being spent
    on individuals rather than on the arrangement that keeps producing them. The
    missing piece is **a vision people could agree on.** A proposal built so that
    everyone wins can earn the unanimity that treaty change requires, and treaty
    change is then a question of political will, which follows conviction rather
    than preceding it.

    I do not expect the current administration to want this. Everything here
    shortens the distance between a citizen and a decision, and institutions do
    not usually volunteer to become less necessary. So the route does not start
    in Brussels. It starts with enough people obviously wanting it that
    supporting it stops being brave and starts being the safe option.

    The practical unlock is that much of this does not need twenty-seven
    signatures. English as an official working language can be adopted by one
    state, or by a group that already behaves as a bloc. A digital state can be
    built once and copied, because copying a system that visibly works is a far
    easier political act than legislating one into existence for countries that
    have not seen it yet.

    The long version, including what I cannot answer, is [The Political
    Path](/book/political-path). It is the least finished part of the vision, and
    it says so.

    <WhyEvidenceNote title="What I cannot answer yet">
      I am not a lawyer, and I will not invent a reassuring legal conclusion for
      a website. Five things would have to be checked by somebody who is:

      - what a member state may declare as its own official working language,
        and where that meets the Union's rules on languages and equal treatment;
      - whether a binding national citizen vote can bind a government's conduct
        at European level;
      - which of the existing routes for a subset of states to act together
        apply here;
      - whether one state's identity and records system can be adopted by
        another without a new legal instrument;
      - what the rules allow when pension contributions are invested in public
        infrastructure rather than in financial assets.

      If you are a European lawyer and any of that is answerable, that answer is
      worth more to this project than another chapter of mine.
    </WhyEvidenceNote>
  </WhyAnswer>
</WhySection>

<WhySection
  eyebrow="Objection 04"
  title="Who pays for it?"
  lead="Two answers, and both turn the question around."
>
  <WhyAnswer>
    **We already pay.** Every member state funds its own digitalisation, its own
    systems, its own procurement and its own mistakes, twenty-seven times over.
    Doing it once, together, is not a new budget line — it is a less wasteful use
    of the one we already have. Building one shared administrative system instead
    of twenty-seven divergent ones is the cheapest money in this entire proposal.

    **And we have already saved.** Pension contributions are money set aside for
    exactly this future. Part of it can be invested in building the robotics that
    will serve that future — **so that we own it**, publicly, rather than renting
    it later from whoever got there first. Unlike most pension investments, this
    one delivers the service itself: machines paid for by contributions, doing
    the work those contributions were meant to buy, from the beginning rather
    than after some distant payoff.

    There is a third source, and it is already flowing. The conflict with Russia
    is pushing money into robotics for military purposes right now. That research
    can be stimulated for defence and converted afterwards to civilian use — the
    same autonomy, sensing and manufacturing capacity, pointed at demographic
    collapse instead of at an enemy. It is the realistic route to funding
    robotics at European scale, and unlike the other two it needs no new decision
    to start.

    <WhyEvidenceNote>
      The numbers I owe you here:

      - what the twenty-seven states actually spend, separately, on public
        digitalisation each year, and which part of that is genuinely
        duplicated;
      - the legal and prudential limits on investing pension contributions in
        public infrastructure, member state by member state;
      - a worked case of military robotics research converted to civilian
        service use, with its cost and its lag.
    </WhyEvidenceNote>
  </WhyAnswer>
</WhySection>

<WhySection
  eyebrow="Objection 05"
  title="What if you are wrong?"
  lead="The question I take most seriously, because the answer is that parts of this are guesses, and I would rather mark them than defend them."
>
  <WhyAnswer>
    The part I am confident about is the demography. It already happened, in the
    maternity wards, decades ago: the people who will not be working in 2056 were
    not born in 1996. Nothing on this page depends on a forecast for that.

    The part that could be wrong is the machines, and specifically their pace. If
    service robotics stays expensive, fragile and confined to demonstrations,
    the strategy loses its ceiling, and Europe is left with a shared language and
    a digital state — worth having anyway, but not enough on their own to staff a
    care home. That is the real risk, and I would rather hold it openly than
    hedge it.

    What I am afraid of is more specific than being wrong. That governments patch
    the demographic hole with mass immigration because it is the reflex, at a
    cost we did not need to pay. That there is chaos because nobody prepared.
    That retirement arrives and the money buys nothing, because the system still
    assumes workers who no longer exist — nobody to care for us, nobody to bring
    food. And that a generation spends its best years on bullshit jobs instead of
    on the concrete thing that needed building.

    **That last fear is the reason this project exists.** The cost of being wrong
    in the direction I am arguing is that Europe ends up with cheap automation,
    an administration that works, and a common language it can do business in.
    The cost of being wrong in the other direction is a continent of pensioners
    holding money that buys nothing. Those two mistakes are not the same size.

    And none of it waits for permission. If you repeat the argument, attack it,
    send it to someone who can break it, or build one piece of it, the
    probability of it happening goes up — because that is the only way things
    like this have ever happened. You are not the audience for this. You are the
    condition of it.
  </WhyAnswer>
</WhySection>
